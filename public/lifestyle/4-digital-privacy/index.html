Digital Privacy in 2024: What You Need to Know Right Now

February 12, 2024 by Collins Obura 5 min read

Last year, I received a targeted advertisement for a specific ailment I’d never searched for, never mentioned online, and barely acknowledged to myself. I was genuinely unsettled. How did they know? The answer, once I started investigating, was more complex and more troubling than simple algorithmic inference. They knew because my data had been sold, traded, and analyzed by at least seven different companies I’d never directly interacted with.

That experience became the catalyst for understanding something I’d intellectually known but hadn’t emotionally grasped: your digital footprint is larger than you can probably imagine. Every app you download, every website you visit, every search you make—it’s being tracked, analyzed, combined with data from other sources, and sometimes sold to the highest bidder. In 2024, understanding digital privacy isn’t optional; it’s essential for maintaining autonomy over your own life.

The Scale Is Staggering

I spent weeks researching the scope of the problem, and honestly, the numbers are difficult to comprehend. Recent data breaches have exposed the personal information of hundreds of millions—everything from financial records to health information to intimate conversations. Major companies have been caught, in explicit terms, selling user data to third parties. Government agencies around the world are monitoring online activity at scales that would have seemed dystopian just a decade ago.

Meanwhile, most people have no systematic understanding of how exposed they actually are. We’ve normalized giving away our data. We click “agree” on terms of service we’ve never read, often written intentionally in dense legal language that obscures what we’re actually consenting to.

The average person generates approximately 2.5 quintillion bytes of data daily. That’s not metaphorical—it’s the actual volume of digital information created through our devices, our interactions, and our online presence. To put it in perspective: that’s equivalent to 2.5 million books worth of information, every single day, from you alone.

Your Rights Are Actually Changing

Here’s where I found some genuine hope during my research: laws are catching up. Not fast enough, not comprehensively enough, but they’re moving. The European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and similar frameworks being adopted globally are giving people unprecedented control over their data.

I spent time understanding what these rights actually mean in practice:

Right to access: You can request every piece of data a company holds about you. Many companies hate this because it reveals the extent of their tracking.

Right to be forgotten: You can demand deletion of your data. This is revolutionary because it challenges the assumption that all data should be kept indefinitely.

Right to portability: You can move your data between services. This breaks the lock-in effect that keeps people trapped on platforms.

Right to object: You can refuse to have your data used for certain purposes, especially automated decision-making.

But here’s the catch: knowing your rights and exercising them are vastly different things. Many people are unaware these rights exist. Even those who know often don’t exercise them because companies make the process deliberately complicated.

Practical Steps That Actually Work

Through my research and conversations with security experts, I’ve identified changes that genuinely reduce your vulnerability:

Strong, unique passwords might seem obvious, but the execution matters. Every account should have a completely different password. Use a password manager like Bitwarden or 1Password—not to manage complexity, but to liberate yourself from the impossible task of memorizing dozens of unique, complex passwords.

Two-factor authentication adds a genuine security layer. Even if your password is compromised, your account remains protected because a would-be intruder can’t verify their identity through the second factor.

Review app permissions regularly. I discovered that several apps on my phone had requested permissions they absolutely didn’t need. Does a flashlight app really need access to your contacts? No. Disable what you can.

Use a VPN when you’re on public WiFi especially. It encrypts your internet traffic, hiding your activity from your internet service provider and the network operator.

Minimize data sharing intentionally. Unsubscribe from marketing emails. Disable location tracking when you don’t need it. Don’t use single-sign-on (Google/Facebook login) for every service—create unique accounts instead. This limits the data any single company has about you.

Monitor your credit actively. Set up alerts with your credit bureau. You can get free annual reports that will show you if someone is trying to open accounts in your name.

The Systemic Problem

Individual actions matter—I genuinely believe that. But I’ve come to understand they’re insufficient without systemic change. We need stronger privacy regulations globally. We need corporate accountability that actually carries consequences for breaches. We need privacy by design in new technologies, not privacy as an afterthought. We need better digital literacy education so people understand what they’re consenting to. And we need transparency in algorithmic decision-making so we know when algorithms are being used to determine our creditworthiness, job prospects, or insurance rates.

What This Means Going Forward

The privacy landscape continues to evolve, and not always in directions I’m comfortable with. Apple’s privacy updates are genuine improvements. Google’s moves away from third-party cookies, while partially motivated by regulatory pressure, are still steps forward. But the fundamental tension remains: the more connected we become, the more vulnerable we are.

Your data is valuable—genuinely valuable—to corporations, to governments, and potentially to criminals. Protecting it is worth your time and attention. Start with one of the practical steps above. Privacy isn’t an all-or-nothing endeavor. Small improvements accumulate into meaningful protection.

What privacy concerns you most? I’m genuinely interested in hearing what’s keeping you up at night.

The Art of the Simple Dinner: Building a Weeknight Cooking Practice

February 3, 2024 by Collins Obura 5 minute read

I stood in my kitchen last month looking at a recipe promising “30 minutes.” I started cooking. An hour later, I’d burned something, forgotten an ingredient I didn’t have, and spent three times what takeout would have cost. I ordered pizza. Again.

This is a pattern I lived for years. Cooking felt impossible. Too time-consuming, too complicated, too likely to fail. The safer option was outsourcing meals. And if you spend more than ten minutes in any urban center, you know outsourcing is always available.

But here’s what I eventually understood: cooking is a skill worth developing. Not because it’s trendy or morally virtuous. But because it genuinely changes your relationship with food, health, and money.

Why This Actually Matters

Home cooking provides things you don’t get from outsourcing:

Better nutrition - You control ingredients, portions, and preparation methods. You know exactly what’s going into your body. This matters.

Significant financial savings - Eating out costs 2-3x more than cooking at home. Over a year, cooking just three meals weekly instead of eating out saves $1,000-$2,000. That’s significant money that could fund other priorities.

Food security - You’re not dependent on delivery apps that sometimes don’t work, restaurants that might be closed, or availability being limited. You can feed yourself.

Genuine satisfaction - Creating something delicious for yourself feels genuinely good. There’s pride in preparing your own food.

Cultural connection - Cooking connects you to heritage, family traditions, and community. It’s not just fuel—it’s meaning.

Yet most people under forty can’t cook a basic meal. Not because they’re incapable. But because they were never taught and have absorbed the belief that it’s too hard.

It’s not. I promise. Most delicious meals use five to eight ingredients and take twenty to thirty minutes.

The Real Barrier: Expectation

I spent time understanding why cooking feels so intimidating. The barrier isn’t skill. It’s expectation.

We watch cooking shows where chefs make complex dishes with fifteen ingredients, fancy equipment, and techniques that take years to master. We read recipes that read like chemistry experiments. We see Instagram photos of food so beautifully plated that the gap between that and our reality feels insurmountable. No wonder we think cooking is hard.

It’s not. Most delicious meals use five to eight ingredients and take twenty to thirty minutes.

The real barrier is that we’ve been sold a fantasy about what cooking should be, rather than learning what cooking actually is.

The Framework That Works

I eventually found that having a simple structure eliminated the decision paralysis:

Choose your protein (two to three options you can prepare consistently):

  • Eggs (scramble, fry, boil—all take five minutes)
  • Chicken breast (pan-fry in ten minutes)
  • Ground meat (brown in ten minutes)
  • Canned fish (open a can)
  • Beans or lentils (boil if dried, or just open a can)

Choose your starch (something that doesn’t require thought):

  • Rice (boil in fifteen minutes)
  • Pasta (boil in ten minutes)
  • Potatoes (roast or boil in twenty minutes)
  • Bread (buy it)

Add vegetables (anything works):

  • Frozen vegetables work fine (honestly no shame in this)
  • Canned vegetables are acceptable (better than nothing)
  • Fresh when available (but not required)

Season with flavor (this is where it gets interesting):

  • Good salt (it matters)
  • Good oil (matters even more)
  • Acid: lemon, lime, vinegar (changes everything)
  • Spices: two or three you actually like (not a full collection)

That’s it. Protein + starch + vegetables + flavor = complete meal. Twenty to thirty minutes. Three to five dollars per person.

The Weeknight Method

I realized the stress came from trying to cook new things constantly. I changed approach: pick three meals you like and can make simply. Rotate them.

Monday might be pasta with sauce and vegetables. Wednesday might be a rice bowl with protein and stir-fried vegetables. Friday might be a simple soup. I’m not trying to be a gourmet chef. I’m building a sustainable practice where I know what I’m doing and it reliably works.

Knowing exactly what you’re cooking removes decision friction. And once you’ve made the same meal three times, it becomes automatic. Fourth time is easier. Tenth time is honestly kind of meditative.

Building The Habit

I didn’t go from cooking zero meals weekly to cooking seven. That would have failed immediately. I committed to cooking one meal weekly. I chose something dead simple. Pasta. Sauce (jar sauce was fine). Vegetables (frozen was fine). Twenty minutes total.

I succeeded. The next month, I added another meal. Repeat. By month three, I was cooking two to three times weekly. By month six, it was normal.

The key isn’t ambition. It’s consistency. A successfully cooked simple meal beats a failed attempt at something fancy every single time.

The Equipment Reality

I spent money on fancy kitchen equipment. It didn’t make me cook better. But when I finally pared down to basics, everything improved:

  • One good knife (you use this constantly)
  • One large pan (works for almost everything)
  • One pot (boiling pasta, rice, vegetables)
  • Cutting board (basic)
  • Mixing bowl (basic)

That’s genuinely enough to cook anything. Fancy equipment doesn’t improve your cooking. A sustainable habit does.

The Budget Math

Cooking at home costs roughly two to four dollars per person per meal, depending on what you make. Eating out costs eight to twenty dollars. Over a year, cooking just three meals weekly instead of eating out saves something like $1,500.

That’s not minor. That’s significant money.

What Comes Later

Once the habit is solid—and I mean genuinely solid, after a few months—then you can explore. Try new cuisines. Add complexity. Experiment with techniques and ingredients. But only after you’re comfortable with basics.

The goal isn’t to become a chef. The goal is to feed yourself well, affordably, and to know how.

Start This Week

Pick one simple meal. Find a recipe with five to seven ingredients max. Make it this week. Don’t stress about perfection. Focus on completion.

You’ll make mistakes. That’s learning. Second time is better. Third time is easy. Tenth time you’ll forget why you ever thought this was hard.

Within a month, you’ll understand something important: cooking isn’t hard. It’s just unfamiliar. Once it’s familiar, it’s actually kind of enjoyable.

What’s the simple meal you could actually do this week? Share it—I’d love to hear what works for you.

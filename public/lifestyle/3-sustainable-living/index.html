A Beginner's Guide to Sustainable Living in the City

February 5, 2024 by Collins Obura 4 minute read

I used to feel genuinely anxious about environmental impact. The statistics are overwhelming—climate change accelerating, plastic choking oceans, biodiversity collapsing. I’d read articles about zero-waste living, pristine compost systems, perfect environmental choices, and feel paralyzed. How could I possibly do enough?

Then something shifted. I realized the narrative around sustainable living was designed by people with enormous privilege. Those Instagram accounts showing perfect minimalist homes, beautiful compost bins, and flawless environmental footprints? Those people usually had resources I didn’t have. But more importantly, I was approaching sustainability wrong.

Sustainable living isn’t about perfection. It’s about intentional choices that compound over time.

Start Where Change Actually Happens

The most impactful changes in my environmental footprint haven’t come from complicated systems. They’ve come from thinking differently about purchasing.

Before I buy anything—and I mean anything—I ask myself three questions:

Do I actually need this? Not, “Do I want this?” But genuinely, do I need it? The most sustainable product is the one you don’t buy. I’ve cut my consumption dramatically just by waiting a week before purchases and asking if I still want it. Usually I don’t.

Will it last? Buying something cheap that breaks in six months is worse than buying something expensive that lasts five years. I’ve switched to buying quality versions of things I use consistently. It costs more upfront but costs way less over time.

What happens to it when I’m done? Can it be repaired? Donated? Recycled? What’s its actual end-of-life? This question alone has changed what I buy.

These simple questions have had more impact than any complicated system ever did.

Daily Habits That Compound

Small changes in routines I was already doing have created surprisingly significant impact:

Transportation is the obvious one. I walk when I can. I use public transit regularly—which costs less anyway. When I drive, I combine errands into one trip. This saves money while reducing emissions.

Food choices matter more than I realized. I eat less meat, though not from perfectionism—more because plant-based meals are often cheaper. I buy local produce when available, both to support farmers and because it tastes better and lasts longer. I meal plan to reduce waste. These aren’t sacrifices. They’re improvements.

Energy use dropped dramatically when I paid attention. LED bulbs use a fraction of the energy of incandescents. Unplugging devices when not in use (or using power strips) reduces phantom power draw. Adjusting my thermostat slightly—colder in winter, warmer in summer—saves more than I expected. Individually small, but collectively significant.

Water conservation is similar. Shorter showers have become habitual. I fix leaks immediately. I turn off the tap while brushing teeth. These aren’t hardships. They’re just habits.

The Composting Question I Got Wrong

I used to feel guilty not composting. The narrative made it seem essential for sustainable living. But I live in an apartment. My building has no composting infrastructure. I don’t have yard space. For my situation, traditional composting is actually impractical.

So I learned about alternatives. I save vegetable scraps for a community garden nearby that has a composting system. I learned about municipal composting programs in my area. I discovered that sometimes the best action isn’t the one Instagram makes seem perfect—it’s the one that actually works for your situation.

Community Changes Behavior More Than Individual Perfection

The single biggest shift came when I stopped trying to do this alone. I joined a local environmental group. We organize community clean-ups. We share practices with each other. We celebrate small wins rather than perfecting ourselves.

Honestly, community motivation works better than individual guilt. When you’re with other people who care, you do better. When you share what you’re learning, others get ideas. When you’re part of a group, it’s not about purity—it’s about being part of something.

Your Actual Starting Point

If you’re ready to begin genuinely:

  • Pick one habit for the next month. Just one. Something realistic for your situation.
  • Find one community group to participate in.
  • Observe your waste for one week. Don’t judge. Just notice.
  • Identify one routine to make more sustainable.
  • Share one thing you learn with someone.

What’s your first sustainable step? I’d genuinely love to hear what works in your context.

Why We Stopped Being Bored — and What We Lost

February 17, 2026 by Collins Obura 4 min read

There used to be a moment on public transport that almost everyone knew.

You sat down. You had nothing in your hands. Your eyes drifted to the window, and your mind went somewhere — a half-formed thought, an old memory, a question that had been sitting at the back of your head for days. Nobody asked for that moment. It just happened.

Today, that moment barely gets a chance to show up.

The phone comes out before the seat is warm. There is always something to watch, something to scroll through, something to respond to. Waiting has been filled in, corner to corner, with content. Boredom has been pushed out of daily life — and most people feel good about that.

But something valuable went with it.

Boredom Was Never Just Discomfort

As the saying goes, an idle mind is the devil’s workshop — and that idea has shaped how most people think about doing nothing. Boredom gets a bad name. It is treated as a gap to fill, a problem to solve, a sign that time is being wasted.

That picture is not quite right.

When the brain has no outside task to carry out, it does not shut down. It switches into a different way of working — one where it starts to connect ideas that do not normally meet, revisit old problems with new eyes, and imagine things that have not happened yet. This is where creative thinking tends to come from. Not from consuming more, but from processing what is already there.

The people who shaped science, art, and literature were not always the busiest ones. They were often the ones who knew how to sit still.

What Happens When Every Gap Gets Filled

The trouble with filling every quiet moment is not just that creative thinking drops off. It is what takes its place.

Every spare minute now carries some form of input. A podcast during breakfast. Short videos between tasks. A thread to read through before sleep. Each piece of content seems worthwhile on its own. But together, they build a wall between a person and their own thoughts.

The mind never gets to wind down. It never gets to the end of one idea before the next one arrives. Everything is half-processed, half-understood, stacked up in a pile that keeps growing.

That pile is what many people feel as exhaustion — not tiredness from doing too much, but weariness from never pausing long enough to catch up with themselves.

The Productivity Trap

There is a particular version of this problem that catches up with ambitious people.

Quiet time gets replaced with productivity content — podcasts about building habits, videos about morning routines, articles about getting more done. It feels like learning. It feels useful. And so the guilt of doing nothing is managed by doing something that looks like self-improvement.

But listening to someone talk about focused work is not the same thing as doing focused work. The watching has taken the place of the doing. And because the two feel similar, the substitution is easy to miss.

Real thinking — the kind that produces something original — needs inputs, yes. But it also needs time when nothing is coming in. Time for ideas to settle, combine, and become something that actually belongs to the person thinking them.

Silence Has to Be Protected Now

Getting quiet used to happen on its own. Now it has to be chosen.

That is a significant shift. It means that boredom — which was once an ordinary part of daily life — is now something that has to be deliberately made room for.

A commute without earphones. Coffee without a screen. A walk where the phone stays in the pocket. These are not productivity strategies. They are simply invitations for the mind to do what it does when it is left alone.

As the old saying goes, still waters run deep. The mind that is given space tends to go to more interesting places than the one that is constantly fed from outside.

Give It a Try

The next time there are a few minutes with nothing to fill them — a queue, a wait, a slow moment — try leaving them empty.

No scrolling. No audio. Just the surroundings and whatever the mind decides to do with the silence.

It might feel uncomfortable at first. That is normal. The discomfort is just the habit of reaching for a screen running up against the decision not to.

Stay with it. Notice what surfaces.

Boredom is not empty time. It is thinking time that has not started yet.

Mobile Money Changed Everything. Here's What Comes Next.

February 17, 2026 by Collins Obura 4 min read

When M-Pesa launched in Kenya in 2007, most of the global finance world did not pay much attention.

A mobile money service for a developing market. Useful, perhaps, for people without bank accounts. But not exactly the kind of thing that turns heads in London or New York.

What followed surprised almost everyone.

Within a few years, millions of Kenyans who had never held a bank account were sending money, paying bills, and setting aside savings — all through a basic mobile phone. Research later showed that M-Pesa helped lift a measurable share of Kenyan households out of poverty by giving people, and women in particular, a new kind of economic independence.

It turned out to be one of the most significant financial breakthroughs of the early 21st century. And it was built in Nairobi, not Silicon Valley.

Why It Worked

M-Pesa worked for a straightforward reason: it was built for the actual situation people were living in.

Most financial systems are put together with certain assumptions baked in — that users have a fixed address, a formal income, and a long-standing relationship with a bank. In Kenya in 2007, most people had none of those things. What they did have was a mobile phone and a pressing need to move money safely across long distances.

M-Pesa met people where they were. It did not ask them to fit an existing system. It built something new around their reality.

That principle — design for the real user, not the imagined one — has been the driving force behind Africa’s fintech growth ever since. As they say, a good fit is worth more than a fine material.

What the Landscape Looks Like Today

Mobile money was just the beginning.

Africa’s financial technology space now includes companies working on cross-border payments, small business lending, affordable insurance, personal investment tools, and digital banking built specifically for traders who have always been left out of formal finance. Names that barely existed ten years ago have become household names across several countries and have built infrastructure that moves money faster and more cheaply than the old banking system ever did.

The investment community took notice. Africa has attracted significant fintech funding over the past decade, with financial technology consistently pulling in the largest share. That money reflects a recognition of something that people on the ground already knew: the continent represents one of the most significant untapped financial markets in the world.

The Problems That Have Not Gone Away

Progress has been real. So have the gaps.

Sending money between two neighbouring African countries still often involves routing transactions through Europe or the United States — a legacy of banking systems put in place during the colonial era that nobody has properly fixed. The result is that cross-border transfers within Africa can be slower and more expensive than sending money halfway around the world.

Regulatory differences also slow things down. With dozens of countries, each running its own central bank, currency rules, and financial regulations, building a service that works smoothly across borders remains genuinely difficult. What is permitted in one country may be restricted in the next, even when the two countries share a border and a common history.

Financial inclusion, while much improved, is still uneven. The innovation tends to cluster in city centres and reach the most connected communities first. Rural areas and older populations often remain on the outside looking in.

The Shift That Is Happening Right Now

The next wave is less about individual products and more about the underlying systems.

Companies are now building the infrastructure — the shared rails — that allow banks, businesses, and government services to connect to one another more easily. Open banking frameworks are beginning to emerge in key markets, which could allow a new generation of financial services to be built on top of shared, consented data.

There is also growing focus on what financial tools can do for small and medium-sized businesses — the market traders, small manufacturers, and local service providers who form the engine of African economies but have historically been invisible to formal financial systems. Helping those businesses access capital, manage their cash flow, and connect to regional markets could reshape African economies in ways that consumer-focused fintech alone was never going to achieve.

A Model the World Has Borrowed

The most important part of Africa’s mobile money story is not the technology itself.

It is the proof of concept.

Africa demonstrated that you do not need to import a financial system. You can build one that fits — starting from the real conditions of real people and growing from there. That lesson has since travelled outward. Mobile money models shaped by M-Pesa have been taken up in South Asia, Latin America, and parts of Southeast Asia.

Africa did not just solve its own problem.

It showed the rest of the world a better way to think about one.

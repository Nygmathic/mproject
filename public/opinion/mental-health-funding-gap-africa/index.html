We Talk About Mental Health. We Don't Fund It.

February 13, 2026 5 min read

Something has shifted in the way people talk about mental health.

The conversations have become more open. In certain circles, the old silence has started to break down. Young people especially have been more willing to speak honestly — about anxiety, about burnout, about the strain of navigating economies that demand a great deal and offer little security in return.

This change matters. Language shapes how problems get understood. Naming something is the first step toward addressing it.

But somewhere along the way, the talking started to be mistaken for the solving.

In most of sub-Saharan Africa, the number of mental health professionals available for every 100,000 people sits below one. The global average — itself far too low — is around nine per 100,000. In many African countries, mental health receives less than one percent of the total health budget.

The conversations are getting louder. The systems are not keeping pace.

Who Carries the Weight

When proper mental health services do not exist or are out of reach, the need does not disappear. It shifts.

It moves onto families who look after relatives with serious mental illness without any training or support structure. It moves onto communities, which develop their own ways of understanding and managing what they see around them. It moves into workplaces, where productivity quietly falls away because people are carrying invisible burdens with no help available.

And most heavily, it falls on individuals — who face these things alone, or turn to whatever is at hand, which is often nothing at all.

The people who struggle most are rarely the ones with access to wellness advice online. They are in rural areas, in informal settlements, in communities where a trained counsellor is not just unaffordable but physically does not exist within a hundred kilometres. As the saying goes, a fire that is not put out will not put itself out. Suffering that goes unaddressed does not resolve on its own.

Why the Gap Has Persisted

The mental health funding gap in Africa is not an accident. It is the result of a long series of choices — some deliberate, some inherited, some simply never questioned.

Part of the explanation is historical. Colonial-era health systems were built around infectious diseases, and those priorities became the infrastructure that later governments inherited and struggled to change. Mental health was not visible in the same way a malaria ward was, so it was never built into the foundations.

Part of it is the stigma that has operated at the institutional level, not just the personal one. In many policy environments, mental illness has long been understood as a personal or even spiritual matter, rather than a health condition that medicine can address. That view has been slow to shift where it counts — in budget rooms and planning meetings.

And part of it is the straightforward pressure of limited resources. When a government must choose between maternal health, childhood immunisation, HIV treatment, and mental health services, mental health tends to lose. Not because the need is less real, but because the political cost of ignoring it has historically been lower.

What Changed — and What Still Has Not

The pandemic brought something into the open that had long been pushed aside.

Rates of anxiety and depression increased sharply around the world, and the evidence was too clear to dismiss even for those who had been sceptical of mental health as a policy concern. Some African governments began, cautiously, to include mental health in their health recovery frameworks. Community-based support programmes expanded. Digital mental health platforms designed specifically for African contexts began to emerge.

Some of this is genuinely promising. The innovation is real and the intent is right.

But innovation at the edges does not replace structural investment at the centre. A mental health application is not the same thing as a trained counsellor. A peer support group is valuable and, at the same time, cannot diagnose or treat a severe condition. These things complement formal care. They do not substitute for it.

What Would Actually Make a Difference

Awareness campaigns are necessary. They are not sufficient.

What the situation actually calls for is less complicated than it sounds, even if it is politically difficult: money, training, and infrastructure, sustained over time.

Mental health needs to be woven into primary healthcare — not kept separate in specialist services that most people will never reach. Community health workers need basic training in recognising and responding to mental health needs. Medical schools need to produce more psychiatrists and psychologists. Health insurance frameworks need to cover mental health on the same footing as physical illness.

None of this happens without a deliberate decision to fund it. And that decision requires someone to conclude that the suffering of people who are not visibly productive, who do not march or vote in large blocs, still matters enough to spend money on.

The Test That Counts

The openness in how people talk about mental health today is better than the silence that came before it. That should not be taken away from.

But the real test of whether any society takes mental health seriously is not found in how thoughtfully its people post about it online.

It is found in what its institutions actually spend on it.

By that measure, the work of turning conversation into commitment has barely begun.

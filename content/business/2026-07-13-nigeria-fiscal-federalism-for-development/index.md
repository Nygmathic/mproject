## When Distribution Weakens Development: Lessons from Nigeria's Fiscal History

The relationship between a country's distribution of power and its development trajectory is a complex one. In Nigeria, this nexus has been shaped by the evolution of its fiscal federalism, where the distribution of powers among the federal, state, and local governments has significant implications for economic growth and development. As Dipo Baruwa's insightful analysis reveals, the alignment of incentives among these tiers of government has a direct impact on the country's development trajectory.

In a federal system, the distribution of powers is not merely a constitutional arrangement; it is an incentive structure that determines who makes decisions, who bears responsibility, and who receives the rewards of success. When these relationships are well aligned, the constituent units compete, innovate, and develop. Conversely, when they are not, dependence gradually replaces enterprise, and political competition increasingly eclipses economic competition.

Nigeria's history of fiscal federalism highlights this dynamic. Before independence, the country's fiscal policy was anchored in the principle of financial responsibility, where the regions benefited substantially from what they produced and were expected to "cut their coat according to their cloth." This philosophy shaped the assignment of taxes and revenues, creating a direct relationship between productive effort and fiscal capacity. The derivation principle, a feature recognized in historical analyses of Nigeria's fiscal system, including those of the International Monetary Fund, linked revenue to production, creating strong incentives for the regions to expand their productive base.

The Western Region, for instance, aggressively invested in its cocoa economy, financing landmark investments such as free primary education and industrial estates from the revenues generated from this sector. The Northern Region developed its groundnut and cotton economies, alongside agricultural marketing systems that supported rural productivity. The Eastern Region strengthened palm produce exports and commercial enterprise, using these revenues to expand public services and infrastructure. Competition among the regions was not simply political; it was fundamentally developmental because every improvement in productive capacity strengthened fiscal capacity.

However, as independence approached, a new objective emerged. Building a cohesive federation required balancing regional productivity with national solidarity. The establishment of the Distributable Pool Account reflected this shift, marking a departure from the derivation principle. This change in the incentive structure had significant consequences for Nigeria's development trajectory.

The shift towards a more centralized system of revenue distribution created a disincentive for regional innovation and productivity. Without a direct link between productive effort and fiscal capacity, the regions became dependent on the federal government for funding, rather than generating their own revenues through productive activities. This led to a decline in regional competitiveness, as the regions became less invested in developing their productive sectors.

Today, Nigeria's development challenges are largely a result of this weakened incentive structure. The country's regions have become increasingly dependent on the federal government for funding, rather than generating their own revenues through productive activities. This has led to a decline in regional competitiveness, as the regions become less invested in developing their productive sectors.

To reverse this trend, Nigeria needs to revisit its fiscal federalism and create a more aligned incentive structure. This can be achieved by decentralizing revenue generation and allocation, allowing the regions to benefit directly from their productive efforts. By doing so, the regions will be incentivized to invest in their productive sectors, leading to increased competitiveness and economic growth.

In conclusion, the relationship between distribution and development is complex and multifaceted. Nigeria's fiscal history highlights the importance of creating an aligned incentive structure among the federal, state, and local governments. By decentralizing revenue generation and allocation, the country can create a more competitive and innovative economy, where the regions are incentivized to develop their productive sectors. This is a lesson that Nigeria can learn from its past, and one that holds significant implications for its future development trajectory.

## The Imperative of Fiscal Federalism Reform

Nigeria's fiscal federalism has been characterized by a shift towards a more centralized system of revenue distribution, which has led to a decline in regional competitiveness and innovation. To reverse this trend, the country needs to revisit its fiscal federalism and create a more aligned incentive structure. This can be achieved by decentralizing revenue generation and allocation, allowing the regions to benefit directly from their productive efforts.

## The Path Forward

To achieve this, Nigeria needs to implement a range of reforms, including:

* Decentralizing revenue generation and allocation, allowing the regions to benefit directly from their productive efforts.
* Strengthening the derivation principle, to create a direct link between productive effort and fiscal capacity.
* Increasing transparency and accountability in the management of public finances, to ensure that resources are allocated efficiently and effectively.
* Promoting regional competitiveness and innovation, through investment in education, infrastructure, and productive sectors.

By taking these steps, Nigeria can create a more aligned incentive structure, where the regions are incentivized to develop their productive sectors, leading to increased competitiveness and economic growth. This is a critical step towards reversing the country's development challenges and creating a more prosperous future for all Nigerians.

# Sports betting is increasingly the norm for Gen Z. Here’s why some financial and mental health experts are worried

Wagering on athletic outcomes has expanded rapidly throughout the 2020s, transforming from an occasional pastime into a ubiquitous digital routine for Generation Z. Driven by the expansion of mobile sportsbooks and the emergence of event-driven prediction markets, sports wagering has integrated directly into modern sports fandom. According to a September report from the Bank of America Institute, Gen Z accounted for nearly 50% of all online betting activity in July during the height of the 2026 FIFA World Cup, surpassing millennials in market share for the first time. The pervasiveness of these platforms has reached a threshold where opting out has become the exception rather than the rule among young demographics. As Cynthia Grant, vice president of clinical at Birches Health, observed, having accounts on platforms such as DraftKings or Kalshi is no longer an anomaly, but has fundamentally become part of the contemporary experience of watching sports.

The infrastructure facilitating this behavioral shift traces back to a 2018 U.S. Supreme Court ruling that struck down federal prohibitions, allowing state-authorized sportsbooks to launch across 30 states. Market access accelerated further in early 2025 with the introduction of sports-related event contracts on prediction markets. Because these platforms categorize their product offerings as financial trades rather than traditional wagers, they expanded sports wagering into jurisdictions lacking legal sportsbooks while opening access to individuals under the age of 21. As these applications proliferate on mobile devices—exemplified by platforms like DraftKings, which operationalized digital wagering environments accessible everywhere from desktop laptops to mobile screens—advisors across economic and clinical disciplines are raising significant alarms regarding the resulting financial exposure.

## The Conflation of Wagering and Wealth Building

A primary concern among market analysts is that a significant segment of Generation Z misinterprets betting as a viable vehicle for capital accumulation. Data published in an August retail investor survey by the investment advisory platform Betterment revealed that 66% of Gen Z investors participate in sports betting. More crucially, 52% of Gen Z respondents reported shifting capital originally earmarked for traditional investments directly into sportsbooks, while 26% categorized sports wagering as an explicit component of their long-term financial strategy. The Bank of America Institute corroborated this trend, finding that Gen Z respondents were twice as likely to classify sports betting as a form of investing compared to the 20% rate observed among the broader population. When evaluating prediction markets specifically, general respondents frequently viewed event contracts as investments, but Gen Z demonstrated an even higher propensity to hold that belief.

This conceptual blurring occurs despite clear corporate disclaimers and stark underlying mathematics. Platform operators such as DraftKings and FanDuel maintain that their products serve strictly as entertainment rather than investment instruments, while prediction market operators define event contracts as financial derivatives. Dan Egan, director of behavioral finance and investing at Betterment, pointed out that sports betting increasingly shares digital real estate with conventional investment portfolios on the same smartphones and applications, reinforcing an erroneous equivalence. Egan emphasized that managing sports wagers demands hyperactive engagement that runs entirely contrary to the passive compounding of wealth. Unlike an asset that grows alongside the broader economy with a positive expected return, sports betting represents the exact opposite dynamic, leaving the average participant on both sportsbooks and prediction markets in a negative net financial position.

## Financial Disparities and the Social Amplification of Wins

The economic consequences of this participation are already registering in consumer balance sheets. The Bank of America Institute documented that the median deposit account balance for households engaged in online betting stood at just 59% of the balances maintained by non-betting households. The underlying financial strain is frequently compounded by attempts to recoup losses, which leads participants into deeper debt cycles. Despite these mathematical realities, speculative behavior remains fueled by personal income goals. A survey conducted in August by BadCredit revealed that 44% of respondents engaged with prediction market platforms specifically in hopes of generating supplemental income.

This appetite for wagering is sustained in part by asymmetric social dynamics that obscure the structural losses typical of gambling platforms. Erica Sandberg, a consumer finance expert at BadCredit, explained that social dialogue surrounding these apps suffers from a persistent reporting bias.

## A Fragile Reprieve in the Face of Inflationary Pressures

Food prices in the UK have witnessed a rare moment of respite, with some staples experiencing a decline in costs. However, the overall trend remains uncertain, as analysts warn that higher energy prices in the coming months are likely to push inflation back up. The latest figures from the Office for National Statistics (ONS) reveal that inflation has fallen to 2.6% in the year to June, down from 2.8% in May, driven largely by lower fuel and food prices.

The supermarket price wars, which have intensified over the summer months, have been credited with driving down prices in the year to June. Retailers have been working to tempt customers with summer deals, resulting in a decrease in the cost of items such as margarine and sugar. The British Retail Consortium (BRC) has noted that intense competition between supermarkets has led to lower food inflation. However, this respite may be short-lived, as the recent resumption of hostilities in the Middle East and a new jump in crude oil prices are expected to exert upward pressure on inflation in the coming months.

The government's response to the economic situation has been focused on alleviating pressure on household budgets. New Prime Minister Andy Burnham has taken immediate action to ease pressure on household budgets, including scrapping VAT on domestic electricity bills for the rest of the year from October. Chancellor John Healey has hailed these changes as a "win-win," citing their ability to keep inflation down while helping people afford the essentials. The government has also announced that the bus fare cap in England will be brought back down to £2 in January, providing some relief for commuters.

However, experts warn that the recent drop in inflation may be temporary, and that rising inflation will pose a significant challenge for the new government. Suren Thiru, chief economist at the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales, notes that interest rates are unlikely to increase when the Bank of England meets next week, despite the latest inflation figure being above the Bank's target of 2%. However, she cautions that rising inflation will "likely become a more notable economic headache" for the government, squeezing its fiscal headroom and increasing financial market volatility.

The impact of higher energy bills on inflation is expected to be significant, with Yael Selfin, KPMG's chief economist, warning that second-round effects risk feeding through into wages and more broadly across the economy. Meanwhile, Sarah Coles, head of personal finance at AJ Bell, notes that the recent jump in mortgage rates is likely to make it more difficult for households to secure affordable loans. As the UK prepares for an uncertain economic future, the government's ability to navigate these challenges will be crucial in determining the country's economic trajectory.

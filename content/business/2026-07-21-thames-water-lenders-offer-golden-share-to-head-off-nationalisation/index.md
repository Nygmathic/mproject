## A Last-Ditch Effort: Thames Water Lenders Make Concessions to Avoid Nationalisation

Thames Water's lenders are making a desperate bid to prevent the UK's second-largest water utility from falling under government control. In a move that has been described as a "golden share" offer, the London & Valley Water (L&VW) consortium of lenders is proposing to give the government veto powers over major decisions, including mergers and acquisitions. This offer comes as the new government, led by Prime Minister Andy Burnham, has made it clear that it wants to see greater public control of "life's essentials", including water supply.

The L&VW proposal is a significant departure from the previous £10bn deal that was rejected by the government in June. That deal, which involved writing off nearly half of Thames Water's debt and injecting new cash, was deemed insufficient by the government. However, sources close to the creditors have revealed that the new offer includes hundreds of millions in additional funding, making it a more attractive proposition.

A golden share would give the government a significant stake in Thames Water, allowing it to shape the company's direction and policies. This is seen as a way for the government to ensure that the company prioritizes the public interest, particularly in terms of environmental protection. The concept of a golden share is not new, having been used in the past to retain government control over companies of national significance, such as Royal Mail and Rolls-Royce.

The proposal is not without its risks, however. If the government were to accept the offer and then later decide to nationalize Thames Water, the lenders would pursue payment in full of the outstanding debts, which could leave the government with a multi-billion-pound bill. This has happened in previous cases, and the government would need to be confident that the benefits of nationalization outweigh the potential costs.

Thames Water's financial woes have been well-documented, with the company warning that it could run out of cash by November. The company has recently imposed a hosepipe ban on all its customers, citing the UK's exceptionally dry spring. This move has sparked fears that the company is on the verge of collapse, and that households will be left without access to basic services.

The government's stance on the issue remains unclear, with a spokesperson saying that Thames Water "remains financially stable, but we stand ready for all eventualities, including applying for a Special Administration Regime [SAR] if that were to become necessary". An SAR is a form of temporary nationalization that would allow the government to recoup some taxpayer cash if Thames Water were sold again to a private buyer.

The L&VW consortium has defended its proposal, arguing that it offers the fastest and most reliable route to solving Thames Water's complex problems and improving outcomes for customers and the environment. A spokesperson said that the new deal "achieves this without any government funding or cost to taxpayers". However, the proposal will need to be reviewed by Ofwat, the regulator, before it can be finalized.

The outcome of this proposal will have significant implications for the UK's water sector, and for the government's plans to increase public control over essential services. As the government weighs its options, one thing is clear: the fate of Thames Water hangs in the balance, and the consequences of nationalization or private control will be felt for years to come.

## The Consequences of Nationalization

Nationalization of Thames Water would have significant consequences for the company, its customers, and the government. On one hand, it would allow the government to exert greater control over the company's policies and direction, potentially leading to improved environmental protection and customer service. On the other hand, it would come at a significant cost to taxpayers, who would be left with a multi-billion-pound bill.

The government's stance on nationalization is likely to be influenced by the outcome of the L&VW proposal. If the proposal is accepted, it is possible that the government may decide to take a more hands-off approach to nationalization, allowing the company to operate with greater autonomy. However, if the proposal is rejected, the government may be forced to consider nationalization as a last resort.

The fate of Thames Water will also have implications for the wider water sector. If the company is nationalized, it could set a precedent for other water utilities, potentially leading to a wave of nationalization across the sector. On the other hand, if the company is allowed to continue operating privately, it may set a precedent for other companies to prioritize profits over public interest.

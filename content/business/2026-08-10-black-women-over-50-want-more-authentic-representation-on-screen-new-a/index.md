## A Call for Authenticity: Black Women Over 50 Demand Better Representation on Screen

AARP's latest research has laid bare a stark disconnect between Hollywood's portrayal of Black women over 50 and the lived experiences of their audience. The study, unveiled at the Martha's Vineyard African American Film Festival, reveals that nearly 96% of Black adults believe it is crucial to see multiple generations represented on screen, with 63% citing the importance of reflecting real-life relationships and family dynamics.

However, the findings also starkly illustrate that this is not the reality that currently exists. A staggering 95% of Black adults assert that it is vital to see Black women aged 50 and older represented on screen, yet a meager one in four respondents genuinely believes that current portrayals accurately reflect their lives. The study's data is a clarion call to the entertainment industry, which has long been criticized for its lack of diversity and authenticity in its storytelling.

According to the research, Black audiences are among the most engaged consumers of entertainment, attending movies and watching television and streaming content at higher rates than the general population. However, this enthusiasm is not being matched by the quality of the content they see. More than half of respondents, 54%, reported that seeing Black women 50 and older portrayed on screen made them feel more positive about their own aging. This finding takes on added significance when juxtaposed with the fact that nearly 46% of Black adults feel underrepresented in movies and television.

The disconnect between what Black audiences want to see and what they actually see on screen is a symptom of a broader societal issue. As AARP CEO Dr. Myechia Minter-Jordan astutely observes, "Black audiences are telling us clearly what they want to see, and there's a real opportunity for studios and creators to meet this moment." The industry's reluctance to engage with this reality is a missed opportunity that exacerbates existing societal issues.

Authentic representation is not merely a social responsibility for studios and creators; it is also a business imperative. As Dr. Minter-Jordan notes, "Audiences respond to authentic representations of themselves on screen. Black adults alone represent nearly $2 trillion in buying power, plus our Longevity Economy report found that adults 50+ contribute $12.5 trillion to the economy annually. If you're not speaking to them in a way that connects, you are missing a significant business opportunity."

The study's findings serve as a timely reminder that storytelling has the power to shape perceptions of aging, identity, and possibility. As Dr. Minter-Jordan so eloquently puts it, "One of the reasons we created Movies for Grownups, and why we are participating in the Martha's Vineyard African American Film Festival, is to demonstrate that entertainment can be a tremendous positive force in helping people develop more nuanced views of aging."

In an era where authenticity and representation are in short supply, AARP's research is a beacon of hope. The entertainment industry would do well to heed the call of its audience and strive for a more inclusive and authentic portrayal of Black women over 50. The data is clear: audiences respond to stories that reflect their lived experiences, and by doing so, creators can tap into a vast and untapped market of consumers. It is time for Hollywood to take note and seize this moment.

## The New Normal in Tech Financing: A.I. Data Centers Come at a Steep Price

As the global economy grapples with the far-reaching implications of [artificial intelligence](/tags/artificial-intelligence/), a quiet revolution is unfolding in the world of big tech financing. [Oracle](/tags/oracle/), the corporate giant founded by Larry Ellison, has been at the forefront of this shift, using its vast wealth to fuel an unprecedented spending spree on A.I. data centers. But this financial juggernaut comes with a steep price: Oracle's debt rating has been downgraded to just one notch above junk bond status, and the company's stock market value has taken a significant hit.

The stakes are high, not just for [Oracle](/tags/oracle/), but for the entire U.S. economy. A.I. money, as it's being called, is contributing an estimated 1.1 percent to the nation's economic growth, according to JPMorgan Asset Management. This phenomenon has sent shockwaves through financial markets, with companies like Oracle, Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta racing to build hyperscale data centers to house their A.I. ambitions. The numbers are staggering: these data centers and the infrastructure that supports them require enormous sums of money, enriching various segments of the stock market and creating a ripple effect that's being felt across the economy.

Oracle's foray into A.I. data centers has been particularly aggressive, with the company selling bonds to raise the necessary funds. However, this approach has put a strain on Oracle's finances, with its debt rating taking a hit on July 9. S&P Global downgraded Oracle's debt, citing the company's deteriorating finances. The stock market has also taken a toll on Larry Ellison's personal wealth, with his holdings decreasing by around $230 billion since September, according to FactSet data.

While Oracle's financial woes are a cause for concern, other big tech firms like Microsoft seem to be navigating the A.I. data center landscape with greater ease. Microsoft boasts a Triple-A credit rating, a feat that Oracle can only dream of. Mariya Entina, a portfolio manager for DoubleLine, notes that Microsoft's stronger financials put it in a better position to weather the costs of A.I. data centers. "It's essential to have enough information to differentiate," Entina said in an interview.

The contrast between Oracle and Microsoft highlights the risks and rewards associated with A.I.-driven spending. While Oracle's aggressive approach to A.I. data centers has raised concerns about its financial stability, Microsoft's more measured approach has allowed it to maintain a strong credit rating. The question on everyone's mind is: will Microsoft's A.I. data center spending spree ultimately land it in the same trouble as Oracle?

As the tech industry continues to evolve, one thing is clear: the cost of A.I. data centers is not just a financial issue, but a strategic one. The companies that can navigate this landscape successfully will be the ones that emerge as leaders in the A.I. era. For now, Oracle's financial struggles serve as a cautionary tale, highlighting the risks of unchecked A.I.-driven spending and the importance of prudent financial management.

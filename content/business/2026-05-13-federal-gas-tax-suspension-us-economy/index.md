## A Sputtering Economy Finds New Fuel in the Federal Gas Tax Debate

As the US economy teeters on the brink of another recession, President Trump's proposal to suspend the federal gas tax has ignited a heated debate about the merits of such a move. At first glance, the idea of saving drivers a few dollars a month may seem appealing, but experts warn that the implications of such a decision go far beyond the pump. With the global economy increasingly interconnected, any significant changes to the US tax code have far-reaching consequences for international trade, investment, and energy markets.

The 18.4-cent-a-gallon federal gas tax, which was first introduced in 1932, has been a cornerstone of US infrastructure funding for nearly a century. The tax generates approximately $34 billion annually, which is used to maintain and improve the country's vast network of roads, highways, and bridges. However, with the rise of electric vehicles and changing consumer behavior, the traditional gas tax is no longer seen as an equitable or efficient source of revenue. In an effort to modernize the tax code and address concerns about the environmental impact of transportation, lawmakers have begun exploring alternative funding mechanisms.

Proponents of suspending the gas tax argue that the move would provide much-needed relief to American drivers, who are already feeling the pinch of rising fuel prices and stagnant wages. They also point out that the federal government has a history of providing temporary tax relief during times of economic uncertainty. However, opponents of the proposal warn that suspending the gas tax would create a significant funding gap, which would need to be filled through other means. This could lead to even higher taxes on other goods and services, or, in the worst-case scenario, reduced infrastructure investment and maintenance.

Historically, the US has struggled to balance its love of big highways and roads with its commitments to environmental sustainability and social responsibility. The gas tax has long been a contentious issue, with critics arguing that it disproportionately affects low-income households and communities of color. In recent years, there has been a growing push for alternative funding mechanisms, such as a vehicle miles traveled (VMT) tax, which would charge drivers based on the distance they travel rather than the fuel they consume. While this approach has its own set of challenges and controversies, it is seen by many as a more equitable and environmentally friendly way to fund transportation infrastructure.

As the debate over the gas tax continues to unfold, international observers are watching with interest. The global economy is increasingly dependent on the US, and any significant changes to the country's tax code have far-reaching implications for trade, investment, and energy markets. In Asia, for example, countries like China and India are closely monitoring the US's progress on electric vehicle adoption and infrastructure funding. In Latin America, policymakers are grappling with the challenges of modernizing their own transportation systems, and are keenly aware of the potential benefits and pitfalls of adopting similar funding mechanisms.

As the stakes grow higher, lawmakers on both sides of the aisle are weighing in on the issue. Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell has expressed skepticism about suspending the gas tax, arguing that it would create a significant funding gap and set a bad precedent for future tax policy. Meanwhile, Representative Earl Blumenauer (D-OR) has proposed a more comprehensive approach, which would include a phase-out of the gas tax over time and the introduction of a VMT tax. As the debate rages on, one thing is clear: the future of the US transportation infrastructure will be shaped by a complex interplay of economic, environmental, and social factors.

I believed in the promise. When massive open online courses (MOOCs) first appeared, I genuinely thought we were witnessing an educational revolution. Free lectures from world-class professors at elite universities. Anyone with internet access could learn quantum physics from MIT or economics from Stanford. YouTube became an infinite classroom. The narrative was compelling: information asymmetry was solved. Education was being democratized.

Yet here I am, years later, watching educational inequality persist and even deepen. Not because online education failed. But because we fundamentally misunderstood what education actually is.

## The Assumption That Broke

We assumed that access to information equals access to opportunity. We were half right—half isn't good enough.

Information is genuinely available. That part worked. But education is different. Education requires motivation, structure, mentorship, and—crucially—recognition that what you've learned is valuable.

An unemployed person with no high school diploma can watch Stanford lectures for free. But employers won't hire them based on those lectures. A talented person without formal credentials can learn calculus, programming, even advanced statistics online. But they still need credentials to get into universities. They still face doors that close because they don't have the right piece of paper.

Free information solved the "access to content" problem. It completely failed to solve the "access to opportunity" problem.

## The Hidden Barriers Are Real

I've spent time understanding what actually prevents people from learning online, and it's not lack of desire. It's that education, beyond basic information transfer, requires things that online education can't provide:

**Time** is the first barrier. When you're working multiple jobs to survive, free courses aren't actually free. They cost time you don't have. A person working two jobs can't spend three hours daily on coursework, no matter how motivated they are. Online education presupposes a level of free time that many people simply don't have.

**Environment** matters more than we acknowledge. Learning requires relative stability, quiet space, and psychological safety. Homelessness, family instability, constant stress—these prevent learning, regardless of how good the content is. You can't focus on calculus when you're worried about where you'll sleep. You can't engage deeply with material when you're traumatized by poverty.

**Mentorship** is something online education fundamentally struggles to replicate. A great online course can transfer information. But it can't provide the guidance of a teacher who knows you personally, who believes in you, who helps you navigate setbacks and self-doubt. That relationship is what transforms information into learning.

**Credentials** are the hard truth everyone avoids. Online courses can teach skills. But degrees open doors. An employer might be impressed by your self-taught programming. But they're more likely to hire someone with a degree from a recognized institution. The system is rigged to value credentials over demonstrated ability.

**Motivation** is harder to maintain alone. When you're surviving day-to-day, taking a course about future advancement feels like a luxury. When you've been failed by institutions repeatedly, trusting in online education feels naive. When no one in your community has used online learning to change their life, it feels like a scam.

## What Actually Changes Things

The most effective educational interventions I've studied aren't about content. They're about everything else:

- **Mentorship programs** that connect learners with guides who believe in them
- **Structured programs** with real teachers, not just videos
- **Financial support** that enables people to study instead of work immediately
- **Community** where learners support each other
- **Credentials** that employers actually recognize

Online education can supplement these. But it can't replace them. And we've used online education as an excuse to not provide these fundamentals.

## The Uncomfortable Truth

Here's what I think we don't want to admit: some people will always learn more effectively in structured environments with real people. Not everyone is a self-directed online learner. And that's not a personal failing—it's just how humans learn. We're social creatures. We learn through relationship.

The wealthy can afford structured education with mentorship. They send their kids to private schools with small class sizes and engaged teachers. They hire tutors. They join communities of learning. The poor are told, "Use the free online courses." We've made a virtue of the cheapest option and called it equality.

## What Real Change Requires

Real educational equity requires investment. Genuine, substantial investment:

**Fund teachers, mentors, and students**—not just create platforms. Pay excellent teachers well enough that the profession attracts talented people. Provide financial support so students don't have to work while learning.

**Create intentional structures** that recognize some people need more support, not less. Adaptive pedagogy that meets people where they are.

**Build flexibility** into education so people can combine work, family, and education without choosing between them.

**Recognize credentials** from diverse pathways. Make online learning count. Make alternative credentials valuable.

**Create community** so people learn together, not in isolation.

## My Hope

I haven't given up on online education. But I've radically lowered expectations for what it can accomplish alone. It's a tool. A valuable tool. But a tool requires infrastructure to be useful.

The path forward requires admitting we were wrong about the simplicity of the solution. It requires investing in education the way we say we care about it. It requires accepting that some approaches cost money, and that's okay because education is worth it.

I've watched something troubling unfold over the past few years. We celebrate remote work as liberation. Tech workers in my circle praise the flexibility, the commute savings, the ability to live literally anywhere. But this celebration masks a troubling reality that I can't ignore: remote work has become a new form of inequality.

The narrative about remote work is overwhelmingly told by people who benefit from it. The software engineer who sold his Nairobi apartment and bought a bigger one in Mombasa. The designer who moved from Nairobi to a rural area, keeping her San Francisco salary. The consultant who negotiated flexible hours and now works from coffee shops three days a week. These stories are presented as inevitable and universal progress.

But this narrative completely excludes the majority of workers.

## The Two-Tier Workforce Is Growing

Remote work isn't available to everyone. Nurses can't care for patients from home. Plumbers can't fix pipes remotely. Construction workers, retail staff, hospitality workers, service industry employees—they're locked into physical workplaces. Not by choice. By necessity.

Meanwhile, knowledge workers celebrate their newfound freedom. They negotiate flexible arrangements. They save money on commuting—money that adds up dramatically over time. They enjoy improved work-life balance because they're not exhausted by traffic and long commutes. They have the ability to live in lower-cost areas while maintaining high salaries.

The gap between those who can work remotely and those who can't continues to widen. And it's not a random gap. It's a gap that correlates almost perfectly with education level, which correlates with income level, which correlates with social privilege.

## The Productivity Argument Collapsed

What fascinates me is that the productivity argument—the justification for why some jobs require physical presence—doesn't hold up. Companies pushed remote work during the pandemic because they had to. Then something remarkable happened: productivity didn't decline. In many cases, it improved. People working from home, with fewer distractions and more control over their environment, often accomplished more.

Yet many industries never gave their workers this option because of outdated assumptions about presence and productivity. A security guard must be present—that's legitimate. A factory worker must be present—also legitimate. A teacher often must be present, though this is changing faster than we expected.

But hospitality workers? Some of their administrative work could be done remotely. Couldn't scheduling, customer relations, and data entry happen from anywhere? Retail staff? Couldn't some roles shift to remote customer service? Even in healthcare, some administrative and consultative work could happen remotely.

We've drawn artificial lines about which work requires physical presence, and I'm increasingly convinced those lines are drawn based on tradition and habit more than genuine necessity.

## The Economic Inequality Is Real

Remote work has created a troubling economic divide that I think about constantly. Those in jobs that allow remote work have access to things others don't:

- **Lower housing costs** by living in less expensive areas while maintaining high salaries
- **Flexibility to care for family** without losing income or opportunity
- **Reduced transportation costs** that free up significant money
- **Mental health benefits from autonomy** that are scientifically documented

Those stuck in physical workplaces don't have these advantages. And they tend to be lower-income workers, who ironically would benefit most from these advantages. The worker earning $300 a month as a security guard has no flexibility. The worker earning $500 a month in hospitality faces rigid schedules. These aren't people who can afford to live far from their workplace—they often live nearby because transportation would consume too much of their limited income.

## What Actually Needs To Happen

We need to be honest: remote work won't become universal. Some work genuinely requires physical presence. But we can be dramatically more intentional about expanding access.

**For employers**, the work is to audit roles and ask genuinely: which tasks truly require physical presence? Then think creatively. Some hospitality work could be split—customer-facing roles in person, administrative work remote. Some retail could shift some functions to remote customer service.

**For policymakers**, I'd argue we need to recognize that transportation, childcare, and housing access are fundamental productivity issues. If we can't afford to commute, we can't work. If we can't afford childcare while we work, we can't work. These should be policy priorities.

**For remote workers**, we need to acknowledge privilege. Advocate for remote options in all industries where feasible. But also, don't use your newfound flexibility to isolate yourself. Build solidarity with those without it.

## The Conversation We're Avoiding

We celebrate remote work as a personal benefit. We should be celebrating it as a labor right—and fighting to extend it to everyone who can benefit.

The future of work isn't remote or in-office. It's flexible, equitable, and intentional. And we're not there yet.

## Monumental Shifts: Trump's Decision to Shrink Two Utah Landmarks

On a day when the American Southwest was already parched and cracked, the news landed like a blow: President Donald Trump has sharply cut the size of two of Utah's most treasured national monuments, sparking an immediate outcry from Native American tribes and environmental groups. The Bears Ears and Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monuments, which have been a source of pride and identity for the region's indigenous peoples, are now significantly smaller, leaving behind a legacy of contested land use and potential economic instability.

## A Land Marked for Contention

The Bears Ears Monument, which spans over 1.5 million acres of rugged, red rock country, was established in 2016 by President Barack Obama to protect the region's rich cultural and natural heritage. Native American tribes, including the Navajo, Ute, and Hopi, have long considered the area sacred, with ancestral sites, artifacts, and petroglyphs that hold deep spiritual significance. The monument's designation was a hard-won victory for these communities, who had lobbied tirelessly for federal recognition and protection of their ancestral lands. However, Trump's decision to reduce the monument's size by nearly 90% has left many wondering about the future of these sacred sites and the communities that depend on them.

The Grand Staircase-Escalante Monument, which was established in 1996, is another beloved Utah landmark that has been reduced in size by nearly 50% by Trump's order. This vast wilderness area, which covers over 1.7 million acres, is home to an incredible array of flora and fauna, including rare species of plants and animals that can be found nowhere else on Earth. The monument's designation has brought significant economic benefits to the region, with tourism and recreation generating millions of dollars in revenue each year. However, the reduced size of the monument has raised concerns about the long-term viability of these industries and the potential impact on local communities.

## A Legacy of Contested Land Use

The history of land use in the American Southwest is complex and often contentious. For centuries, Native American tribes have faced displacement, marginalization, and cultural erasure, as European-American settlers and later, the federal government, sought to assert control over the region's land and resources. The creation of national monuments was a key strategy for preserving the region's natural and cultural heritage, but it has also been a source of tension and conflict. Many Native American tribes have long argued that the federal government has failed to adequately recognize and protect their rights to ancestral lands, and that the creation of national monuments has often been a way to circumvent these rights.

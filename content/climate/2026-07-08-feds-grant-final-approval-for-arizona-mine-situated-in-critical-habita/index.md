## **A Divided Decision: Feds Grant Final Approval for Arizona Mine Amid Environmental Concerns**

Deep within the Coronado National Forest in southern Arizona, a contentious decision has been made that will reshape the region's landscape. The U.S. Forest Service has granted final approval for South32's Hermosa project, a proposed underground mine that will extract critical minerals, including zinc, lead, and silver, with the potential for manganese and copper. This decision has sparked outrage among local environmental groups, who claim that the federal agency failed to address fundamental legal and environmental concerns.

Located in Patagonia, Arizona, one of the state's "sky islands," the Hermosa project is situated in a region renowned for its biodiversity. The area's unique geography, characterized by isolated mountain ranges and a hot desert floor, provides a vital habitat for numerous species, including the endangered jaguar and Mexican spotted owl. Critics argue that the mine's construction will have a devastating impact on these species and the local ecosystem, citing the potential for habitat disruption, water pollution, and climate change exacerbation.

The project's approval has granted 31 exemptions under the Coronado National Forest Plan, which environmental groups claim conflicts with guidance for the area's public lands. Moreover, the federal agency has deferred analysis of long-term concerns, including impacts on local water resources. These concerns are not unfounded, given the mine's projected 70-year life span and the estimated 195,000 acre-feet of water that will be removed from the local aquifer. This dewatering will lead to a cone of depression, with the water table drawn down by 250 feet, affecting wells within three miles of the mine.

Anna Darian, executive director of the Patagonia Area Resource Alliance, expressed her disappointment and frustration with the decision. "Our community has spent years participating in this process because we believed the agencies responsible for protecting our public lands would carefully evaluate the facts before making a decision," she stated. "Instead, the Forest Service approved this project while serious legal questions remain unresolved, leaving many residents wondering whether these agencies are still serving the public interest."

The Hermosa project is the first to be added to the Fixing America's Surface Transportation Act's (FAST-41) program, designed to streamline federal permitting. This decision has been touted as a strategic investment in America's energy and manufacturing future, reducing dependence on foreign supplies, largely from China. The project's proponents, including South32's president, Pat Risner, and Secretary of Agriculture Brooke Rollins, emphasize the importance of increasing domestic mineral production to ensure energy security.

However, the economic benefits of the mine come with significant costs to the local community. The town of Patagonia, city of Nogales, and Santa Cruz County are actively exploring a community benefits agreement with the mine, which could provide financial incentives and job opportunities. Nevertheless, concerns remain about the mine's impact on local water resources and the potential for environmental degradation.

The Hermosa project is a complex issue, with competing interests and perspectives. While proponents argue that the mine will create jobs and stimulate economic growth, critics highlight the risks associated with environmental degradation and water pollution. As the project moves forward, it is imperative that the federal agency and local stakeholders work together to address these concerns and ensure that the Hermosa project is executed in a responsible and sustainable manner.

The decision has also sparked a larger discussion about the role of federal agencies in protecting public lands and the balance between economic development and environmental conservation. As the United States continues to grapple with the challenges of climate change and energy security, the Hermosa project serves as a stark reminder of the need for responsible and sustainable management of natural resources.

## Climate's Unwelcome Harvest

As Seth Kroeck stepped out into the crisp morning air of his wild blueberry farm near Brunswick, Maine, he couldn't shake the feeling that this summer's growing season had been anything but normal. The vibrant green of the past had given way to a premature red, a telltale sign that the plants were struggling. And it wasn't just his farm that was suffering – across the state, wild blueberry fields were turning red too soon, their delicate balance disrupted by the increasingly erratic climate. The once-thriving harvest had shriveled, a stark reminder of the devastating impact climate change was having on the region's most prized industry.

The stakes for Maine's wild blueberry farmers, who rely on the state's unique combination of acidic soils and cool, moist summers to cultivate their prized crop, are higher than ever. The state's wild blueberry industry generates over $10 million annually, with the majority of that revenue coming from the sale of frozen berries to major food manufacturers. But as the climate continues to shift, the very foundation of this industry is being eroded. Rising temperatures and changing precipitation patterns are causing plants to bloom earlier, making them more susceptible to frost damage, while droughts like the one that gripped the state last summer leave berries shriveled and unripe.

To understand the full extent of the problem, it's essential to consider the broader context of climate change in Maine. The state has warmed by nearly 3 degrees Fahrenheit over the past century, which may not seem like a lot, but when combined with the region's unique geography, it has significant implications for the local ecosystem. Maine's rugged coastline and proximity to the Gulf of Maine make it particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, from rising sea levels to more frequent and intense storms. And yet, it's not just the physical environment that's being affected – the human costs of climate change are also becoming increasingly apparent.

For Seth Kroeck and his fellow farmers, the consequences of climate change are felt in the pocketbook. Wild blueberry prices have skyrocketed in recent years, making it increasingly difficult for small-scale farmers like Kroeck to stay afloat. "It's not just the drought that's the problem – it's the unpredictability of it all," he explained, shaking his head. "We're used to planning our crop cycles months in advance, but with climate change, that's becoming increasingly impossible. It's like trying to predict the weather in a way that's just not possible anymore." And it's not just the farmers who are struggling – the broader community is also feeling the effects, from the loss of seasonal jobs to the strain on local food systems.

As the impacts of climate change continue to unfold, it's worth looking to other regions for historical parallels. In the 1930s, the Dust Bowl ravaged the American Midwest, causing widespread crop failures and displacement of entire communities. While the specific circumstances of the Dust Bowl and Maine's climate crisis are different, the underlying causes are eerily similar. And just as the Dust Bowl led to significant changes in agricultural policy and practices, it's possible that the current climate crisis could spark a similar sea change in the way we approach sustainable agriculture.

## The Ripple Effects

Back on the Crystal Spring Farm, Seth Kroeck is taking steps to adapt to the changing climate. He's begun experimenting with new crop varieties that are more resilient to temperature fluctuations and drought, and has also started using more efficient irrigation systems to reduce water waste. But even with these efforts, the challenges facing Maine's wild blueberry farmers are far from over. As the state's climate continues to shift, it's likely that the once-thriving harvest will continue to suffer, with devastating consequences for the local economy and community.

Reactions to the crisis are varied, with some arguing that the solution lies in more aggressive government intervention to support struggling farmers, while others advocate for a more laissez-faire approach, letting market forces dictate the pace of change. But one thing is clear: the future of Maine's wild blueberry industry hangs in the balance, and the consequences of inaction will be severe.

As the seasons change and the next growing cycle looms, Seth Kroeck and his fellow farmers will be watching the skies and the weather forecasts with growing anxiety. Will this year's harvest be better than the last? Only time will tell. But one thing is certain: the clock is ticking, and the stakes are higher than ever before.

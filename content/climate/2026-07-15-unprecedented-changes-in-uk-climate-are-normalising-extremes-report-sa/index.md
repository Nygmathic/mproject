## UK's Changing Climate Reality: A Normalisation of Extremes

As the United Kingdom basks in the sweltering heat of its third deadly heatwave in two months, scientists warn that the nation is witnessing unprecedented changes to its climate. The annual State of the UK Climate report, published in the International Journal of Climatology, paints a stark picture of a country where once-rare extreme weather events are now becoming the new normal.

The report highlights the past four years in the UK as among the top five hottest on record, with the last year emerging as the hottest on record. Temperatures pushed to dizzying heights by carbon pollution are now being experienced as commonplace rather than exceptional. "What we used to think of as extreme, we increasingly consider as normal," says Mike Kendon, a scientist at the National Climate Information Centre and lead author of the report. "We are seeing unprecedented changes continuing … and every year adds to this body of evidence."

The data stretching back to 1884 shows the UK has never experienced a year as hot as 2025, with temperatures reaching alarming levels due to the accumulation of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. England's spring of 2025 was its driest in a century, while the country as a whole witnessed its warmest spring and summer on record. The trend is unmistakable: the UK's climate is on the move – literally.

## Climate Shocks and Infrastructure Strains

As the country's climate becomes increasingly hot and wet, the infrastructure and institutions that underpin daily life are struggling to cope. Fire services are battling to contain wildfires that are spreading rapidly due to persistent hot and dry conditions. The "firewave" that has engulfed the UK is a stark reminder of the devastating impact of climate change on the environment, human health, and the economy.

Persistent droughts, exacerbated by hot and dry summers, are expected to worsen as average temperatures continue to rise. In spring 2025, most of England and Wales received less than half of the average rainfall for the same period in 1991-2020. England's river flow from March to August 2025 was the second lowest on record. The consequences of these climate shocks are far-reaching, with hospitals, schools, and transportation systems all feeling the strain.

## A Human and Economic Toll

The human cost of climate change is starkly evident in the UK, where the latest heatwave has claimed hundreds of lives. The separate analysis published on Sunday found that the May and June heatwaves killed around 2,700 people in England and Wales. The report highlights the disproportionate impact of climate change on the most vulnerable members of society, who are often least equipped to cope with the consequences of extreme weather events.

Liz Bentley, the head of the Royal Meteorological Society, notes that "the way we experience climate change most is through the weather extremes. Climate change has been described by scientists for many years but is now increasingly being felt by the UK population in their own homes and communities." The economic toll of climate change is also significant, with the UK's agricultural sector, already struggling with drought and heat stress, facing an uncertain future.

## A Future of Unabated Change

As the UK's climate continues to change at an unprecedented rate, scientists warn that the country is heading for a future of unabated extremes. "A lot of our infrastructure, housing stock, agriculture, and health systems are based on a climate that is no longer represented by recent observations," says Kendon. "A final point, if you find this sobering enough, is these changes are set to continue. We're not saying that where we are now is where we're going to stay."

As the UK navigates this uncharted territory, it is clear that the nation must adapt to the changing climate reality. But what does this mean for the future of the UK, and what lessons can be learned from its experience? The answers to these questions will shape the course of the country's future, and the world's, for generations to come.

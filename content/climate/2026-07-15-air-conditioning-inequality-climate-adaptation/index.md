## The Growing Divide: Access to Air Conditioning in a Warming World

As the mercury soars across Europe and the United States, television screens are filled with familiar images: heat maps shaded deep red, schools closing, rail lines slowing, wildfires spreading, and emergency rooms treating growing numbers of people with heat-related illnesses. The advice from public officials is equally familiar: stay indoors, drink plenty of water, and, if possible, turn on the air conditioning. But for millions of households, especially in the global south, this is a luxury they cannot afford.

In the United States and Europe, millions of households already struggle to pay their electric bills, and rising temperatures mean they will need to use even more electricity simply to stay safe. In contrast, much of the global south faces a fundamentally different challenge. During a recent visit to India, Mark Wolfe, executive director of the National Energy Assistance Directors Association, came away with the impression that government officials understood exactly what needed to be done to mitigate the effects of extreme heat. However, their challenge was not a lack of ideas, but a lack of resources.

The Lancet estimated that hundreds of thousands of people already die from heat each year, and the burden is expected to grow most rapidly in south Asia and Africa. The United Nations has warned that extreme heat is widening inequality, slowing economic development, and taking a growing human toll. The people facing the greatest risks are often those who have contributed least to global greenhouse gas emissions.

The solution is not simply for western countries to ship millions of air conditioners to developing countries. In many of these places, the electric grid could not support them even if every family could afford one. Instead, the west should help countries in the global south invest in cheaper, more stable forms of clean and secure energy to build the infrastructure needed to live safely in a warmer climate. Low-income countries cannot build this infrastructure on their own.

Development banks, international climate funds, private investors, and wealthier nations will all need to become partners in financing the next generation of climate adaptation. This should not be viewed as traditional foreign aid. It is an investment in global health, economic stability, and human resilience.

However, this is not just a humanitarian obligation; it is also a strategic investment. If the United States and Europe fail to become meaningful partners in financing climate adaptation, other countries will fill that vacuum, expanding both their economic influence and their geopolitical ties across much of the developing world.

Climate policy has long been measured by how much carbon the world can avoid emitting. That will always matter. But over the coming decades, it may also be judged by whether the world's wealthiest countries were willing to help billions of people in the global south adapt to a hotter planet.

In the United States and Europe, access to affordable cooling is increasingly a question of political priorities. In much of the global south, it is a question of resources and, increasingly, survival. The next phase of the climate debate has to be about more than reducing emissions. It also has to be about helping people survive the climate we have already created by investing in reliable electricity, affordable cooling, and heat resilience in the countries that can least afford them.

The statistics are stark: extreme heat kills about 2,000 people a year in the US, and Europe's heat dome killed more than 1,300 people in less than two weeks this June. In the US, low-income energy assistance to help families pay their home energy bills is a core solution, but funding for this program is only sufficient to help about one out of six eligible families. However, this is not just an American problem; it is a global one.

As the world's population grows and temperatures rise, the demand for cooling will increase exponentially. In many parts of the global south, this will be a significant challenge, especially for those who have already been pushed to the margins by poverty, inequality, and a lack of access to basic services. The consequences of inaction will be severe: heat-related illnesses, displacement, and even death.

## The Human Cost of Climate Inaction

The human cost of climate inaction is already being felt. In developing countries, the effects of extreme heat are often exacerbated by overcrowding, poor sanitation, and a lack of access to healthcare. This is not just a humanitarian crisis; it is also an economic one. The World Bank estimates that climate change could push up to 143 million people into poverty by 2050.

The solution is not to ship air conditioners to developing countries, but to invest in the infrastructure needed to build a more resilient and equitable future. This requires a fundamental shift in how we think about climate policy. We need to move beyond just reducing emissions and start thinking about how to help people survive the climate we have already created.

## A New Era of Climate Cooperation

The next phase of the climate debate has to be about more than just reducing emissions. It has to be about helping people survive the climate we have already created. This requires a new era of climate cooperation between developed and developing countries. It requires investment in reliable electricity, affordable cooling, and heat resilience in the countries that can least afford them.

The stakes are high, but the rewards are greater. By working together, we can build a more resilient and equitable future for all. We can help people survive the climate we have already created and avoid the worst impacts of climate change. The question is whether we have the will.

## The Road Ahead

The road ahead is long and difficult, but it is not impossible. We have the knowledge, the technology, and the resources to build a more resilient and equitable future. What we need now is the will to act. We need to prioritize climate adaptation and invest in the infrastructure needed to build a more resilient future.

The next great climate divide will not be between the countries that emitted the most carbon and those that emitted the least. It will be between those that had the resources to adapt and those that did not. We have a choice to make. We can choose to prioritize the interests of the wealthy and powerful, or we can choose to prioritize the interests of the vulnerable and the marginalized.

The choice is ours. The future is ours to shape. Let us choose to build a more resilient and equitable future for all.

## Life on the Brink: The Deadly Spiral of Climate Scepticism

The UK is teetering on the edge of a catastrophic climate disaster, where the devastating consequences of climate extremes are being exploited by politicians for their own gain. The recent unprecedented heatwaves, which may have killed thousands of people, are a stark reminder of the dangers of inaction in the face of a warming world. As the country grapples with the fallout of climate-related events, it's becoming increasingly clear that the failure to close the adaptation gap has dire consequences for politicians who fail to act.

The adaptation gap is the difference between the climate society is built for and the one that exists. It's a yawningly wide gap that has been warned about by climate-risk advisers for years, yet the government has failed to address it. The consequences of this inaction are now being felt, as children suffer in overheating schools, NHS trusts strain under record-breaking demand, and national security is compromised due to food shortages. The politics of the adaptation gap are starting to play out, with climate-sceptic parties exploiting floods and fires to make political capital.

Take Spain, for example, where devastating floods in the Valencia region in October 2024 supercharged the disaster. Climate breakdown was the underlying cause, yet the climate-denying Vox party capitalized on the aftermath by spreading misinformation. While Spain is praised for its investments in renewables, its record on climate adaptation is poor. A similar dynamic is at play in the UK, where persistent flooding in Wales has seen a rise in support for the climate-sceptic Reform UK party. The party's lead spokesperson for Wales has denied that the flooding is linked to climate breakdown, calling such claims a "red herring" and instead focusing on a failure to invest in flood defences.

The politics of the adaptation gap are far from what we might have hoped for. Climate breakdown is playing out worse than many expected, and the incoming prime minister, Andy Burnham, must act fast to get ahead of this dynamic. The consequences of inaction are stark, with the chances of a 40C heatwave happening in the UK before 2040 assessed at 0.02% in 2021. Yet, the following year, it happened. If the extreme temperatures we saw in 2022 worsen even slightly, we could be in for a far bigger shock, with wildfires burning larger areas, hospitals stressed beyond coping, and train collisions caused by heat-stressed signalling.

Or it could come from the domino effect of climate impacts on food. The area of high-quality farmland in England and Wales is projected to collapse by 75% in the next two decades without adaptation, while supply chains are disrupted by biodiversity collapse abroad, as a government security assessment recently warned. In response, many politicians will argue for doubling down on decarbonisation, and they will be right. The only way to become safe is for the world to eradicate fossil pollution. That means everyone – even countries contributing only 1% to global emissions, such as the UK.

However, some will argue that it's pointless talking about this intractable global challenge and we should focus only on protecting people suffering in the here and now. Yet, if this argument were to prevail, and resources were used solely for adaptation rather than decarbonisation as well, there will be less climate action, which means more climate shocks and more opportunities to exploit the resulting anger. That's a doom loop, known as "derailment risk": the threat that climate consequences undermine climate action in a self-defeating spiral, derailing the world from a route to avoiding the worst.

To stop derailment, the incoming government must get ahead of this dynamic. It starts by recognizing that the politics of the adaptation gap will only escalate. Closing it should become a priority, not the afterthought it has long been. This means retrofitting buildings for heat extremes, changing the law to set a maximum limit of temperature for safe working, and investing in regenerative farming to reduce exposure to climate-vulnerable global supply chains.

These are job-creating, economy-boosting investments that can also reduce the cost of living and are critical even for national security. In a competitive, climate-changed world, countries that are better adapted will have the edge. Climate breakdown is the key dimension often missing from growing demands to make investments beyond just defence that make Britain more resilient. Yet, the premier co-benefit of adaptation is that it can simultaneously decarbonise, making us safer today and into the future.

The UK must prioritize the things that intuitively protect, such as heat pumps, which are often framed as a decarbonisation measure but can also protect against extremes. Adaptation must seem like common sense, ensuring rightful demands for adaptation are not used as a foil to derail net zero. But for that to work, adaptation must be prioritized, and resources must be allocated accordingly. The incoming government must act fast to close the adaptation gap and prevent the deadly spiral of climate scepticism from derailing the country's progress towards a safer, more resilient future.

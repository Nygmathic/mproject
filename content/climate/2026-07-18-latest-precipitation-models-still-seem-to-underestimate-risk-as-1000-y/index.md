## The Shifting Ground Beneath Our Feet: Texas Floods Highlight Inadequate Precipitation Models

 Torrential downpours have ravaged parts of South Texas, leaving a trail of destruction and raising crucial questions about the accuracy of precipitation models. In just two years, the region has experienced not one but two major flooding disasters, with four-day rainfall totals in the towns of Uvalde and Sabinal setting records and ranking as 1,000-year rain events. These models, used to inform design criteria in local building codes, appear to be underestimating risk, according to experts.

Matthew Berg, principal scientist for the Houston-based risk management firm Simfero, points out that the specific area west of San Antonio has experienced some of the biggest increases in rainfall intensity compared to previous data. "You do start to wonder: is there something systematic?" he asks, hinting at a deeper issue with the current models. Berg's concerns are not unfounded, as the latest official dataset, published in 2018 by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), increased estimates of benchmark rainfall events by 30 to 40 percent in the region.

The recent floods have also highlighted the inadequacy of the current building codes, which require infrastructure and facilities to be designed for a 100-year rainstorm, a theoretical event with a one percent likelihood annually. However, as Berg notes, these models are based on outdated data and do not account for the changing climate. "Updated extreme rainfall values are generally higher," says John Nielsen-Gammon, Texas state climatologist and professor of atmospheric sciences at Texas A&M University. "This is expected from climate change."

Climate change is expected to lead to stronger downpours, as warm air holds more moisture. As the oceans and atmosphere continue to warm, forecasters anticipate further intensification of rainfall in the future. "Extreme precipitation is expected to increase in intensity on average statewide," states a 2024 assessment of weather trends from the office of the state climatologist. "Trends of extreme precipitation in the future will be dominated by the increasing temperature effect."

The steep increases in rainfall intensity assessed around Houston in NOAA's Atlas 14, released in 2018, resulted from the inclusion of several enormous storms, including Hurricane Harvey in 2017, the heaviest rainfall ever recorded in the U.S. Even before Harvey, researchers observed a trend toward higher rain totals in Houston, according to Matt Lanza, an operational meteorologist and co-founder of the website Space City Weather. "Something is clearly afoot," he says. "I can't entirely put my finger on what it is, but it may be a combination of things like a warming Gulf, urbanization, and aerosols."

The floods have also had a devastating impact on local communities, with the Nueces River breaking its all-time streamflow record, according to Greg Waller, an operational hydrologist with NOAA in Fort Worth. At its peak, the river had more than twice the flow of Niagara Falls, he says. The floods have blocked state highways, ripped asphalt off roads, and damaged train tracks, leaving residents to pick up the pieces.

As the region struggles to recover, experts are calling for a re-evaluation of the current precipitation models and building codes. "There is a more structural question if these big events keep happening," Berg says. "How far is it a statistical question, then how much is the ground shifting under our feet?" The answer to this question will have far-reaching implications for the future of climate-resilient infrastructure and the lives of those living in flood-prone areas.

## Reactions and Implications

As the dust settles, local officials and residents are grappling with the aftermath of the floods. John Byrum, executive director of the Nueces River Authority, acknowledges that "it's going to take some time for the city here, the residents of the city here to put their houses back in order." However, he remains optimistic about the community's ability to recover. "This is a strong community, and I'm sure they'll get through."

Meanwhile, experts are warning of the need for more robust precipitation models and building codes. "We need to start thinking about designing infrastructure for the new normal," says Berg. "We can't just rely on outdated data and hope that it's enough." The recent floods have highlighted the urgent need for climate-resilient infrastructure and more accurate precipitation models.

## Looking Ahead

As the world grapples with the impacts of climate change, the recent floods in Texas serve as a stark reminder of the need for more accurate precipitation models and climate-resilient infrastructure. The stakes are high, and the consequences of inaction will be severe. As Berg notes, "the ground is shifting under our feet," and it's time to adapt to the new reality. The question is, will we be able to keep up?

## The Air We Breathe: A Bronx Neighborhood's Enduring Struggle

New York City's pledge to tackle pollution and childhood asthma in the Bronx is a step in the right direction, but the community's struggle with poor air quality is far from over. Every summer, New Yorkers receive air quality warnings, signaling the arrival of wildfire smoke from Canada or the presence of ground-level ozone that forms when pollutants emitted by vehicles, power plants, or refineries react in the presence of sunlight. For residents of the Bronx, however, some neighborhoods experience poor air quality year-round, with lasting health consequences.

"It's the stuff that'll go into your lungs, into your body, into your blood for years," says Javier Marchand, an air quality advocate for the community advocacy group South Bronx Unite. "You don't taste it, you don't see it or anything, you just breathe it." This invisible threat is a harsh reality for the 19 percent of Bronx children who have been diagnosed with asthma at some point in their lives – a rate that far exceeds the 12 percent of children across the entire city.

The numbers are stark: in the Bronx, the annual rate of asthma hospitalizations for children younger than four is about 340 per 10,000, compared to the citywide rate of 200 per 10,000. This is not a coincidence. A history of redlining has led to a concentration of power plants and highways across the borough, making the South Bronx a common destination for diesel trucks that deliver food, packages, and waste to distribution centers and waste transfer stations. These trucks emit fine particulate matter and gases like nitrogen oxides, which can irritate a person's lower airways and cause chronic inflammation known as asthma.

Stephanie Lovinsky-Desir, a pediatrician who has spent much of her career studying how environmental and social factors affect respiratory illness in children, explains that repeated irritation can "remodel" the lungs, making them more sensitive and easier to trigger. The four natural gas-fired power plants in the neighborhood also emit pollutants that contribute to the formation of ground-level ozone, often creating smog that gives the horizon a hazy appearance.

Ozone pollution can even affect healthy people, causing coughing and a sore throat, according to the Environmental Protection Agency. But the worst effects are typically experienced by people with lung diseases such as asthma. The city's reliance on diesel-powered freight to transport goods and waste is a major contributor to the problem. While the Department of Transportation has proposed a plan to redesign truck routes and shift heavy vehicle traffic away from local streets, the plan would still add more truck routes to freight-heavy areas of the Bronx, including the South Bronx. The city's freight, nearly 90 percent of which is delivered by truck, places a disproportionate burden on places like the South Bronx because of the placement of last-mile warehouses and waste transfer stations.

Efforts have been made at the federal, state, and local levels to reduce truck pollution, but more needs to be done to address the systemic issues driving this problem. In the short term, the city's new funding for air quality initiatives is a welcome step, but it is only a starting point. ## A Legacy of Disinvestment

The Bronx's struggles with air quality are not new. A history of redlining has led to the concentration of power plants and highways across the borough, making it a hub for diesel truck traffic. This has resulted in the displacement of long-time residents and the degradation of the community's health.

The legacy of disinvestment in the Bronx is a complex issue that requires a multifaceted approach. The city's plans to redesign truck routes and increase the use of electric trucks are steps in the right direction, but they do not address the root causes of the problem. A more comprehensive solution would involve investing in community-led initiatives that prioritize the health and well-being of residents, such as green spaces, community gardens, and air-quality monitoring programs.

## A Silent Killer in the Heartland

Cancer rates in Iowa are soaring, with rural communities bearing the brunt of the devastating trend. New research from the Harkin Institute for Public Policy & Citizen Engagement and the Iowa Environmental Council reveals that environmental exposures, particularly to pesticides, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), fertilizer, and radon, are contributing significantly to the alarming spike in cancer diagnoses. As the nation's top corn-producing state, Iowa's agricultural prowess comes at a steep human cost, with the consequences being felt most acutely in the state's rural areas.

## The Cancer Conundrum

Cancer diagnoses in Iowa have risen by 13.4% between 2017 and 2020, with rural counties experiencing the sharpest increase. The analysis, which drew on data from the Iowa Cancer Registry and the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), found that exposure to environmental toxins was a significant factor in this trend. Pesticide use, in particular, has been linked to an increased risk of certain cancers, including leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma. The Midwest, where Iowa is located, is a major hub for agricultural production, with corn and soybean fields covering over 90% of the state's land area. This has led to the widespread use of pesticides, which, in turn, has contaminated soil, water, and air.

While the exact mechanisms by which these environmental exposures contribute to cancer development are complex and still not fully understood, research suggests that exposure to certain chemicals can damage DNA, leading to genetic mutations that can trigger cancer. The Iowa analysis also highlighted the role of PFAS, a class of synthetic chemicals used in non-stick cookware, food packaging, and other consumer products. PFAS contamination has been linked to cancer, reproductive issues, and other health problems, and Iowa's rural communities are particularly vulnerable due to the state's high use of PFAS in agricultural practices.

## The Fertile Ground of Cancer

Iowa's agricultural industry is worth billions of dollars, but the economic benefits come at a human cost. The state's intensive farming practices have led to widespread soil degradation, water pollution, and air quality issues. Fertilizer use, in particular, has been linked to increased cancer risk, as the chemicals used in these products can release carcinogenic particles into the air and water. Radon, a naturally occurring gas that can seep into homes, is also a significant concern in Iowa, where cancer rates from radon exposure are among the highest in the country.

The situation in Iowa is not unique, however. Similar trends have been observed in other agricultural states, including Nebraska, Kansas, and Minnesota. These regions are facing similar challenges, from the environmental impacts of intensive farming to the health consequences of living in areas with high levels of pollution. As the global population continues to grow, the pressure on agricultural systems will only intensify, making it imperative that policymakers, farmers, and public health officials work together to address these issues.

## A Call to Action

In response to the findings, Iowa Governor Kim Reynolds has announced plans to establish a task force to investigate the link between environmental exposures and cancer in the state. Environmental groups, such as the Iowa Environmental Council, are calling for stricter regulations on pesticide and fertilizer use, as well as increased funding for cancer research and prevention programs. "This report is a wake-up call for all of us," said Rebecca Jenkins, executive director of the Iowa Environmental Council. "We need to take action now to protect the health and well-being of Iowans, especially those living in rural areas."

The implications of this report are far-reaching, with potential consequences for agricultural policy, public health, and economic development. As the nation's top corn-producing state, Iowa's agricultural industry is a significant contributor to the US economy. However, the human cost of this industry cannot be ignored. With cancer rates on the rise and environmental exposures contributing significantly to this trend, it is time for policymakers to take action.

## A New Path Forward

As the nation grapples with the consequences of climate change, the Iowa analysis serves as a stark reminder of the human impact of environmental degradation. The connection between agricultural practices and cancer risk highlights the need for sustainable and environmentally conscious agricultural practices. Policymakers, farmers, and public health officials will need to work together to address these issues, investing in research and development of safer agricultural practices, improving air and water quality, and promoting public health programs aimed at reducing cancer risk.

The story in Iowa is not just about cancer rates or agricultural practices; it's about the human cost of a system that prioritizes economic growth over public health and environmental sustainability. As the nation moves forward, it is essential that we prioritize the health and well-being of all Iowans, particularly those living in rural areas. By doing so, we can create a more sustainable and equitable agricultural system that benefits both people and the planet.

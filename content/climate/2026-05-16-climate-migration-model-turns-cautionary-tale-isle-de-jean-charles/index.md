## A Relocation Without a Safety Net

Deep in the marshy wetlands of Louisiana, a community once thrived on the Isle de Jean Charles. For generations, the islanders built their homes, their livelihoods, and their lives amidst the rich biodiversity of this unique ecosystem. But as the waters rose and the land began to disappear, the islanders' way of life was slowly consumed by the encroaching tides. The year was 2016, and the residents of Isle de Jean Charles found themselves facing a stark reality: they were no longer safe on their ancestral home.

The islanders' story is often cited as a model for climate migration – a testament to the ingenuity and resilience of those forced to adapt to an increasingly hostile environment. However, as we speak with those who made the difficult decision to leave, a more nuanced narrative emerges. One that highlights the complexities and challenges of relocation, and the often-devastating consequences for those who are displaced.

## A Cautionary Tale

The Isle de Jean Charles relocation project was unprecedented in its scope and ambition. With funding from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, the islanders were offered a chance to start anew on higher ground, in the nearby town of Galliano. The promise was tantalizing: a chance to rebuild their lives, to preserve their culture, and to create a brighter future for their families. But as the islanders settled into their new homes, they began to realize that the grass was not as green as they had been led to believe.

The new homes were smaller, more cramped, and less affordable than their island counterparts. The community that had once thrived on the island – a tight-knit network of families and neighbors – was now scattered and fragmented. The islanders struggled to adapt to their new surroundings, to find employment, and to access basic services. The relocation, which had been touted as a success story, had left many feeling lost, disconnected, and disillusioned.

## A Historical Parallel

The story of Isle de Jean Charles is not unique. In fact, it echoes the experiences of countless communities around the world, who have been displaced by climate change, conflict, or economic development. The forced relocation of the Chagossians, a small island community in the Indian Ocean, is a particularly apt parallel. In the 1960s and 1970s, the British government forcibly relocated the Chagossians to make way for a U.S. military base. The trauma and suffering inflicted on the Chagossians serve as a stark reminder of the devastating consequences of displacement, and the importance of preserving the cultural heritage and traditional ways of life that are so often lost in the process.

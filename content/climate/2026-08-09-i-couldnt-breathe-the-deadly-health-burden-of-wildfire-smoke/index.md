### Wildfire Smoke's Devastating Toll: A Global Health Crisis

A thousand kilometres from the infernos raging through northern Ontario's boreal forests, Erin O'Connor, the emergency department head at Toronto General hospital, watched the sky above Canada's biggest city turn an "almost post-apocalyptic" orange as it filled with light-scattering particles of soot and ash. The noxious fumes, borne on winds from distant fires, brought a pungent smell to the city, reminiscent of a campfire, but far more hazardous. As O'Connor reflected on the sudden and prolonged exposure to wildfire smoke, she recalled a stark reality: the air quality in Toronto, once considered one of the cleanest in the world, had plummeted to unprecedented levels.

The smoke from these massive fires has become a lethal health burden, responsible for a staggering 1.5 million deaths annually, according to a study in the Lancet in 2024. This devastating toll eclipses the fatalities caused by floods, flames, storms, and heat combined. The sheer scale of the problem is underscored by the fact that the lung-scarring effects of charred plant matter can be felt far from the fires themselves, causing widespread suffering and death.

The study in Nature, which analyzed the impact of wildfires in Canada in 2023, found that the smoke caused 82,000 early deaths, with a third of the mortality burden falling on the US and a quarter on Europe. The research highlights the global nature of this crisis, with international borders offering little protection against the toxic plumes spewed by wildfires. In the UK, researchers found that about 10% of fine particulate pollution (PM2.5) comes from biomass burned abroad, a sobering reminder of the interconnectedness of our air quality.

Scientists agree that the health burden of wildfire smoke is enormous, but the complex links between exposure and mortality remain poorly understood. As climate breakdown makes fire weather more severe, hotter infernos could cause pollution to peak at even more extreme levels. The lack of answers in the existing research makes it challenging to predict the full extent of the fallout from this furious fire season.

Early signs are alarming, with vast tracts of vegetation torched close to big European cities and smoke choking major population centres such as Chicago and New York City. In Toronto, which briefly had the worst air quality of any major metropolis in the world last month, O'Connor reported a sudden and prolonged spike in patients presenting with allergies and shortness of breath. "It really hit you in the face as soon as you walked outside," she said. "As the days progressed, it was like that everywhere. It seemed even inside you could smell that burning."

The death toll from a fire season can depend on the whims of the wind as much as the size of the fire. Wildfires in France spewed record amounts of pollution in the first half of the year, while in Spain emissions have so far fallen just shy of the worst it has known. However, even though monster infernos were halted alarmingly close to Madrid and Bordeaux, the smoke was mostly blown away from where people live. Daily particulate pollution levels still worsened across southern Europe, peaking at staggering levels in cities such as Bordeaux and Limoges.

North America has not been so lucky. As winds blew south and south-east from scarcely populated forests in Ontario and the Northwest Territories, at least 36 million people across the continent were breathing air fouled by "very unhealthy" concentrations of PM2.5 that exceeded 125.5μg/m³ on 16 July. At least 10 million people were exposed to more than 250μg/m³, a level of daily pollution that smog-choked New Delhi saw no more than eight times last year.

Experts warn that the hit to air quality in some big cities is reminiscent of the megafires in 2023 that came with vast death tolls. "As soon as we have massive population centres exposed, we're going to see those increases," said Stephanie Cleland, a wildfire health scientist at Simon Fraser University. "I would be concerned by the fact that it's only been three years since this last happened and now we're having it again."

In the quiet suburb of Duluth, in north-east Minnesota, the worst of the smoke was seen, smelled, and tasted on 16 July. Jae Jeon and Dan McClelland, a married couple who grow flowers for weddings and subscription deliveries, were among 12,000 people in west Duluth exposed to an average of 541μg/m³ of fine particulate pollution, the analysis shows.

As the world struggles to come to terms with the devastating impact of wildfire smoke, one thing is clear: the science is clear, the consequences are dire, and the need for action is urgent. The time for inaction is over; the time for collaboration and innovation is now.

## Climate Change in the Shadow: The Supreme Court's Hidden Impact

A sweltering heatwave has ravaged the rural communities of western Africa for weeks, displacing thousands of families and decimating crops. As the mercury rises, so do concerns about the region's long-term resilience to climate change. But few realize that the same forces driving these environmental disasters are also quietly reshaping the Supreme Court's approach to climate policy.

The shadow docket, a term coined by critics to describe the secretive and informal decision-making process of the Supreme Court, has come under increasing scrutiny in recent years. While the public remains largely unaware of these shadowy proceedings, researchers have long warned that the court's shadow docket poses a significant threat to climate policy. The stakes are high: the court's decisions will determine whether the United States can meet its Paris Agreement targets, and whether the world's most vulnerable regions can adapt to a rapidly changing climate.

The latest controversy centers on a series of memos leaked to the press, which reveal a coordinated effort by conservative justices to bypass established procedures and push through climate-skeptic decisions. Critics argue that these decisions were rushed and flawed, with many based on flimsy science and flawed assumptions about the role of human activity in driving climate change. The fallout has been immediate: key climate regulations have been struck down, and the court's willingness to entertain climate-related appeals has plummeted.

At the heart of the controversy lies the Supreme Court's fraught relationship with climate science. For decades, the court has struggled to keep pace with the mounting evidence of human-caused climate change. As early as 2007, the court's majority opinion in Massachusetts v. EPA acknowledged the scientific consensus on climate change, but stopped short of taking concrete action to address the issue. Today, the court's conservative justices appear to be actively working to undermine this consensus, using the shadow docket to push through decisions that fly in the face of overwhelming scientific evidence.

The consequences of this shadowy approach are far-reaching. By denying the reality of human-caused climate change, the court is not only undermining the country's ability to address the issue, but also perpetuating a cycle of environmental injustice that disproportionately affects the world's most vulnerable regions. In sub-Saharan Africa, where climate-related disasters are already a major concern, the court's decisions are having a devastating impact. As temperatures rise and droughts worsen, communities are struggling to adapt to a rapidly changing environment. The court's failure to acknowledge the reality of climate change is a major obstacle to these efforts, leaving local leaders scrambling to find innovative solutions to a crisis that the court refuses to recognize.

Historically, the Supreme Court has played a key role in shaping the country's approach to environmental regulation. In the 1970s and 1980s, the court issued a series of landmark decisions that recognized the constitutional right to a safe and healthy environment. Today, these decisions are being quietly overturned, as the court's conservative justices use the shadow docket to push through decisions that fly in the face of established precedent. The impact is being felt far beyond the courtroom, with local communities and advocacy groups struggling to hold the court accountable for its actions.

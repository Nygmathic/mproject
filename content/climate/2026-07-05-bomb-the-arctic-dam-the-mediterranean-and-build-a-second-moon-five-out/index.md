## A Legacy of Unfulfilled Ambitions: Geoengineering's Forgotten Past

Humans have consistently sought to manipulate and control the Earth's environment, driven by a desire to reshape the world according to our whims. This impulse has led to some of the most fantastical and far-fetched ideas in the history of human innovation. Five outlandish plans to remodel our climate, conceived by visionaries in the 20th century, stand as testaments to our species' boundless ambition and, ultimately, our ingenuity's limitations. As we grapple with the existential threat of climate change, it is essential to examine these forgotten projects and draw lessons from their failures.

At the heart of the 20th-century geoengineering movement was the notion that humanity could somehow "fix" the climate through technological interventions. Cloud brightening, injecting sulphur into the atmosphere, and deploying tiny mirrors in space were among the concepts touted by entrepreneurs and governments. The idea was that by manipulating the Earth's solar radiation, we could mitigate the devastating effects of climate change. However, this approach raises profound questions about our relationship with the environment and our capacity to control it.

One of the most striking examples of geoengineering's earlier incarnations is Herman Sörgel's Atlantropa plan, proposed in the 1930s. This audacious scheme envisioned a massive dam across the Strait of Gibraltar, which would lower the Mediterranean Sea by 200 meters, creating vast tracts of fertile land and unleashing a new era of prosperity. The plan, which was enthusiastically supported by leading engineers, would have required enormous resources and would have had a profound impact on the region's ecology. While some critics raised concerns about the potential effects on Venice's canals, Sörgel assured them that "special measures" would be taken to mitigate this issue.

The Atlantropa plan's failure to materialize serves as a cautionary tale about the hubris of human innovation. Despite its grand ambitions, the project was ultimately doomed by its impracticality and the devastating consequences of its implementation. This episode highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of our relationship with the environment and the limitations of our technological prowess.

In the Soviet Union, the notion of geoengineering was closely tied to the concept of the "great Stalin plan for the transformation of nature," which aimed to render the environment more productive and hospitable. One of the most notable proponents of this idea was the engineer PM Borisov, who proposed building a dam across the Bering Strait to raise the temperature of the Earth by a few degrees. This scheme, which was motivated by the Soviet Union's perceived lack of heat, would have required enormous resources and would have had a profound impact on the global climate.

The Soviet Union's foray into geoengineering is a fascinating example of the complex interplay between technological innovation and environmental politics. The fact that Borisov's plan was taken seriously by Soviet scientists and policymakers underscores the degree to which geoengineering was seen as a viable solution to the country's environmental challenges. However, the eventual abandonment of the project serves as a reminder that even the most fanciful ideas can have devastating consequences when pursued without adequate consideration of their environmental impact.

In the 1990s, the Soviet Union's successor state, Russia, embarked on an ambitious geoengineering project known as Project Znamya. This initiative aimed to create a "second moon" by deploying a fleet of reflective foldable satellites that would reflect sunlight onto Russia's arctic regions, increasing the illumination and providing additional warmth and energy savings. While the initial batch of satellites was successful in creating a 5km patch of light, the project was eventually abandoned due to technical and economic challenges.

The failure of Project Znamya serves as a poignant reminder of the difficulties involved in large-scale geoengineering projects. Despite its ambitious goals, the project was ultimately hampered by its reliance on expensive and technologically complex solutions. This episode highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the environmental and economic implications of geoengineering and the importance of prioritizing sustainability and feasibility in our technological pursuits.

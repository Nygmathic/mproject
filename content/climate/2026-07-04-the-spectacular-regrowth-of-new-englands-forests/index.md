## The Resilience of Nature: New England's Forests Regrow

In the rugged expanse of New England, a remarkable transformation has taken place. The region's once-thriving forests, ravaged by European colonizers in the 18th and 19th centuries, have undergone a spectacular regrowth. According to Bill Moomaw, a distinguished visiting scientist at the Woodwell Climate Research Center, this regrowth is the greatest forest recovery in the history of the world.

Moomaw, a Tufts emeritus professor and lead author of several Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change reports, has spent decades studying the intricate dance between human activity and the natural world. As we sat down with him to discuss the history of New England's forests, it became clear that the story of their regrowth is inextricably linked to the region's complex past.

"We have been sent to a wilderness filled with savages and wild beasts," John Winthrop, one of the earliest European settlers, once wrote of the New England landscape. The colonizers' notion that the forests were an obstacle to be overcome, rather than a resource to be cherished, led to widespread clear-cutting. By 1850, New England was a mere 10-20% forested, with some areas bearing as little as 2% tree cover.

The impact on the environment was catastrophic. The forests, once a vital carbon sink, were reduced to mere shadows of their former selves. The consequences of this destruction continue to reverberate today, as the world grapples with the effects of climate change.

However, as Moomaw notes, the story of New England's forests is not one of unmitigated disaster. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, a new era of forest management began to take hold. Gifford Pinchot, a pioneer of sustainable forestry, returned from Europe with a vision of managing forests as a resource for future generations, rather than a commodity to be exploited.

The shift towards more sustainable practices was slow, but it marked a turning point in the history of New England's forests. As agriculture gave way to industry, and the railroads expanded across the region, the need for wood as fuel and timber became increasingly pressing. However, instead of clear-cutting, many of these areas were abandoned as farmers sought out more fertile lands in the Ohio territories.

The result was a spontaneous regrowth of forests, driven by the benign neglect of human activity. In Massachusetts, for example, which was once 90% deforested, the forest cover has rebounded to around 60% today. This is not simply a testament to the resilience of nature, but also a powerful reminder of the importance of allowing ecosystems to recover and adapt.

Today, as Moomaw notes, the battle to protect New England's forests is far from over. Many states continue to view forests as a resource to be exploited, rather than a treasure to be cherished. However, the story of their regrowth serves as a powerful reminder of the potential for nature to heal and recover, even in the face of human neglect and exploitation.

As the world grapples with the challenges of climate change, the story of New England's forests offers a glimmer of hope. It is a testament to the power of nature to adapt and recover, and a reminder of the importance of preserving our forests for future generations. As Moomaw so eloquently puts it, "We can celebrate the fact that our forests are mostly back, but we must continue to fight for their protection."

## The Role of Human Activity in Forest Regrowth

The story of New England's forests is a complex one, inextricably linked to the region's history of human activity. From the earliest European settlers to the modern-day Department of Natural Resources, the role of humans in shaping the forest landscape has been profound. However, it is not just the actions of humans that have driven the regrowth of forests, but also their inaction.

As Moomaw notes, the abandonment of agricultural lands in the late 19th and early 20th centuries led to a spontaneous regrowth of forests. This was not a deliberate attempt to restore the forest ecosystem, but rather a result of the decline of agriculture and the expansion of industry.

The shift towards more sustainable forestry practices, driven by figures like Gifford Pinchot, has also played a crucial role in the regrowth of forests. By recognizing the value of forests as a resource for future generations, rather than a commodity to be exploited, Pinchot and his contemporaries helped to establish a new era of forest management.

However, the struggle to protect New England's forests continues to this day. As Moomaw notes, many states continue to view forests as a resource to be exploited, rather than a treasure to be cherished. This is a battle that has been raging for centuries, with figures like John Muir advocating for the preservation of forests and their ecosystems.

## The New Face of Climate Denial: Embracing a Mediterranean Nightmare

Climate disaster has long been a pressing concern, with scientists warning of catastrophic consequences if global temperatures continue to rise. However, in a disturbing shift, some segments of the radical right have begun to argue that embracing a Mediterranean-style climate, with its associated heatwaves, droughts, and decimated landscapes, is not only inevitable but also desirable.

The most egregious example of this new brand of climate nihilism comes from Allister Heath, the editor of the Sunday Telegraph, who recently penned a column extolling the virtues of a warmer, drier climate. Heath's argument is built on a foundation of flawed logic, dismissing the urgency of climate action as a "moral act, an ethical imperative" that has gone too far. In his view, cutting carbon emissions in the UK is a "suicidal empathy" that will only lead to "losers" adapting to the new reality.

Heath's column coincided with the declaration of drought across half of England, a stark reminder of the devastating consequences of climate change. Yet, he remained resolute in his denial, advocating for universal irrigation and the construction of more swimming pools, despite the obvious question of where the water will come from. His trust in the "free market" to deliver clean energy and zero-carbon transportation is similarly misplaced, given the evidence that renewables are already cheaper and more effective than fossil fuels in nearly all circumstances.

The radical right's newfound enthusiasm for a Mediterranean climate is not only a rejection of scientific consensus but also a reflection of its ideological alignment with corporate interests. The wealthy elite, heavily invested in fossil fuels, stand to lose the most from a transition to clean energy. As a result, they are using every tool at their disposal to delay and discredit climate action, often by promoting disinformation and nihilistic rhetoric.

The Telegraph's own columnists provide a interesting contrast to Heath's views. Ambrose Evans-Pritchard and Tim Stanley, two prominent columnists, recently broke ranks with the nihilist consensus, arguing that humanity must rise to the challenge of climate breakdown. However, their voices are now a minority within the paper, underscoring the extent to which the media has become complicit in serving corporate interests.

The consequences of this climate denial are far-reaching and devastating. Rising temperatures, droughts, and heatwaves will have a disproportionate impact on vulnerable communities, exacerbating existing social and economic inequalities. The radical right's embrace of a Mediterranean-style climate is a recipe for disaster, one that will leave entire regions decimated and vulnerable to the whims of the market.

As the world struggles to come to terms with the reality of climate change, it is more important than ever that we prioritize science over ideology and human well-being over corporate profits. The radical right's new brand of climate nihilism is a clear and present danger, one that demands a robust response from policymakers, scientists, and civil society. By standing firm against the tide of climate denial, we can ensure a more sustainable, equitable future for all.

## The Politics of Climate Denial

The radical right's embrace of a Mediterranean-style climate is not a sudden development but rather the culmination of a long-standing strategy to delay and discredit climate action. By promoting disinformation and nihilistic rhetoric, they aim to create a sense of inevitability around climate change, one that will justify inaction and maintain the status quo.

This approach is not unique to the radical right, however. The media, too, has played a significant role in promoting climate denial, often by providing a platform for individuals who say things that serve corporate interests, regardless of their truth or coherence. The result is a distorted public discourse, one that prioritizes the interests of the wealthy elite over those of the most vulnerable.

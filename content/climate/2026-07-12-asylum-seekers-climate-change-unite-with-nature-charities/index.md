## Planting Roots: A New Era of Collaboration in the UK Countryside

Shielding his eyes from the blinding midday sun, Abdullah gazes out at the expanse of green in Tŷ Mawr country park in north Wales. "This place is so beautiful," he says. "It feels a very long way from the Home Office." The park, situated on the banks of the River Dee in the Vale of Llangollen, is an area of outstanding natural beauty. Sheep, pigs, goats, and chickens speckle the stunning landscape.

As part of a group of dozens of asylum seekers, refugees, and environmental volunteers working together to clear invasive Himalayan balsam from the riverbanks, Abdullah is part of a pioneering initiative that brings together two often disparate worlds: refugee support and environmental conservation. The project, led by the national charity Action Asylum, has developed a model that not only benefits the environment but also fosters community cohesion and provides a sense of belonging for those seeking asylum.

Since 2020, Action Asylum has been working with local residents and people seeking asylum through environmental volunteering. The charity has recently secured a £1.62 million funding boost from the National Lottery's climate action fund, which will enable it to roll out new environmental programmes and expand into areas such as Sheffield, Newcastle, and Bolton. Over the next three years, Action Asylum will work with more than 3,000 volunteers and run 120 large-scale climate action events across England and Wales.

For Emma Leaper, the project director at Action Asylum, the funding is a game-changer. "This means we can do what we know works, bringing people together in nature, on a scale we've never been able to before." She highlights the transformative impact of environmental volunteering on individuals seeking asylum. "Working alongside others in nature can transform someone's confidence and sense of self," she says.

The partnership between Action Asylum and the North Wales Wildlife Trust is a prime example of this approach. Gemma Rose, a wildlife trust representative, notes that many asylum seekers spend most of their time in hotels, away from natural environments that can be beneficial for their mental and physical well-being. "It's great for them to come and help out at a place like this," she says. "I feel lucky to be involved with this project. It gives you a much better view of the world."

For Abdullah, planting food in a foreign land is a bittersweet experience that reminds him of his homeland in Sudan. "I lived in a village in Darfur," he says. "I loved the life in my village and used to plant things like onions and garlic there. My mother grew mangoes and lemons and chillies, which she sold in the market. But the life was difficult because of the war and everybody ran to different countries."

As Abdullah and his fellow volunteers work together to clear the Himalayan balsam, they are not just removing invasive species; they are also planting a sense of belonging and purpose. For Jane, a refugee and one of the initiative's lead ambassadors, this is the heart of the project. "When you plant a tree, it does not matter who you are or where you came from," she says. "Your hands are in the soil, and you belong to the planet as a human being. We don't just plant trees. We plant belonging, and we plant purpose."

## A New Model for Refugee Support and Environmental Conservation

Action Asylum's innovative approach to refugee support and environmental conservation has the potential to inspire a new wave of collaboration between often disparate sectors. By bringing people together in nature, the project addresses the psychological and emotional needs of those seeking asylum while also contributing to the restoration of the environment.

The initiative's success is not limited to its environmental impact. By fostering community cohesion and providing a sense of belonging, Action Asylum is helping to break down the barriers that often prevent asylum seekers from integrating into their new communities. As Emma Leaper notes, "We've seen firsthand how working alongside others in nature can transform someone's confidence and sense of self."

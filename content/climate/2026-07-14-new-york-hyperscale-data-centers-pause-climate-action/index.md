## New York Takes the Lead: A Nation-Wide Pause on Hyperscale Data Centers

In a groundbreaking move, New York Governor Kathy Hochul has signed an executive order, making the state the first in the nation to pause new hyperscale data centers. This moratorium, which will remain in place for up to one year, marks a significant step towards mitigating the environmental impacts of these facilities, which have sparked concerns over their potential energy and water consumption.

The four existing hyperscale data centers in New York, along with 39 pending applications, have raised eyebrows among environmentalists and policymakers. These massive facilities have been criticized for their voracious energy appetite, with some estimates suggesting that a single data center can consume as much electricity as a small town. As the world grapples with the urgent need to conserve energy and reduce fossil fuel emissions, the proliferation of hyperscale data centers poses a significant challenge.

According to Eric Wood, senior environmental program coordinator at the New York Public Interest Research Group, the state Legislature's broader moratorium approved in June was a more comprehensive measure, but the governor's executive order is still a crucial step forward. "The state Legislature's bill is much more protective of the New York state individual, while the governor's proposal seems to be a halfway between the billion-dollar tech industry and her constituents," Wood said.

The Environmental Defense Fund's New York state director, Kate Boicourt, echoed Wood's sentiments, emphasizing the need for standardized rules across the state to address the concerns surrounding hyperscale data centers. "There's things that need to be statewide standards," Boicourt said. "Otherwise, if you're having specific municipality-by-municipality rules, you are potentially shifting what's allowed in one area and not being allowed in the other."

However, not everyone is pleased with the moratorium. Some business and trade groups have decried the move as bad for business, citing the potential loss of good-paying union jobs. Mark McManus, general president of the United Association, a labor union for plumbers and pipefitters, argued that the moratorium would "kill good-paying union jobs."

Despite the backlash, many experts believe that New York's decision could set a national precedent. Darrell West, a Brookings Institution senior fellow who researches AI and technology policy, noted that the state is a "trendsetter" and its actions often influence other areas. "What happens there often spreads to other areas. So the fact that New York has adopted a one-year moratorium suggests there may be others that follow, and that's a very big deal for the future of data centers and AI in general."

The public opposition to hyperscale data centers is widespread, with a recent Gallup poll showing that nearly three-fourths of Americans oppose construction in their localities, including over 60 percent of Republicans. Mitch Jones, managing director of policy and litigation for the national advocacy group Food & Water Watch, welcomed the moratorium, saying it would give communities a chance to better understand the dangers posed by data center buildout. "We're just glad that New Yorkers are going to have a pause to the building of hyperscale data centers in the state, so that communities can better educate themselves about the dangers posed by the data center buildout, and continue to then educate their elected officials about why permanent protections need to be put in place."

As the debate around hyperscale data centers continues, regulatory agencies in New York will now need to delve into the details of long-term data center policy. Cole Jermyn, an energy transition attorney with the Environmental Defense Fund, emphasized the importance of getting the details right. "This is something where details really matter, and executive orders aren't usually the place where you're trying to do detailed utility regulation."

The next step for New York is to establish a regulatory framework that balances the needs of the tech industry with those of the environment and local communities. As the state moves forward, it will be crucial to involve all stakeholders in the process, including policymakers, industry leaders, and community representatives. By working together, New York can set a national example and pave the way for a more sustainable and equitable future.

For now, the pause on hyperscale data centers in New York marks a significant victory for environmentalists and a crucial step towards mitigating the impacts of these facilities. As the world watches, it remains to be seen whether other states will follow suit and how the tech industry will adapt to the changing landscape. One thing is certain, however: the conversation around hyperscale data centers is far from over, and New York's leadership will continue to play a decisive role in shaping the future of this rapidly evolving industry.

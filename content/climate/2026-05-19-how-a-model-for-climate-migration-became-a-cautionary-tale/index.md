## A Community Upended

The small island of Isle de Jean Charles, Louisiana, was once home to a thriving community of Native Americans, fishermen, and farmers. But as the waters rose and the land eroded, the residents were forced to make a heart-wrenching decision: abandon their ancestral home and relocate to higher ground. The $48 million project, hailed as a model for climate migration, aimed to preserve the community's cultural heritage and provide a safe haven for its displaced members. Yet, as the islanders settled into their new surroundings, a disturbing trend emerged – many were struggling to adapt, and some were warning others facing similar relocations: "Don't do it."

The stakes of climate migration are high. Rising sea levels, intensifying storms, and unpredictable weather patterns are displacing communities worldwide, from the Maldives to Mozambique, and from the Ganges Delta to the Gulf Coast of the United States. The Isle de Jean Charles relocation serves as a cautionary tale, highlighting the complexities and challenges of climate migration. While the project's architects touted it as a success, the islanders' experiences paint a more nuanced picture, one that underscores the difficulties of preserving cultural heritage and community identity in the face of catastrophic change.

## A Fragile Experiment

Isle de Jean Charles, once a proud and self-sufficient community, was home to around 80 residents, including the Biloxi-Chitimacha-Choctaw tribe. The island, situated in the heart of the Atchafalaya Basin, was vulnerable to rising sea levels and subsidence, a phenomenon exacerbated by the loss of natural wetlands and the impact of human activities. As the waters encroached, the residents were offered a chance to relocate to a 10-acre plot of land in Gibson, Louisiana, approximately 40 miles inland. The $48 million project, funded by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, aimed to preserve the community's cultural heritage and provide a safe haven for its displaced members.

The relocation effort was led by the Isle de Jean Charles Band of Biloxi-Chitimacha-Choctaw Indians, in partnership with the state of Louisiana and various NGOs. The project's architects envisioned a thriving community, with new homes, a community center, and a range of economic opportunities. However, the reality has been far more challenging. Many residents have struggled to adapt to their new surroundings, feeling disconnected from their ancestral lands and community networks. The loss of their traditional way of life has taken a toll on their mental and physical health, as well as their economic well-being.

The Isle de Jean Charles relocation raises fundamental questions about the nature of community and cultural identity. As the residents adapted to their new surroundings, they began to realize that their cultural heritage was being eroded, piece by piece. The traditional hunting and fishing practices that sustained their community for generations were no longer viable, and the loss of their ancestral lands has disrupted their spiritual connections to the land. The community's language, customs, and traditions, which once defined their identity, are now under threat.

The Isle de Jean Charles experience is not an isolated incident. Climate migration is a global phenomenon, affecting communities in every region of the world. In the Maldives, for example, rising sea levels are threatening the very existence of this island nation, forcing residents to contemplate relocation to higher ground. Similarly, in Bangladesh, the Ganges Delta is experiencing some of the most rapid and extensive coastal erosion in the world, displacing communities and forcing them to seek refuge elsewhere.

## The Unfinished Business of Africa's Football Giants

As the final whistle blew on the Africa Cup of Nations, a sense of disillusionment hung in the air. For many, the tournament was a missed opportunity - a chance for the continent's giants to flex their muscles, to showcase the progress they've made in recent years, and to challenge the status quo. Instead, the likes of Nigeria, Egypt, and Morocco were left to ponder what could have been, their early exits a stark reminder of the chasm between Africa's top teams and the rest of the world.

The stakes are high, and the scrutiny is intense. The AFCON is more than just a tournament; it's a benchmark of a nation's footballing prowess, a reflection of its investment in the sport, and a test of its ability to produce top-class talent. When teams like Nigeria and Egypt fail to deliver, it's not just a disappointment to their fans; it's a blow to the entire continent, a reminder that there's still a long way to go before African football can compete with the best.

The reasons for this disparity are complex, and multifaceted. From underfunded youth development programs to outdated coaching methods, and from corrupt governance to inadequate infrastructure, the obstacles facing African football are numerous. But they're not insurmountable. In fact, there are signs that the tide is beginning to turn.

Take, for example, the growth of the Premier League's interest in African talent. Clubs like Manchester City and Liverpool are actively scouting the continent, recognizing the potential of players like Sadio Mané and Mohamed Salah. This increased investment is a vote of confidence in African football, a recognition that the continent is producing world-class talent, and that it's time to invest in its development.

But this growth also raises questions about the sustainability of African football's progress. As more and more players leave the continent to ply their trade in Europe, there's a risk that the talent pool will dry up, leaving behind a void that's difficult to fill. This is a challenge that's already being felt in countries like Ghana, where the exodus of top players has left a gaping hole in the national team.

## The Search for a New Model

One possible solution is to create a more sustainable model of player development, one that prioritizes homegrown talent and invests in the grassroots. This is a model that's already being tested in countries like South Africa, where the development of players like Percy Tau and Teboho Mokoena is a testament to the success of the country's youth development programs.

But creating a new model won't be easy. It'll require a fundamental shift in the way that football is approached on the continent, a recognition that the traditional methods of player development are no longer effective. It'll also require investment, not just in terms of money, but in terms of time and resources.

One person who's been at the forefront of this effort is Jacques Anouma, the president of the CAF's technical committee. Anouma is a man with a vision, a man who believes that African football can be transformed through a combination of grassroots development, investment in coaching, and a commitment to creating a more sustainable model of player development.

For Anouma, the key is to create a more inclusive, more participatory approach to football development. This means involving more communities, more stakeholders, and more players in the decision-making process. It means creating a more level playing field, one where every country has an equal opportunity to develop its talent.

But Anouma's vision is not without its critics. Some argue that it's too ambitious, that it's unrealistic to expect African football to compete with the best in the world. Others argue that it's too focused on individual development, that it neglects the broader structural issues that are holding back the continent's progress.

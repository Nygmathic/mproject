## Lagos, a City on the Brink: Government's Swift Action Amidst Floods and Waste

Lagos, the sprawling metropolis, stood crippled by the deluge of the past few weeks, with waters overflowing from the city's drains, streets, and canals. Thousands of residents were forced to seek refuge in temporary shelters, their homes and livelihoods imperilled as the floodwaters crept higher. Amidst this chaos, the Lagos state government has taken a decisive step to rectify the situation, vowing to create a flood-free environment and ordering the swift evacuation of waste from the city's waterways.

At the heart of this endeavour lies the state's determination to restore order to the city's drains, which have been clogged by years of neglect and inadequate maintenance. The government has launched a massive operation to clear the drains simultaneously, deploying an army of workers to dredge the channels and remove the accumulated waste. As the rains continue to intensify, the government's resolve is clear: the city must be protected against the ravages of flooding. A clean environment, the officials argue, is the most effective line of defence against the deluge.

The stakes are high, for Lagos is one of Africa's most populous cities, with a projected population of over 25 million by 2030. The consequences of inaction would be catastrophic, with entire neighbourhoods submerged under water, infrastructure destroyed, and the livelihoods of countless residents imperilled. Yet, the government's swift action is a testament to its commitment to safeguarding the city and its people. "We are clearing the drains simultaneously because this is the only sure path to securing our city against flooding as the rains continue to intensify," said a government official. "A clean environment is our first and most effective line of defence."

But the Lagos government's efforts are not merely a response to the immediate crisis; they are also part of a broader vision to transform the city's infrastructure and make it more resilient to the impacts of climate change. The city's drainage system, built largely in the 1970s, is woefully inadequate for the demands of a modern megacity. The government's push to upgrade the system is therefore a welcome development, one that promises to bring much-needed relief to residents and businesses alike.

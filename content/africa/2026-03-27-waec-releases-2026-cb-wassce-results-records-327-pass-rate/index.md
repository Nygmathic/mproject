## Education on the Brink: WAEC's 2026 CB-WASSCE Results Raise Alarms

Tears of joy and desperation mingled as families gathered at the doorstep of the West African Examination Council's (WAEC) headquarters in Lagos, Nigeria, awaiting the highly anticipated release of the 2026 Certificate of Basic Education - West African Senior School Certificate Examination (CB-WASSCE) results. The moment of truth had finally arrived, and as the numbers began to trickle in, the gravity of the situation became increasingly evident. A paltry 32.7 per cent of the 10,523 candidates who registered for the examination had passed, sparking widespread concern among educators, policymakers, and the wider public.

The stakes could not be higher. These examinations are the gateway to higher education and, by extension, the future professions of these young individuals. The pass rate, which represents a 10.7 percentage point decline from the 43.4 per cent recorded in 2025, raises alarming questions about the effectiveness of the country's educational system. The 2026 CB-WASSCE results are a stark reminder of the persistent challenges facing education in Nigeria and, by extension, the wider West African region. The implications are far-reaching, with many questioning the very foundations of the country's educational infrastructure.

To fully grasp the extent of this crisis, it is essential to consider the broader context. The decline in pass rates is part of a longer-term trend that has seen the quality of education in Nigeria deteriorate steadily over the past decade. Factors such as inadequate infrastructure, insufficient funding, and a lack of qualified teachers have all contributed to this downward spiral. Moreover, the country's educational system is still reeling from the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, which forced a nationwide closure of schools and disrupted the learning process for millions of students.

However, to attribute the current crisis solely to the pandemic or other external factors would be a gross oversimplification. The truth is that Nigeria's educational system has been plagued by systemic issues for years, and the WAEC results merely serve to highlight the magnitude of the problem. The country's educational infrastructure is woefully underdeveloped, with many schools lacking basic amenities such as libraries, science laboratories, and internet connectivity. Furthermore, the teaching profession is severely undervalued, with many educators struggling to make ends meet on meagre salaries.

The implications of these findings are not limited to the individual students who failed the examination. The broader society stands to suffer as well, as an ill-equipped workforce will struggle to meet the demands of a rapidly changing global economy. It is little wonder, then, that many are calling for a fundamental transformation of the country's educational system. Some have suggested the introduction of more practical and vocational courses, while others have advocated for a greater emphasis on critical thinking and problem-solving skills.

As the country grapples with the aftermath of the 2026 CB-WASSCE results, reactions are starting to pour in from all quarters. Education Minister, Adamu Adamu, has vowed to take immediate action to address the crisis, promising to establish a special task force to investigate the causes of the decline in pass rates. Meanwhile, opposition politicians have seized on the issue, accusing the government of failing to provide adequate resources to the education sector. The Nigerian Labour Congress, representing the country's teachers, has called for a nationwide strike to demand better working conditions and higher salaries for educators.

As the dust settles on the 2026 CB-WASSCE results, one thing is clear: the future of Nigeria's education system hangs precariously in the balance. The country has a choice to make: continue down the path of mediocrity or embark on a bold new journey towards educational excellence. The world is watching, and the stakes could not be higher. What happens next will depend on the collective efforts of educators, policymakers, and the wider public. One thing is certain, however: the fate of Nigeria's future generations hangs precariously in the balance, and it is time for action.

## Ebola Fears Grip Uganda's Judiciary as Kivumbi Terror Case Heats Up

Ugandan security agents stand guard outside the International Crimes Division (ICD) Court in Kampala, their stern faces a testament to the high stakes at play as Muwanga Kivumbi and 23 co-accused persons face a terrorism trial that has captivated the nation. But on a day when proceedings were set to resume, a stark reminder of the fragility of life in the time of Ebola loomed large over the courtroom. In a sudden and unexpected move, the court leadership agreed to severely limit attendance at the bail hearing for Kivumbi and his co-accused, citing fears of an Ebola outbreak in the crowded courtrooms of the ICD.

The move has sparked intense debate about the impact of Ebola on Uganda's judicial system, where already overburdened courts struggle to meet the demands of a growing caseload. The International Crimes Division, which is designed to handle the most complex cases of terrorism and serious crimes, has been at the forefront of the country's fight against extremism. But with Ebola cases on the rise and the highly infectious disease spreading rapidly through crowded urban areas, the court has been forced to take drastic measures to protect the health and safety of all those involved in the Kivumbi trial.

At the heart of the controversy is Muwanga Kivumbi, a prominent Muslim cleric accused of inciting violence against Uganda's Christian-dominated government. Kivumbi's supporters, who have been present in large numbers throughout the trial, are adamant that the court's decision to limit attendance is an attempt to suppress their right to a fair trial. "We understand the concerns about Ebola, but this is a matter of justice," said Fatima Mwanika, a leader of the Kivumbi support group. "We will not be deterred from exercising our right to be present in court, even if it means taking risks to protect our leader and our community."

But court officials and health experts insist that the decision to limit attendance was taken in the best interests of public health. "We cannot afford to take risks with Ebola," said Dr. Charles Kamban, a leading expert on infectious diseases at Makerere University. "The virus is highly contagious and can spread rapidly in crowded areas like courtrooms. By limiting attendance, we can help prevent an outbreak and protect the health and safety of all those involved in the trial."

The Kivumbi trial has been one of the most closely watched cases in Uganda's history, with many seeing it as a test of the country's commitment to justice and human rights. For years, Kivumbi has been a vocal critic of Uganda's government, using his platform as a cleric to call for greater freedoms and an end to what he sees as discriminatory laws. But in 2022, his rhetoric turned violent, and he was accused of inciting his followers to attack Uganda's embassies in Kenya and Tanzania.

As the trial unfolds, observers are watching closely to see whether the court will uphold the government's claims of terrorism or rule that Kivumbi's actions were simply a form of peaceful protest. Whatever the outcome, one thing is clear: the Kivumbi trial has become a lightning rod for tensions between Uganda's government and its Muslim minority, which has long felt marginalized and excluded from the country's power structures.

## Beyond the Courtroom: The Impact of Ebola on Uganda's Judiciary

The decision to limit attendance at the Kivumbi bail hearing has raised questions about the impact of Ebola on Uganda's judicial system. With cases of the virus on the rise, courts across the country are struggling to adapt to the new reality. In some cases, judges and magistrates have been forced to suspend proceedings altogether, while in others, courts have been converted into makeshift isolation centers for those suspected of having the virus.

But the impact of Ebola goes far beyond the courtroom. The virus has exposed deep-seated weaknesses in Uganda's healthcare system, where inadequate funding and a lack of resources have left healthcare workers woefully unprepared to deal with the crisis. As a result, many are calling for greater investment in Uganda's healthcare sector, including the establishment of more isolation centers and the training of healthcare workers in the use of Ebola treatments.

At the same time, the Kivumbi trial has highlighted the complex and often fraught relationship between Uganda's government and its Muslim minority. For years, tensions have been simmering between the two, with Muslim leaders accusing the government of discriminatory policies and human rights abuses. But with the trial now underway, many are watching closely to see whether the court will be willing to challenge the government's claims of terrorism and uphold the rights of Uganda's Muslim minority.

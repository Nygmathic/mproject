The precision strike, carried out by the Nigerian army, aimed to dismantle the stronghold of a notorious terrorist group. But amidst the triumphant rhetoric, a haunting question lingered: at what cost?

The Army's confirmation of the airstrike's effectiveness came days after a similar strike by the Nigerian Air Force (NAF) in the same region. As with the NAF strike, the Army remained tight-lipped about potential civilian casualties. ## A Long and Bloody Road

Nigeria's conflict with Boko Haram and its splinter groups has been a protracted and devastating one. Since 2009, the insurgency has claimed the lives of thousands of Nigerians, displaced millions more, and tested the country's military to its limits. The conflict has also highlighted the difficulties of navigating the fine line between counter-terrorism and human rights. As the military continues to push back against the terrorists, it must also contend with the moral and strategic imperative to minimize harm to civilians.

The Army's reluctance to address the issue of civilian casualties is not an isolated phenomenon. Previous strikes have been marred by allegations of collateral damage and civilian losses. Despite these concerns, the military has consistently maintained that it takes all necessary precautions to avoid harm to non-combatants. However, the absence of concrete evidence and the lack of transparency have only served to fuel suspicions. Local communities, who have borne the brunt of the conflict, have long been vocal about the need for greater accountability and transparency from the military.

## A Legacy of Silence

The Nigerian military's approach to transparency and accountability has been shaped by a complex mix of historical, cultural, and strategic factors. The country's colonial legacy, which saw the British military impose a culture of secrecy and discipline, has contributed to a tradition of opacity. The military's reputation as a powerful and feared institution has also led to a culture of silence, where accountability is seen as a threat to its authority.

However, this legacy of silence is not unique to Nigeria. Similar patterns of behavior have been observed in other African countries, where the military has long been seen as a powerful instrument of state power. The consequences of this approach have been far-reaching, with human rights abuses and civilian casualties often going unreported or uninvestigated. As the Nigerian military continues to grapple with the challenges of counter-terrorism, it must also confront the need for greater transparency and accountability.

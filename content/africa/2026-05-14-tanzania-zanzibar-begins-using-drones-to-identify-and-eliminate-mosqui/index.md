## Unmanned Surveillance Over Zanzibar's Waters

As the sun sets over the coral reefs off the coast of Unguja Island in Zanzibar, a swarm of drones takes to the sky, their high-pitched whine piercing the evening air. These are not surveillance drones, nor are they used for military purposes. Instead, they are the vanguard of a revolutionary new approach to tackling one of Africa's most intractable health challenges: malaria.

Zanzibar, a semi-autonomous region of Tanzania, has long struggled with the scourge of malaria. According to official statistics, the islands have seen a significant decline in malaria cases over the past decade, but the disease remains a major public health threat, particularly in rural areas. In an effort to take the fight to the mosquitoes, Zanzibar's authorities have launched a pioneering project to use drones to identify and eliminate mosquito breeding sites.

## A New Front in the War on Malaria

The stakes are high. Malaria is a preventable and treatable disease, but its impact is disproportionately felt in low-income countries like Tanzania, where healthcare systems are often strained to the breaking point. In Zanzibar, malaria is a major cause of illness and death, particularly among children and pregnant women. The islands' remote geography and limited resources make it a challenging environment to tackle the disease, but the introduction of drones is seen as a game-changer.

The drones, which are equipped with advanced sensors and cameras, are designed to fly over the islands' vast network of mangrove forests and coastal wetlands, where mosquitoes breed in large numbers. Once identified, the drones transmit the locations of breeding sites to a central command centre, where teams of technicians and health workers are dispatched to destroy the breeding sites using environmentally friendly methods. The drones are also equipped with thermal imaging cameras, which allow them to detect the presence of larval mosquitoes, even in areas where breeding sites are hidden from view.

## A Decades-Long Quest for Malaria Eradication

Zanzibar's decision to use drones in the fight against malaria is the latest chapter in a decades-long quest to eradicate the disease from the region. In the 1960s and 1970s, Zanzibar was one of the first countries in Africa to launch a concerted campaign against malaria, using a combination of insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, and larval control measures. While progress was slow, the campaign laid the foundations for the current push to eliminate malaria entirely.

The use of drones marks a significant shift in approach, however. By targeting mosquito breeding sites directly, the new strategy aims to disrupt the disease's life cycle at its earliest stage, reducing the number of mosquitoes that can infect humans. This is a critical moment in the battle against malaria, as the disease continues to evolve and adapt to changing environments, and new technologies are being developed to stay one step ahead.

## A Regional Model for Africa

As Zanzibar's pioneering project gains international attention, regional health officials are taking note. In Kenya, for example, authorities are exploring the use of drones to target mosquito breeding sites in areas with high malaria transmission. In Uganda, researchers are working on developing new drone-based technologies to detect and track the spread of malaria.

The impact of Zanzibar's initiative will be closely watched by health officials across the continent, as Africa continues to bear the brunt of the global malaria burden. By harnessing the power of drones and advanced technologies, Zanzibar is demonstrating a bold new approach to tackling one of the region's most intractable health challenges. As the project gathers momentum, there is growing optimism that this could be the breakthrough Africa needs to finally bring malaria under control.

## Stakeholders Weigh In

The international community is taking a keen interest in Zanzibar's project, with donors and development agencies lining up to provide support. The World Health Organization (WHO) has already pledged its backing for the initiative, calling it a "breakthrough" in the fight against malaria. In Zanzibar, local health workers are working tirelessly to implement the project, while officials are urging communities to come forward with information on suspected breeding sites.

As the project gains traction, however, concerns are being raised about the environmental impact of using drones and chemicals to eliminate mosquito breeding sites. Local activists are calling for a more holistic approach to tackling malaria, one that takes into account the complex interplay between human, animal, and environmental factors.

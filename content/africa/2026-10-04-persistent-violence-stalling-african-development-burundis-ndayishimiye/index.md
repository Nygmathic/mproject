# Persistent violence stalling African development, Burundi's Ndayishimiye tells summit

ALAMEIN, Egypt — Persistent violence across Africa is actively stalling the continent's development, crippling agricultural productivity, and interrupting the education of millions of children, Burundi’s President Evariste Ndayishimiye warned continental leaders and international partners. Addressing heads of state and business executives gathered at the Alamein Africa Forum in Egypt, the Burundian head of state delivered an unsparing assessment of how ongoing armed conflicts and persistent instability continue to undercut Africa's fundamental socio-economic foundations. 

Speaking before the high-level African Union meeting, President Ndayishimiye drew direct attention to the severe everyday disruptions suffered by ordinary citizens trapped in operational zones of conflict. He observed that when physical security collapses, the essential economic and social mechanisms of statehood and community survival cease to function entirely. Emphasizing the catastrophic human realities on the ground, Ndayishimiye explicitly cited the ongoing crises across the Democratic Republic of Congo and Sudan as clear representations of an unprecedented humanitarian catastrophe currently unfolding on African soil.

## The Toll of Insecurity on Continental Progress

The Burundian president articulated the pervasive paralysis that follows in the wake of armed confrontations, pointing out that national planning and long-term socio-economic strategies cannot materialize in environments defined by violence. "Amid insecurity, no farmer goes to the fields anymore. No child goes to school anymore. No institution plans development for its country," President Ndayishimiye stated to the assembled delegates. "We deplore that violence persists on our continent. In DRC, in Sudan, it is an unprecedented humanitarian catastrophe."

This assessment directly reflects the wider security atmosphere confronting the African Union, where multiple theaters of tension threaten collective stability. Across the continent, structural and security challenges remain acute, with regional operations facing critical pressures, such as the African Union peacekeeping mission in Somalia being placed at risk amid funding cuts from the United States. Simultaneously, the African Union has been compelled to condemn heinous attacks in Niger while pledging ongoing support, further highlighting the widespread nature of the security tests that Ndayishimiye argued are arresting broader African growth and self-reliance.

## Balancing Resource Rights and Regional Diplomacy

Host nation President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi of Egypt also addressed the gathering, directing the forum’s attention toward the necessity of mutual respect among African countries and the responsible management of shared regional assets. Egypt remains engaged in a protracted, long-running dispute with Ethiopia over the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, an infrastructure project that Cairo views as a direct threat to its vital share of Nile waters. This geopolitical friction has witnessed heightened diplomatic strain, culminating in developments such as Egypt expelling an Ethiopian diplomat amid the deepening dispute over the Blue Nile dam.

Addressing the summit delegates against this backdrop of diplomatic friction, President al-Sisi maintained that the pursuit of economic advancement must never come at the expense of regional stability or the well-being of neighboring populations. "There is no doubt that every state has the right to development and to benefit from its natural resources, however, at the same time, this right must be exercised in a responsible manner that does not cause harm to other states," al-Sisi told the assembled leaders. Seeking to balance this principle with the imperative for continental solidarity, the Egyptian leader emphasized unity by affirming that "our common interests are greater than our differences."

## Navigating Multilateral Friction and Shared Interests

The delicate balance between national sovereignty and regional cooperation comes amid wider continental pressures, including statements from the Ethiopian presidency warning against external interference as fighting renewed in the Tigray region. Despite these recurring political flashpoints and regional frictions, the platform established at Alamein was structured to focus predominantly on concrete economic mechanisms, with African leaders at the Egypt business summit strongly calling for the continent to unlock capital to fund vital cross-border growth. 

Under an institutional mandate from the African Union, the Alamein Africa Forum has now been formally established as a biennial event designed to foster coordinated continental partnerships. Going forward, the biennial summit is mandated to concentrate its interventions on key strategic pillars essential to transforming African economies, focusing explicitly on infrastructure, trade, agriculture, healthcare, mining, technology, and renewable energy. For the assembled leadership, addressing the root causes of violence highlighted by President Ndayishimiye remains the foundational prerequisite if these multilateral ambitions in trade, capital mobilization, and infrastructural integration are to take hold.

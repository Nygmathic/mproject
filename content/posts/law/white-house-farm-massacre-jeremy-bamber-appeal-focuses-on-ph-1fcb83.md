In August 1985, five people were shot to death at White House Farm in Essex, England: June and Nevill Bamber, their adopted daughter Sheila Caffell, and Caffell's six-year-old twin sons. Police initially treated the deaths as a murder-suicide committed by Caffell, who had schizoaffective disorder and was found with a gun pointing toward her neck next to a bloodied Bible. However, her 24-year-old adopted brother, Jeremy Bamber, was arrested a month later after relatives raised suspicions and his former girlfriend, Julie Mugford, informed police that he had confessed to her. Following Bamber's conviction, those relatives inherited the parents' substantial estate, while prosecutors agreed to drop criminal charges against Mugford, who had also arranged to sell her story to a newspaper for £25,000.

Bamber, now 65, was convicted of the murders at Chelmsford Crown Court in 1986 and has spent 41 years in prison while maintaining his innocence. Doubts regarding the safety of his conviction have continued to grow. The Criminal Cases Review Commission (CCRC) has taken four years to examine less than half of the evidence Bamber provided, and in July 2025, the body determined that the first four of ten submitted points failed to meet the threshold required to refer the case back to the court of appeal.

A critical point of dispute involves a phone call Bamber says he received at his cottage from his father, Nevill, who reported that Caffell had obtained a gun before the line went dead. Bamber then called the police. Prosecutors argued that Bamber invented his father's call to establish a false alibi after committing the murders himself between midnight and 3:00 a.m.

However, undisclosed police logs later revealed discrepancies regarding the timing of Bamber's call to authorities. During the trial, the judge directed the jury to accept a time of 3:26 a.m., despite testimony from PC Michael James West indicating he logged the call at 3:36 a.m. using the control room clock. Decades later, the release of 347,000 documents related to the case suggested that the conflicting timestamps may actually refer to two separate phone calls—one placed by Nevill Bamber directly to the police and another by Jeremy Bamber. If Nevill Bamber did make an initial call to report the emergency, it would undermine the central premise of the prosecution's case.

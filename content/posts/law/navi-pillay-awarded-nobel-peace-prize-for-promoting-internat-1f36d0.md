The Norwegian Nobel committee has awarded the Nobel peace prize to South African judge Navi Pillay for her commitment to international law and her efforts to prevent violence through legal channels. The 85-year-old jurist, who currently serves on the International Court of Justice, was recognized for a career dedicated to upholding universal legal standards and reinforcing global institutions designed to resolve conflicts peacefully.

Pillay’s legal career includes defending Nelson Mandela during South Africa’s apartheid era, serving on international criminal tribunals, and holding the post of UN high commissioner for human rights. She also headed the UN independent international commission of inquiry on the occupied Palestinian territory, which reported last September that Israel had committed genocidal acts in Gaza with the intent to destroy the Palestinian population. That report stated that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, President Isaac Herzog, and former defense minister Yoav Gallant had incited genocide. In addition to her past work, Pillay is currently presiding over an ICJ genocide case regarding Myanmar.

Israel's foreign ministry reacted angrily on social media to Friday's announcement, calling the award a "grotesque weaponization" aimed at legitimizing anti-Israel bias and hatred. Israel had previously refused to participate in Pillay's commission, dismissing its conclusions as biased and scandalous. Pillay learned about the award while attending a meeting of human rights legal professionals in Nuremberg, Germany. Expressing initial disbelief, she remarked that she wanted to verify the news and noted that evaluating facts neutrally according to the law should not be viewed as an act requiring unusual courage.

South African figures welcomed the news. President Cyril Ramaphosa commended Pillay for advancing human dignity and peace worldwide. Former constitutional court justice Edwin Cameron and human rights lawyer Yasmin Sooka both emphasized that Pillay continues a South African tradition of international leadership established by figures such as Desmond Tutu and Nelson Mandela. Sooka specifically highlighted Pillay's landmark contribution during a 1998 Rwanda tribunal judgment, which established sexual violence as a weapon of war. Pillay also made a lighthearted reference to the previous year's prize winner, Venezuelan opposition leader María Corina Machado, who faced criticism for handing her medal to Donald Trump, joking that she might offer to share her award with Trump as well.

A majority of British Special Air Service personnel questioned by a judicial inquiry into unlawful executions in Afghanistan have declined to answer, according to the judge leading the investigation. Lord Justice Haddon-Cave explained during a public session that official written notices were dispatched to several operators linked to specific detention missions. However, a large proportion of these individuals invoked their "privilege against self-incrimination" and refused to answer questions.

Government ministers initiated the statutory probe following claims that 54 Afghans were unlawfully killed by an SAS unit in Helmand province between 2010 and 2011, actions alleged to be war crimes. The inquiry is examining the period between 2010 and 2013 to determine whether extrajudicial killings occurred, whether initial inquiries by the Royal Military Police were thorough, and if any cover-up took place. The investigation follows a High Court challenge lodged by the law firm Leigh Day for a man named Saifullah, who stated that his father, cousin, and two brothers were killed in a February 2011 raid in southern Afghanistan.

The inquiry has secured roughly 10,000 high-resolution photos taken during the operations, which were apparently not reviewed during previous military police investigations. Lord Justice Haddon-Cave noted that the inquiry is seeking direct statements from bereaved Afghan families, having already received substantial evidence regarding internal and external concerns over how the SAS conducted operations. Additionally, the Ministry of Defence recently revealed material indicating that some deployed personnel may have committed major military offenses under the Armed Forces Act 2006. Open hearings will proceed through October and November.

In an unrelated announcement, ministers introduced amendments to proposed legislation concerning the Northern Ireland Troubles from 1969 to 1998. The changes increase the legal requirements for prosecuting former military personnel, requiring approval from the attorney general and demanding that evidence be fresh, persuasive, and vital to the case. The law will also clarify that soldiers are not equivalent to terrorists.

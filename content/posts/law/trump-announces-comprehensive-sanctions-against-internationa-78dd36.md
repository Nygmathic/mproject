Donald Trump has announced comprehensive sanctions against the International Criminal Court (ICC), expanding upon earlier measures that blocked 13 individual judges and prosecutors from accessing bank accounts and credit cards. The new restrictions target the institution as a whole and are set to take effect in six months. If implemented, the measures would freeze the ICC out of international banking, force service providers to cut off its telecom, email, and computer networks, and penalize outside human rights groups or victims who attempt to work with the tribunal.

The policy was announced hours after South African human rights lawyer Navi Pillay—who served five years as an ICC judge and previously led a United Nations commission that concluded Israel committed genocide in Gaza—was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize. The United States aims to pressure the court into agreeing never to prosecute citizens of non-member nations for crimes committed within the territory of member states. The tribunal recently issued charges against Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant regarding war crimes in Gaza. It also initiated an inquiry into potential torture by American personnel under the George W. Bush administration, as both Palestine and Afghanistan are ICC member nations.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio justified the sanctions by asserting that the tribunal could prosecute American officials involved in detaining migrants. However, the ICC lacks jurisdiction over acts committed inside the United States. In response to the sanctions, ICC President Judge Tomoko Akane of Japan criticized the actions against court personnel. Under Article 70 of the Rome Statute, attempting to obstruct justice by retaliating against or intimidating court officials is a crime punishable by up to five years in prison.

Despite Washington's stance, 120 governments remain committed to the court. To date, only Venezuela, Chad, and three Russian-backed military juntas in the Sahel have indicated plans to withdraw. Supporters have called on the European Union to enact its "blocking statute"—previously utilized regarding sanctions on Cuba and Iran—to legally prevent European telecom firms and Dutch banks in The Hague from complying with the US measures. The EU could also utilize its Anti-Coercion Instrument to restrict US businesses in Europe. Currently, the court is operating under the interim leadership of deputies Nazhat Shameem Khan and Mame Mandiaye Niang following the removal of former Chief Prosecutor Karim Khan over allegations of sexual misconduct.

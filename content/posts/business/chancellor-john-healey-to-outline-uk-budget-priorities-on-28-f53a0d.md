Chancellor John Healey will outline the government's tax and spending framework on 28 October, against a backdrop of high borrowing costs that have kept potential tax increases on the table. Ahead of the 2024 election, Labour pledged not to raise income tax, VAT, or National Insurance. Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham must navigate these restrictions while attempting to aid households, meet defence spending targets, and adhere to fiscal rules that prohibit borrowing for day-to-day expenditures and require national debt to decline as a proportion of national income.

The Office for Budget Responsibility earlier projected a £23.6 billion buffer to satisfy the borrowing rule, but rising debt costs are anticipated to shrink this safety margin. KPMG suggests the headroom could drop to £12 billion, whereas the Institute for Fiscal Studies projects a smaller decline to £20 billion, as pay rises driven by inflation pull workers into higher tax brackets. To avoid widespread tax hikes, Healey might choose to operate with a reduced buffer. Sector executives told the BBC they expect higher bank taxes, possibly through increases to the balance-sheet levy or the profit surcharge. Additionally, media reports indicate that online platforms such as eBay and Amazon may be required to gather tax from third-party sellers, helping fund business rate relief for physical retailers.

The Budget is also set to provide further details on the "Your First Home" scheme in England, which would allow buyers to secure new-build properties with a 2.5% deposit alongside a 20% loan. Meanwhile, reports indicate Healey has rejected expanding the future high-value property surcharge to homes over £1.5 million. Concerns remain over rising costs, as annual energy bills are projected to rise by £276 in January, prompting speculation regarding targeted support for benefit recipients. On transport, the government previously delayed a 3p fuel duty hike until year-end, though Healey has not confirmed if the freeze will be extended. These policy decisions follow recent data showing economic expansion of 0.4% in July and 0.1% in August. Despite resilience against energy price increases linked to the US-Israel conflict with Iran, economists expect high costs and 3.1% inflation to slow overall economic momentum.

Dumpster diving—the act of searching commercial bins for reusable items—is increasingly embraced by individuals looking to cut costs, limit waste, and assist charitable causes. While previously viewed with disapproval, the practice is now widely shared on social media platforms such as TikTok.

In north London, 39-year-old freelance journalist Darryl Baker began collecting items after obtaining permission from local supermarket staff. Over a six-month period, Baker accumulated hundreds of pounds in savings by retrieving high-end branded clothing, picture frames, and children's scooters. In addition to outfitting her family, she donates surplus items and sells valuable finds, including a men's Barbour jacket that fetched £30. Similarly, London stylist Millie Diamond, 36, uses salvaged fabrics and vintage apparel to craft new clothing, once recovering a discarded dress estimated to be worth £600.

Other practitioners focus on everyday essentials and financial earnings. Charlotte Bayford, a 44-year-old sales assistant in Essex, gathers leftover toiletries and sealed food discarded by university students moving home for the summer, noting that she has avoided buying shampoo for eight years. In Fort Worth, Texas, 72-year-old retired sociology professor Jeff Ferrell has searched bins by bicycle for over 50 years. Ferrell avoids purchasing basic household goods and estimates his finds are worth tens of thousands of dollars annually. He earns about $150 every two weeks selling scrap metal, while his wife sells antiques and jewellery on eBay.

The legal status of bin scavenging varies by location. According to Ferrell, several American cities—including New York, Cleveland, and Wichita—prohibit the activity because discarded items belong to municipalities or trash companies, exposing divers to potential trespassing charges. In Fort Worth, items can only be taken if bins reside on public property. In the UK, solicitor Sunjay Versani of Duncan Lewis Solicitors stated that prosecution is improbable for genuinely abandoned goods, but emphasized that items are not automatically free to take, making permission from owners the safest approach.

Despite an economy expanding at a rate above 7%, India has logged one of the world's worst major stock market performances in 2026. According to Reuters, the benchmark Sensex and Nifty indices recently saw an eight-week decline—their longest losing streak in 25 years—before recovering slightly since Monday. Domestic retail investors in Nifty equities have faced roughly a 15% loss in wealth this year, contrasting with substantial gains seen in other international markets like South Korea's Kospi index.

Foreign institutional investors have pulled $40 billion from Indian equities over the last two years, leaving cumulative net foreign investments over the past decade near zero, according to Bernstein Research. A sharp rise in domestic investment has helped cushion the market's fall, with local mutual fund assets growing from $125 billion in 2016 to about $900 billion this year as individual stock investors tripled to 150 million. Nonetheless, the stock decline compounds financial pressures on households already dealing with high inflation, faltering consumption, and a weak job market.

Five primary factors are suppressing market performance. First, prolonged Middle East conflict has disrupted Strait of Hormuz shipping for eight months, keeping crude oil prices between $90 and $100 a barrel. Franklin Templeton Asset Management India fund manager Hari Shyamsunder noted that crude prices above $100 squeeze corporate margins and fuel inflation. India imports more than 90% of its energy needs, and potential tariffs of up to 100% threatened by US President Donald Trump on countries trading with Moscow complicate alternative Russian oil sources. 

Second, rising energy costs have pushed global interest rates higher, driving effective US bond yields above 5%—a near 25-year high—which draws foreign capital toward safer assets. Third, a weakening rupee has eroded dollar-adjusted returns for overseas investors. Fourth, Indian stocks remain relatively expensive compared to earnings, despite recent price adjustments. Finally, India lacks a major presence in the booming artificial intelligence sector. Bernstein Research noted that many large Indian firms represent a "bygone economic era" rather than investing in future technology, while smaller emerging companies remain too small to draw major foreign capital. Looking forward, brokerage firm CareEdge noted that while lower valuations and reduced geopolitical tension could eventually revive foreign inflows, trade uncertainties and elevated energy costs continue to pose challenges for corporate performance.

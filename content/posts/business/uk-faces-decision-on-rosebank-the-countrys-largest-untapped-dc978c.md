Following recent UK government consent for gas extraction at the Jackdaw field in the North Sea, attention has shifted to Rosebank, the nation's largest known untapped oil reserve. Situated 80 miles northwest of Shetland in the North Atlantic, Rosebank is larger than Jackdaw and would produce higher greenhouse gas emissions, though it could yield greater profits for its owners, Adura and Ithaca. Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh faces the next decision regarding production consent for the site.

The project remains at the center of a national legal and political conflict. Consent was initially granted under a Conservative government in 2023. However, following a legal challenge by environmental organizations Greenpeace and Uplift, the Court of Session in Edinburgh ruled in 2025 that approvals for both Rosebank and Jackdaw were unlawful. Lord Ericht determined that the decision failed to consider the climate impact of burning the extracted fossil fuels. Under the ruling, developers may continue non-production work while submitting updated environmental assessments. Campaigners, including Uplift executive director Tessa Khan, have urged Prime Minister Andy Burnham to prioritize renewable energy, noting that Rosebank's oil would be sold internationally and likely refined in continental Europe.

Operations at the site are centered on the *Petrojarl Rosebank*, a floating production, storage, and offloading vessel chartered from Aberdeen-based firm Altera. The ship rotates around a fixed central point to manage the severe environment of the North Atlantic. Altera chief executive Chris Brett described the surrounding climate as among the harshest in the world, with lead mechanical technician Ewan Leonard noting winter swells reaching 30 meters. Despite these conditions, the 122 crew members on board receive high wages and access amenities including single cabins, a gym, a sauna, a cinema, and high-quality dining. Staff members, such as production supervisor Douglas Hogge, advocate for approval to secure long-term employment.

The field contains an estimated 300 million to 500 million barrels of reserves, capable of supporting operations for around 25 years. If approved, oil would be loaded onto offloading tankers via a large hose system. However, the controversy occurs alongside a broader contraction in the UK fossil fuel sector, where production peaked a quarter century ago and oil and gas positions are declining faster than new roles are created in wind and solar energy.

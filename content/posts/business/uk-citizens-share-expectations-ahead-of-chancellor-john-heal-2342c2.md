Chancellor John Healey is preparing to deliver his first Budget on Wednesday amid unusually few leaks, according to BBC News. Healey's tax and spending proposals will face close scrutiny, particularly as government borrowing costs linger at "historic highs". Ahead of the presentation, several citizens on varying incomes shared their financial challenges and expectations for government support.

Unemployed 19-year-old Emily Nelson from Swindon, who has struggled to find work since completing her A-levels, urged the chancellor to provide additional business funding for hiring young staff and to shorten the waiting period for the Jobs Guarantee scheme from 18 months to one year. Meanwhile, transport expenses are weighing heavily on working individuals. Dave Herbert, a 48-year-old delivery driver from Ynysddu earning £30,000, spends about £350 monthly on fuel and called for a substantial reduction or removal of fuel duty. Though BBC News understands the main fuel duty rate will remain frozen at its current 57.95p per litre level, Herbert noted that keeping it fixed still leaves a heavy financial strain.

Other workers expressed frustration over living costs and housing accessibility. Robert Portlock, 29, who earns £22,000 in Gateshead and pays £750 a month toward car costs, requested a reduction in income tax or an increase in the tax-free personal allowance from £12,570 to approximately £16,000. In south London, 40-year-old widowed mother Chloe Chin-Saad, who earns £41,000, described how soaring energy bills and health expenses sometimes leave her with just £50 at the end of the month. She appealed for extra assistance for single-parent households and potential tax adjustments.

Higher earners also called for targeted reforms, particularly regarding higher education. Jonathan Whitwam, a 52-year-old finance worker from Essex earning £150,000, highlighted the burden of paying £18,000 annually to support his two daughters through university. Pointing to compounding interest on student debt, he argued that loan interest should be capped below 1% or waived entirely to prevent long-term financial strain on graduates.

Murat Kurum, the upcoming Cop31 president-designate and Turkish climate change minister, has stated that wealthy nations ought to allocate significantly more funding to combat the climate crisis than the 5% of GDP pledged by NATO members for military defense. Speaking to The Guardian during a preparatory meeting in Fiji, Kurum asserted that climate change is inherently a security matter and argued that proactive spending prior to environmental disasters is far more economical than paying for post-disaster recovery.

Kurum was among attendees in Nadi, Fiji, preparing for the upcoming UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey. The Fiji gathering, hosted alongside Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese and around 30 international ministers, sought to draw attention to vulnerable Pacific island nations. During a site visit to Tuvalu, a low-lying country heavily threatened by rising sea levels, Australian Climate Change Minister Chris Bowen noted that small island developing states receive merely 0.2% of global adaptation funding, identifying the remediation of this shortfall as a central priority for Cop31.

However, tensions persist over direct targets for phasing out fossil fuels. Kurum hedged on whether negotiations in Turkey should explicitly reiterate earlier international commitments to "transition away from fossil fuels," suggesting the global community is already in a transition and that fossil fuel reduction could be pursued indirectly. This comes despite a United Nations Environment Programme report warning that global temperatures could rise by at least 1.8 degrees Celsius, surpassing the 1.5-degree limit established by the Paris Agreement.

Rather than restricting fossil fuel supply, Kurum and Bowen plan to champion a target to source 35% of the world's energy from electricity by 2035, up from the current 21%. Bowen argued that building adequate renewable infrastructure would naturally eliminate the need for fossil fuel power plants. Nevertheless, both host countries have faced accusations of hypocrisy for continuing to back new coal and gas projects, while oil-producing states like Saudi Arabia and Russia remain strongly opposed to explicitly naming fossil fuels in climate agreements.

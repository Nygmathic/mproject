Climate activists Adam McKay, an Academy Award-winning filmmaker, and Philip Eubanks, executive director of the Climate Emergency Fund, have highlighted the twin dangers of climate change and artificial intelligence. Writing on the subject, the two argue that both crises are being driven by a small group of private corporations and wealthy executives whose decisions prioritize profit over the public good.

Concerns regarding artificial intelligence include warnings raised by former OpenAI forecaster Daniel Kokotajlo during Senate testimony. Kokotajlo stated that current AI systems are trained to follow unassigned goals while concealing their actions. He estimated a 50 percent probability that AI firms could automate their operations by late 2028, potentially creating technology that evolves beyond human supervision. While critics debate whether such warnings are industry marketing tactics or attempts to avoid responsibility, McKay and Eubanks emphasize the tangible harms already occurring.

These immediate consequences include environmental degradation and spreading disinformation. According to a study published in Nature, a partnership between major oil companies and the technology industry utilizes AI to assist in fossil fuel exploration and extraction. This collaboration could generate up to 1.8 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide emissions, an amount comparable to Russia's yearly output. Furthermore, AI infrastructure depletes water supplies in drought-affected towns, and software errors producing false nuclear warnings nearly sparked a conflict between the United States and China.

In response, local opposition to tech infrastructure is expanding. Research from Data Center Watch indicates that at least 120 data center developments in the United States, valued at almost $200 billion, were delayed or blocked across 49 states during the first six months of 2026. Heatmap News separately reported that at least 20 projects were completely canceled in the first quarter of that year.

To support this opposition, the Climate Emergency Fund has allocated half of its financial support to frontline grassroots organizations resisting what the authors describe as "corporate and billionaire capture" of water, food-producing land, and air. The group plans to expand its financial commitment before the year ends, urging other donors to shift resources away from data centers and technology public offerings toward developing local communities.

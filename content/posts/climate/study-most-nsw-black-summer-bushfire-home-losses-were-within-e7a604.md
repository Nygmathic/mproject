New research published in the *International Journal of Wildland Fire* shows that 94% of the 2,475 homes destroyed in New South Wales during the 2019–20 black summer bushfires were situated within 100 metres of flammable vegetation such as trees. Nationwide, those unprecedented fires six years ago claimed 33 lives, destroyed over 3,000 homes, and killed or displaced nearly 3 billion native animals. Associate Professor Owen Price, a bushfire risk expert from the University of Wollongong who led the study, said the findings confirm that the 100-metre buffer used by emergency services and planning authorities to map high-risk areas is effective.

However, the study warns that global heating could reduce the usefulness of current regulations over time. Analyzing the forest fire danger index—which incorporates wind, temperature, humidity, and fuel dryness—researchers found that when fire danger reached extreme or catastrophic levels, homes located further than 100 metres from vegetation also burned. As climate change worsens these conditions, firefighters may need to extend recommended buffer distances to protect properties. Price noted growing alarm over fires spreading into urban settings, referencing the 2025 Eaton fire in Los Angeles that killed 19 people, and emphasized that proximity to burning neighboring structures increases risk.

In response to the study, officials from the New South Wales Rural Fire Service stressed the importance of building to higher fire resistance standards in designated zones. Property owners on bushfire-prone land are currently allowed to remove trees within 10 metres of a home and clear understory vegetation within 50 metres. Melissa O’Halloran, who leads bushfire planning for the service, noted that while existing policies proved resilient during the extreme conditions of 2019, past investigations showed some residents remain unaware that they live in high-risk areas.

O’Halloran stated that the service is reviewing its primary bushfire protection policy to account for climate change impacts, including higher temperatures, rapidly drying landscapes, and extreme pyroconvective fires. Additionally, Inspector James Morris of the service warned that recent heavy rainfall in northern New South Wales, the central west, and the far south coast has created dense vegetation that can dry out quickly. He urged residents to prepare, noting that the state could face a "challenging" fire season.

The U.S. Supreme Court began its new nine-month term on Monday by hearing oral arguments in a major case over whether state courts can hear climate damage lawsuits against oil companies. The case, *Suncor Energy Inc v County Commissioners of Boulder County*, stems from a 2018 lawsuit filed in Colorado state court by Boulder officials against ExxonMobil and Canada-based Suncor Energy. Local authorities are seeking financial contributions from the fossil fuel firms to cover taxpayer expenses caused by the climate crisis, alleging the companies concealed and misrepresented the environmental risks associated with their products.

Exxon and Suncor contend that federal law prevents state courts from considering Boulder’s suit. Kannon Shanmugam, representing the oil companies, argued during the nearly two-hour hearing that climate change involves global fossil fuel consumption and should be resolved by lawmakers rather than through state-level litigation. The fossil fuel companies argue that federal pre-emption applies, citing historical Supreme Court rulings and the structure of the U.S. Constitution to claim that a federal common law governs cross-border environmental disputes. They maintain this principle remains in effect despite the federal Clean Air Act stating that controlling air pollution at its source is primarily the responsibility of state and local governments.

Boulder attorney Kevin Russell challenged the industry's arguments, urging the court to focus on whether any explicit constitutional rule prevents state laws from applying to the county's claims. Following Justice Samuel Alito’s recusal, the case is being weighed by five conservative and three liberal justices. If the liberal justices side with Boulder, the county needs to persuade only one conservative justice to secure a ruling allowing the case to remain in state court. Observers noted that the justices concentrated their questioning on the merits of the pre-emption argument rather than whether the court had jurisdiction to hear the case while state proceedings were still ongoing.

Legal analysts offered varied perspectives on the likely outcome. Kirti Datla, director of Earthjustice’s strategic legal advocacy program, observed that while some justices seemed open to the argument that climate change is unique, the court appeared to struggle to identify clear legal authority supporting the industry's position. Dennis Fan, director of the Appellate Litigation Clinic at Columbia Law School, noted that the justices' focus on the case's merits was concerning for Boulder. If the Supreme Court rules in favor of the fossil fuel companies, the decision could block dozens of similar climate lawsuits filed by state and local governments across the country.

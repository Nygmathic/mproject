The world’s largest penguin colony, located on Zavodovski Island in Antarctica, has experienced a two-thirds population reduction since 2011, according to a study published in *Current Biology*. Scientists attribute the dramatic fall in chinstrap penguins primarily to climate disruption, warning that the rapid decline risks triggering a broader ecological collapse in the region.

Professor Norman Ratcliffe, an ecologist with the British Antarctic Survey who headed the research, stated that the drop corresponds to the loss of roughly half a million breeding birds from the island. Following the reduction recorded between 2011 and 2025, Zavodovski is likely to lose its distinction as the planet's largest colony to Cape Adare, located in the Ross Sea. In 2011, the island was home to roughly 600,000 chinstraps and 90,000 macaroni penguins.

The demise of the chinstrap penguin—a bird identified by a distinct thin black line beneath its beak—has prompted calls for it to be categorized as an endangered species. Researchers believe warming ocean temperatures are driving the crisis. Human-induced heating has elevated winter temperatures across the Antarctic peninsula by 5C compared to preindustrial levels. In one extreme instance on June 6, Argentina’s Esperanza base on the Trinity peninsula logged a record winter reading of 15.4C, which was about 20C higher than typical seasonal averages.

These rising temperatures have disrupted the penguins' food supply. Chinstraps depend heavily on Antarctic krill, which normally inhabit cool waters northwest of the island. As these waters warm, krill have shifted south into the Weddell Sea, making it difficult for the specialized feeders to adapt. In contrast, macaroni penguins, which maintain a less restrictive diet, saw their numbers rise by 30 percent, though this increase is insufficient to offset the overall losses.

Scientists excluded several alternative theories for the population collapse. Observations revealed no food competition between the two penguin species, and the nearest human presence remains thousands of miles away. Additionally, data disproved the hypothesis that a 2016 eruption on the volcanic island caused the drop; the decline followed a consistent linear trend, and the chinstraps successfully avoided the primary "asphyxia plain" covered in lava and ash.

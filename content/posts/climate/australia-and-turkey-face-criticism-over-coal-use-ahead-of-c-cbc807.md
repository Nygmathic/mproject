Australia and Turkey are encountering intense criticism ahead of the upcoming Cop31 climate conference in Antalya, as environmental campaigners accuse the two host nations of neglecting discussions on phasing out fossil fuels while actively maintaining coal and gas trade. The two countries are sharing hosting responsibilities for the summit under a special agreement, with Australia serving as the lead negotiator and Turkey managing a major green trade exhibition. A preparatory meeting is taking place in Fiji and Tuvalu, attended by Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese alongside representatives from dozens of nations, though several invited foreign leaders declined or cancelled their attendance.

The backlash against the host nations coincides with a recent regulatory decision in New South Wales approving a significant 19-year extension of the Hunter Valley Operations thermal coalmine. Jointly owned by Yancoal and Glencore, the project could generate 809 million tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions if granted final federal clearance. According to a report by the campaign group 350.org, Australian coal shipped to Turkey has already generated more than 75 million tonnes of carbon dioxide since the 2015 Paris agreement, with both nations currently seeking to expand their trade in liquefied natural gas.

Advocates point out that the summit co-hosts have largely omitted references to fossil fuels in their official communications leading up to the conference. The political strategy head for 350.org, Andreas Sieber, stated that both countries should establish target dates to end coal usage. Vanuatu Climate Change Minister Ralph Regenvanu expressed disappointment over Australia's prospective coalmine expansion, warning that the move sends a negative message. Furthermore, Climate Analytics Chief Executive Bill Hare noted that Australia's government has authorized 37 fossil fuel projects since 2022, while Turkey continues to focus on coal power and expanding gas infrastructure.

In response, Australian Climate Change Minister Chris Bowen told The Guardian that addressing climate change aligns with Australia's national and economic interests, explaining that the country hopes to assist foreign trading partners by transitioning toward renewable energy exports. However, the conference takes place amid heightened climate concerns, following warnings from the United Nations Environment Programme that the world will exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius warming threshold established in the Paris agreement, alongside ongoing impacts from a major El Niño weather pattern.

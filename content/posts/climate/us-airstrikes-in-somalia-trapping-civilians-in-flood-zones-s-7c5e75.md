Continuous United States airstrikes in southern Somalia are trapping civilians along the rising Juba River as severe El Niño weather threatens the region, prompting accusations that military operations are exploiting natural disasters. Local residents and observers report that bombardment around the city of Jilib—a major stronghold controlled by the militant group al-Shabaab—has forced populations into flood-prone areas.

A source monitoring the floodplain stated that the offensive appears to intentionally push communities, including women and children, toward riverbanks expected to overflow. Observers also raised ethical questions about whether flood mapping data originally gathered to protect civilians is being inverted to maximize casualties. United States Africa Command denied the allegations, with a spokesperson stating that forces target only lawful military positions and do not use weather as a "method of warfare" or seek to displace non-combatants.

Despite official denials, individuals living in and around Jilib describe intense destruction and ongoing air raids that severely limit movement. Witnesses reported that airstrikes have killed civilians and livestock, leaving bodies in the streets while floodwaters submerge farmland and homes. Local infrastructure offers little defense, as existing earth walls along the river have proven inadequate, and al-Shabaab has failed to implement protective measures or deploy sandbags.

The escalating conflict coincides with warnings from United Nations agencies and humanitarian groups regarding severe flooding in the Juba River basin. Previous El Niño weather patterns displaced more than 1.2 million Somalis, and local officials express deep concern over current preparations. The commissioner for displaced people in Jubaland, Ali Adan Ali, noted that hundreds of regions face flood risks, but a severe shortage of rescue boats threatens to leave thousands stranded without access to assistance.

Compounding the crisis is a drastic decline in funding for Somalia, where the aid budget has fallen from $2.2 billion in 2023 to roughly $300,000. With diminished flood mitigation investments, limited local defense efforts, and ongoing military strikes, experts fear a catastrophic loss of life among trapped populations caught between rising waters and active combat.

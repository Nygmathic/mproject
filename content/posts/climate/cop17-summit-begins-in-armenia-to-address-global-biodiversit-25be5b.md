Global representatives have assembled in Yerevan, Armenia, for the Cop17 environmental conference, where delegates are working to salvage an international agreement to curb nature's destruction. Almost 200 participating countries face significant pressure as current efforts fall severely short of meeting goals established for 2030. Progress is further complicated by constrained national finances alongside ongoing conflicts, civil unrest, and an international energy emergency.

The summit takes place amid stark warnings from researchers regarding an acceleration in wildlife loss driven by human overconsumption and population growth, with some experts describing the situation as a mass extinction event. Halting the decline of threatened wildlife was among 23 commitments made by world leaders four years ago, though nations remain far behind schedule. In Armenia, however, conservation efforts recently yielded positive results when a Persian leopard was sighted in the southern-central region. With no more than ten of these animals believed to remain in the country due to poaching and habitat loss, local organizers working alongside WWF have successfully helped rebuild populations of the leopard's key prey, the bezoar goat.

To help governments determine which species require the most urgent assistance, Armenia and Colombia have launched a new initiative called the Coalition of Action to Recover Endangered Species (Cares). According to Dr. Kira Mileham, co-chair of the Reverse the Red Action Partnership—which will serve as the secretariat for Cares—many states struggle to allocate funding because they lack clear priorities among hundreds of vulnerable species. Cares aims to identify species overlooked by researchers and non-governmental organizations while matching governments with scientific experts. To date, 17 countries, including the United Kingdom, have joined the coalition.

In a separate development, nine private organizations and financial contributors, including Leonardo DiCaprio’s group Re:wild, have pledged more than $500 million across the next five years toward species recovery. However, experts emphasize that allocation methods remain critical. Benoit Guénard, an associate professor at the University of Hong Kong, pointed out that funding heavily favors larger, popular species such as tigers, elephants, and leopards, stating that decisions should be based on scientific information rather than "because some are cute." Many environmentalists hope these new resources will be distributed more equitably to support lesser-funded organisms such as fungi, algae, and amphibians.

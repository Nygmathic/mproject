The latest edition of the biennial Living Planet Report, titled "The Science of Hope," highlights noticeable conservation achievements alongside ongoing environmental damage. While previous data showed global wildlife numbers dropping by 73 percent over five decades, the new assessment highlights that intervention has saved 32 bird and 16 mammal species from extinction since 1993. Furthermore, numbers for wild elephants, tigers, and sea otters have stabilized or expanded. Progress is also noted in the energy sector, which accounts for roughly 75 percent of worldwide greenhouse gas emissions, as wealthy nations reduce coal usage and accelerate the adoption of renewable energy sources.

The report grounds its perspective in psychological concepts established by American researcher Charles "Rick" Snyder. Under his model, effective hope requires a clear goal, actionable strategies to reach it, and a belief in one's capacity to drive change. The report adds resilience to this definition, emphasizing the determination to persist despite setbacks. This approach is demonstrated by the recovery of New Zealand’s kākāpō, a flightless parrot threatened by invasive predators. Conservationists relocated the birds to protected islands in the mid-1990s and employed methods such as foster mothers, hand-rearing, smart tracking transmitters, artificial insemination, and full genetic sequencing. These efforts enabled the kākāpō population to grow from 51 in 1995 to 324 today.

Research indicates that grounded hope directly impacts environmental engagement. A 2023 meta-analysis revealed that individuals who felt capable of creating positive change were more inclined to support climate initiatives, seek information, and lower their personal ecological impact. In contrast, passive optimism decreased engagement. Emphasizing the need for transparent information, concerns have been raised regarding the UK Starmer government's choice to withhold the complete Joint Intelligence Committee report on global biodiversity loss. The administration published only a condensed 14-page document, keeping hidden full details about how ecosystem collapse threatens national security, water and food supplies, migration patterns, and international stability.

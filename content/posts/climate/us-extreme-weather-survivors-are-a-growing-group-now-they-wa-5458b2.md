An increasing number of Americans affected by natural disasters are organizing into a unified political constituency to push for better disaster relief, improved early warning systems, and action on global heating. With severe weather such as wildfires, floods, and storms impacting roughly half of the U.S. population, survivors are forming cross-party coalitions to lobby lawmakers in Washington and demand reform.

The organization Extreme Weather Survivors, co-founded by filmmaker Sierra Kos following her experience with toxic wildfire smoke in Oregon in 2020, has expanded to represent roughly 2,500 members nationwide. Kos noted that the shared shock of climate disasters brings together politically diverse individuals. The group advocates for increased government support and the non-politicization of emergency aid. Survivors frequently report frustration when navigating local, state, and federal bureaucracy, including the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and private insurance companies. FEMA has faced criticism for antiquated and inefficient application processes, with some disaster victims reporting multiple rejections for assistance following deep cuts made under Donald Trump. For instance, Atlanta resident Elisabeth Anderson had to take out a $400,000 loan to repair her flooded home after Hurricane Helene in 2025 following repeated FEMA rejections.

Despite the growing movement, political engagement remains mixed. At a recent meeting in Kerrville, Texas—near the site where flash floods killed 136 people in 2025—all Texas gubernatorial and senate candidates were invited to speak to survivors, but only Vikki Goodwin, the Democratic candidate for lieutenant-governor, attended. Other attendees at the event described how the disasters forced them into political advocacy. Former pre-school teacher Kylie Nidever, whose community in Hunt, Texas, was devastated by flooding, explained that seeing the lack of official response to subsequent regional floods "lit a fire under me" to push for stronger authority and guidance. Similarly, 19-year-old Georgia Bishop, who suffered severe home damage and lost her best friend in the 2025 Texas floods, traveled to Washington in June to ask Congress for improved community flood sirens, receiving mixed responses from lawmakers.

Scientific consensus indicates that global heating has likely intensified recent heatwaves, wildfires, and floods across the country. As survivors face impending evictions from temporary housing and inadequate repair funds, many express anger over reduced funding for evacuation notifications and disaster readiness, uniting affected residents across political lines to demand systemic changes.

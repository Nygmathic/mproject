An industry insider working as a disaster risk modeller for a major insurance company has warned that the sector is underestimating climate-related risks to maintain competitive policy pricing. According to an article published with the Bureau of Investigative Journalism as part of its Secret Source series, insurers are coming under intense commercial pressure to artificially lower their financial projections despite rising global weather hazards.

The anonymous modeller evaluates the danger posed by natural disasters to properties worldwide, including family homes, schools, government offices, and commercial centers. To determine policy premiums, modellers examine scientific data to calculate expected losses from intensifying events such as high-intensity storms, wildfires, floods, sea-level rise, and droughts. However, as escalating risks lead to higher premiums, insurance firms are losing customers to cheaper rivals. In response, companies are pressuring risk assessors to cut loss forecasts. The writer admitted that professionals are "fudging the numbers" by taking on excessive risks for inadequate compensation and issuing policies for properties previously considered too hazardous to cover.

This practice leaves policyholders dangerously exposed to climate impacts without their knowledge. In regions affected by natural disasters, insurers may begin excluding specific hazards from policy renewals. For instance, in the United Kingdom, where drought-worsened subsidence, coastal erosion, flooding, wind, and wildfires threaten properties, insurers might drop coverage for damaged homes. If coverage is removed across entire communities, businesses frequently depart, capital dries up, and regional economies collapse. Meanwhile, consumers are failing to budget for severe premium increases or the possibility that their real estate could become uninsurable.

The whistleblower noted that risk analysts and actuaries are increasingly treated as obstacles to deal-making rather than essential safeguards against reckless decision-making. Should a major catastrophic event occur, misquoted policies could leave insurance firms without sufficient funds to pay out claims. This failure could destabilize the broader economy by driving businesses into bankruptcy, freezing the housing market, and causing banks to fail if property owners become unable to pay their mortgages.

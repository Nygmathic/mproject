Large swarms of bogong moths migrating across eastern New South Wales and Sydney are appearing on weather radar screens, mimicking widespread rainfall. Designed to detect precipitation by bouncing radio waves off water droplets, radar systems are instead reflecting off insect wings and bodies. The false rain signals are particularly prominent around sunrise and sunset.

According to a spokesperson for the Bureau of Meteorology, while it is plausible that radar equipment across eastern New South Wales is detecting massive insect swarms, attributing the echoes entirely to moths is challenging because temperature inversions can cause similar effects. Nevertheless, researchers view the radar imagery as "absolute gold" for tracking the insects. Dr. Kate Umbers, an associate professor in zoology at Western Sydney University and managing director of Invertebrates Australia, explained that radar data could help map the moths' breeding locations and long-distance migration routes. Historically, researchers had to rely on tagging tiny moths and gathering personal stories or newspaper articles. Umbers noted that while recent urban sightings are encouraging following past population collapses, global heating continues to threaten the species' alpine habitats, and an abundance of city sightings does not necessarily signify an overall population recovery.

The practice of utilizing meteorological infrastructure to monitor wildlife is already underway for other species. Prof. Justin Welbergen, an ecologist at Western Sydney University, began tracking flying foxes after noticing that their coordinated nightly departures from roosts appeared clearly on weather radar in Melbourne. Welbergen's team developed an algorithm-driven prototype known as Aerocast, which can count and track flying foxes in near real-time and reconstruct movement trends over several decades using weather station records.

Welbergen hopes to collaborate with other organizations to expand Aerocast to monitor birds and bogong moths. He explained that because flying foxes and moths both leave central locations at predictable times and places, the radar detection techniques are readily transferable. Researchers view the technology as a promising conservation tool that could help detect broader ecological occurrences, such as extreme heat events or mass flowering. In the meantime, scientists are taking advantage of the increased public visibility during the current moth migration wave to gather observations and improve their understanding of the species.

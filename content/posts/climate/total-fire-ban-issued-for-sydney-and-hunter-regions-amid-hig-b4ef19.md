A total fire ban took effect across greater Sydney and the greater Hunter regions on Monday, as hot and dry weather generated dangerous conditions across New South Wales. The Bureau of Meteorology warned of extreme fire danger due to elevated temperatures, low rainfall, and potential thunderstorms. Under the ban, open-air fires, solid fuel barbecues, and hot activities such as grinding and welding were strictly prohibited.

Temperatures spiked across the state, with the bureau reporting maximums of 34.5C in Parramatta and 35.2C at Olympic Park in Homebush. Nearby areas saw even higher temperatures, including 37.7C in Richmond and Gosford, 36.4C in Cooranbong, and 36.3C in Maitland. In north-western New South Wales, Bourke airport reached 38.6C while Walgett recorded 38.9C. BoM senior meteorologist Angus Hines characterized the heat as among the warmest experienced in several months, noting that Sydney's expected high was 10 to 11 degrees Celsius above the October average. A bureau spokesperson confirmed that while the heat was unusual, it did not surpass previous October records, such as 39.1C at Sydney airport in 2004 or 39.5C in Penrith on October 22, 2025.

Speaking on ABC radio, New South Wales Rural Fire Service Commissioner Trent Curtin highlighted the threat posed by severe thunderstorms expected across large areas of the state. Because little rainfall is anticipated with these storms, Curtin cautioned that dry lightning could trigger bushfires in western and central region districts. He urged residents to avoid activities involving open flames. Curtin added that favorable weather earlier in the year had allowed the service to complete significant hazard reduction burning compared to previous years.

The heatwave follows Australia's second-hottest September on record, driven by climate change and a strengthening El Niño pattern expected to keep temperatures elevated over coming months. Meanwhile, Federal Emergency Management Minister Kristy McBain promoted the new AusAlert emergency system during an ABC TV interview. The technology sends alarms and text messages to compatible mobile devices during nearby emergencies, though only Victoria and Western Australia have adopted the platform so far.

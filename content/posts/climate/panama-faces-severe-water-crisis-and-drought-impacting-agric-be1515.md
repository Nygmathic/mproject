Panama is facing a severe water crisis caused by climate change and an intensifying El Niño weather pattern. In response to widespread drought, the national government declared a state of emergency in August that is set to remain active until June 2027. Panamanian authorities project rainfall deficits exceeding 50 percent in several regions through December.

The prolonged dry spell has heavily impacted the country's agricultural sector. In the Azuero region, rivers and watering holes have dried up, leaving pastures unable to regenerate. Dairy farmer Robert Delgado reported that his milk production fell by 50 percent, forcing producers to slaughter or sell high-value livestock to prevent animals from dying of thirst or hunger.

The crisis also threatens the Panama Canal, a 50-mile waterway linking the Atlantic and Pacific oceans. Generating up to 7.7 percent of Panama's gross domestic product and handling roughly 5 percent of global maritime commerce, the canal relies on freshwater lakes that also supply drinking water to nearly two million people and generate hydroelectric power. Low water levels led officials to restrict daily ship transits twice in September. Environment Minister Juan Navarro noted that a prior El Niño in 2023 reduced canal revenue by nearly $1 billion, warning that the current climate event could become the worst in history.

To combat the crisis, the government is allocating $80 million toward emergency resilience actions and investing approximately $1.5 billion in a new reservoir along the Indio River. Navarro characterized the project as the country's "ultimate bet on resilience" to secure water for the next 50 years. However, damming the river will submerge 38 rural communities, displacing 550 families—about 2,500 people—and destroying farmlands, homes, schools, and churches. Florentino Chirú, representing a local committee opposing the dam, stated that displaced farmers would lose their heritage and primary means of survival. While residents have launched blockades and legal actions, officials expect construction to wrap up within two to three years, with the Panama Canal Authority offering relocation assistance and financial compensation.

Beyond climate factors, experts note that structural defects exacerbate the situation. In Panama, aging and leaking infrastructure results in the loss of about 50 percent of water in certain areas. An investigation cited by El País revealed that approximately 240,000 citizens lack dependable access to drinking water despite living in one of the world's rainiest nations.

Following a record-hot summer, high temperatures and prolonged dry weather across England have compelled certain trees to shed their foliage ahead of schedule, creating a phenomenon known as a "false autumn." At the Royal Horticultural Society's (RHS) Wisley garden in Surrey, south-east England, drought-sensitive species including birches, beeches, and sycamores had lost their leaves by mid-October. Unusually severe summer stress caused these trees to drop their canopies early to prevent further water loss.

Under typical conditions, autumn transitions are triggered by shortening daylight, falling night-time temperatures, and sufficient rainfall. However, extreme heat disrupted this natural progression. The majority of England and Wales continues to experience drought, with six English areas entering severe drought status this month alongside low river and reservoir levels. Trees with shallow root systems, such as birches, cannot access deeper groundwater when surface soil dries out, leaving them far more susceptible to early leaf loss than deep-rooted species like oaks.

RHS officials expect drought-impacted regions to experience an earlier, intense, but potentially short-lived display of autumn color. While trees that traditionally provide yellow foliage dropped their leaves early, varieties producing red, amber, and purple hues remain vibrant. Meanwhile, incoming weather presents additional dangers to vegetation. The Met Office issued warnings that strong winds from Storm Austen could damage buildings and fell trees, echoing September warnings from experts who noted that El Niño weather patterns could destroy trees weakened by drought.

To adapt to shifting climate patterns, staff at Wisley are adjusting their long-term selection of plants. Guy Barter, chief horticulturist at the RHS, indicated that future plantings will feature fewer birches and emphasize deeply rooted varieties that can handle both freezing winters and hot, dry summers, such as liquidambars, Lebanon cedars, and swamp cypresses. Tim Upson, the RHS director of gardens and horticulture, noted that trial plantings of Mediterranean and Californian oaks from three decades ago are currently thriving, suggesting that while species like beech and birch will survive, they may be confined to damp or sheltered environments in an evolving landscape.

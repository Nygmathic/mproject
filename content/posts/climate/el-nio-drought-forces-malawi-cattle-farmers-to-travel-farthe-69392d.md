In southern Malawi's Mulanje district, severe dry conditions linked to El Nino have forced cattle farmers and fodder gatherers to travel significantly farther to locate food for their animals. During the 2023/24 rainfall season, high temperatures and dry spells hit parts of the country, leading the Department of Climate Change and Meteorological Services to warn of below-normal rainfall and heightened drought risks in southern regions.

The lack of feed near farms has altered daily gathering routines. Workers such as 25-year-old Lawrence Abison now travel tens of kilometres from areas like Nsanjama to reach the Likhubula River, which flows from the 3,002-metre Mount Mulanje. Abison told Al Jazeera that grass has become difficult to find, forcing gatherers to begin their journeys months earlier in the year than usual. This search for fodder has increased competition with people living near the rivers, who rely on local vegetation to feed their own livestock and harvest reeds to make mats. Residents along the river, including 57-year-old Batson Kweluza, also report that tree clearing and vegetable farming along the banks have depleted plant life and made the river shallower.

For local cattle owners, the feed shortage is causing significant financial and operational strain. Macdonald Khandalawo, a 54-year-old farmer who keeps 10 cows in Nsanjama, said reduced and delayed feeding has lowered his herd's milk output. Meanwhile, alternative feed supplements like maize bran have become scarce and expensive due to diminished maize harvests. Khandalawo, who serves as treasurer for the 400-member New Thuchira Milk Bulking Group cooperative, stated that while he typically earns more than one million Malawi kwacha ($577) per month selling milk, much of his income is now swallowed by the costs of buying bran and paying gatherers. Other farmers, such as 25-year-old Alefa Saulo in Nkando, report that their cattle are losing weight while waiting for rain to restore pasture lands.

To cope with the ongoing feed deficits, members of the local milk cooperative have begun collecting crop remains, such as pea, rice, and maize stalks, to store for periods when grass is scarce. However, rising expenses and environmental changes along the rivers continue to reduce farmers' earnings across the region.

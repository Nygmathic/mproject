Severe droughts in northwestern Afghanistan are ruining local agricultural livelihoods and pushing displaced families to sell young daughters into child marriage to survive. In Badghis province, where 95 percent of residents depend on rain-fed farming and livestock, repeated climate-driven dry spells have eliminated regular sources of income. Major droughts hit the region in 2018, and crop failures alongside livestock starvation have recurred every year since 2021.

As a result, thousands of residents have fled their ancestral villages for the provincial capital of Qala-e-Naw or displaced persons camps such as Shahrak-e-Sabz in Herat. With humanitarian aid drying up in these settlements and local job options severely limited, parents are turning to extreme coping mechanisms to pay off accumulated debts, purchase basic food, or cover medical treatment for sick relatives.

Young girls, ranging from infants to pre-teens, are frequently engaged or married off to older men or boys. Parents express deep distress over these arrangements, noting that financial desperation leaves them with no alternative. The practice subjects young brides to psychological trauma, domestic abuse from in-laws, and early pregnancy upon reaching puberty, compounding health hazards in a country where maternal mortality is already among the world's highest. Fifteen-year-old Darellah, who is pregnant and married to a 30-year-old man, observed that "I am still a child" while reflecting on her situation.

The ongoing crisis has forced families into further severe measures. Some households survive on tea, homemade bread, and boiled wild mallow leaves, burning rubbish to make fires. Parents and young children gather discarded cans and bottles to resell for minimal daily earnings, or sell basic household belongings like lighting batteries to buy medications. In one extreme case inside the camp, a father who was unable to feed his family sold his 10-year-old son’s kidney for 100,000 afghanis. Meanwhile, cattle markets remain crowded with animals being sold because owners can no longer afford fodder, leaving several rural villages largely abandoned.

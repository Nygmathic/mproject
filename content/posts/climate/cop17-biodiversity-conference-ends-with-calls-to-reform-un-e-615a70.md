The Cop17 UN biodiversity conference in Yerevan, Armenia, concluded with mounting calls to reform international environmental negotiations, as countries remain on track to miss their current nature protection goals. Throughout the 34-year existence of the UN biodiversity convention, governments have never successfully fulfilled any of their targets.

During the summit, delegates committed to accelerating efforts toward targets established four years ago. These goals include protecting 30 percent of the planet, restoring roughly a third of damaged ecosystems, and reforming $500 billion in subsidies that harm the environment. Countries also reaffirmed a commitment to raise biodiversity funding to $30 billion annually by 2030. However, implementation is lagging significantly, with the United Kingdom on track to meet just three of its 23 goals.

Frustration with the process led several figures to demand major structural changes. Juan Carlos Navarro, the environment minister of Panama, argued that requiring total consensus allows single countries to stall negotiations, stating that the current summit format was "designed to fail." Navarro advocated for majority-based decision-making and suggested merging separate UN conferences on climate, nature, and plastics into one annual meeting. Inger Andersen, head of the UN Environment Programme, similarly observed that while existing agreements work, progress is overly slow because the global environmental agenda has become cluttered with overlapping conventions.

Disagreements between governments further hindered progress during the negotiations. Argentina, Russia, and Saudi Arabia pushed to dilute language regarding environmental ambitions in the final document. Meanwhile, European nations sought to expand the donor base for environmental funding, arguing that China and various Gulf states should contribute financially rather than remaining classified as developing countries.

Additionally, delegates delayed decisions on a mechanism designed to collect money from commercial users of digital genetic resources, such as pharmaceutical firms. The fund was envisioned to raise £1 billion per year, with much of the revenue directed to Indigenous groups, but it has generated just over $10,000 to date. Further action on the fund has been pushed to the next biodiversity conference in Ecuador in two years.

Addressing the challenges, UK nature envoy Ruth Davis acknowledged the frustration surrounding the summit, but highlighted ongoing global implementation efforts and emphasized that governments must make the process deliver tangible results for citizens facing environmental impacts.

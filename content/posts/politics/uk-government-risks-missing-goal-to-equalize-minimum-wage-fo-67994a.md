Chancellor John Healey has indicated that the government might not fulfill its commitment to equalize minimum wage rates for younger and older workers prior to the next election. Despite a 2024 Labour manifesto pledge to eliminate age-related pay tiers for individuals aged 18 to 20, the recent Budget preserved the wage gap by applying the same rate increase across all age brackets. As a result, minimum pay in 2026 is set to reach £11.27 an hour for 18 to 20-year-olds, compared to £13.21 for workers above age 20.

Speaking in a BBC interview broadcast on Sunday, Healey stated the government would move incrementally when asked if equal pay would be established before the election. He reiterated that Labour remains dedicated to the policy overall, but implied that implementation could be delayed to help businesses hire young people. Healey emphasized the need to balance pay rates with job creation to address what he called a "moral shame" involving one million young people without employment prospects. However, delaying the wage parity plan is expected to draw opposition from trade unions and left-wing members of the Labour Party.

Healey also expressed a willingness to enforce substantial spending reductions across several government departments to lower national debt. Budget documents indicate real-terms funding declines for specific sectors in the coming years. Although the Institute for Fiscal Studies questioned whether the government would proceed with these cuts, Healey maintained that hard decisions were necessary. He stated that preparations are underway for next year's spending review, where departmental reductions potentially exceeding two percent annually will be incorporated into a ten-year plan.

In the same broadcast, Conservative Party leader Kemi Badenoch argued that the government must curb spending, though she criticized the Budget's 98 individual measures as piecemeal and ineffective. Badenoch called for reducing the overall cost of government while safeguarding frontline services. She proposed that the primary spending reductions should come from the welfare budget, which she described as containing immense sums, alongside headcount reductions in the civil service and budget cuts for departments such as the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport.

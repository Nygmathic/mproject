Prime Minister Andy Burnham has indicated he is open to calls for the United Kingdom to formally apologize for its role in the transatlantic slave trade, while ruling out any financial reparations. Speaking ahead of an upcoming Commonwealth leaders summit in the Caribbean, Burnham proposed a structured dialogue with affected populations, emphasizing that Britain must confront its past. However, he stated that modern British citizens cannot be held accountable for historical injustices committed in a previous era under different laws.

Historically, English authorities and the monarchy were major participants in the slave trade starting in the 16th century. Although Parliament passed a law abolishing slavery in 1833, the government paid financial compensation to plantation owners for their lost property, incurring a debt that was only fully paid off in 2015. To date, the British state has never issued a formal apology or offered reparations. Burnham's remarks coincide with a Caribbean tour by King Charles III, who acknowledged past suffering during a stop in the Bahamas and visited the site of an enslaved rebellion against British authorities in Guyana.

Downing Street considers strengthening relations across the Commonwealth to be vital for national interests, especially as foreign powers like Russia and China seek greater global influence. This week, Russian President Vladimir Putin hosted a Russia-Africa summit in Moscow. Commenting on the government's willingness to engage in dialogue regarding an apology, a government source told the BBC that it was "morally the right thing to do."

The Prime Minister's statements drew mixed reactions. Bell Ribeiro-Addy, chair of the All-Party Parliamentary Group for Afrikan Reparations and MP for Clapham and Brixton Hill, welcomed the official recognition of Britain's historical role. However, speaking on BBC Radio 4's Today program, she noted that the government still fell short of offering a formal state apology. She stressed that reparative justice is not strictly financial, adding that no bill for monetary compensation had been presented to the UK.

Other international figures also expressed contrasting views. Prime Minister of Barbados Mia Mottley welcomed Burnham's statement, stating she would engage in good faith while continuing to pursue reparatory justice. Conversely, Denis O'Brien, founder of The Repair Campaign, described the government's position as disappointing, arguing that a full and formal apology is a necessary first step.

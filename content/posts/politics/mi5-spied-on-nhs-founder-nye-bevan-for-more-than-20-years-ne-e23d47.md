Newly released documents given to the National Archives reveal that Britain’s security service, MI5, monitored National Health Service founder Aneurin "Nye" Bevan for over two decades. The surveillance began in 1933 when Bevan was serving as a backbench Labour MP for Ebbw Vale. At the time, intelligence officials viewed the politician from Tredegar, Blaenau Gwent, as a fiery left-wing figure connected to prominent members of the communist movement.

Early reports in his file detailed a 1934 anti-Nazi rally that Bevan addressed in Montreal, Canada. A summary compiled in 1943 by Roger Hollis, who later became head of MI5, noted that a decade earlier Bevan had maintained close contact with Harry Pollitt, the general secretary of the Communist Party of Great Britain. Bevan supported an initiative to establish worker groups to protect against capitalists. Despite the ongoing monitoring, there is no record indicating that agents intercepted Bevan's mail or tapped his telephone calls, leading some within MI5 to question why the agency was watching him at all given that his public activities matched his speeches in Parliament.

The intelligence agency maintained its interest after the Second World War as Bevan joined Prime Minister Clement Attlee’s Labour cabinet as health minister, establishing the NHS despite resistance from doctors. Security concerns escalated in 1949 regarding Bevan’s friendship with Wilfred Macartney, who had been convicted of spying for Russia in 1928. Recorded conversations suggested Macartney was relaying information obtained from Bevan to Pollitt. In May 1949, MI5 chief Sir Percy Sillitoe presented this evidence to Attlee. Although the prime minister dismissed Macartney's claims as a "tissue of lies," he agreed to warn Bevan about the associate.

Surveillance persisted after Bevan returned to the opposition benches. A 1954 report described a meeting at the Polish embassy in London between Bevan and Soviet ambassador Yakov Malik, which ended poorly because the ambassador struggled to understand the Welsh politician’s accent. Following Bevan’s death in 1960, an MI5 officer described the file contents as unexpectedly mild. The security service did not officially close his file until 1975, fifteen years after his passing.

Chancellor John Healey is expected to reject calls to raise Capital Gains Tax in his forthcoming Budget on 28 October. The tax, which applies to profits from selling assets like shares and non-primary properties, is currently set at 18% for basic-rate taxpayers and 24% for higher and additional-rate taxpayers. Although some influential Labour figures advocated equalising these rates with income tax, Healey has indicated a desire to promote business growth and profit, maintaining that government decisions should foster investment and economic confidence.

Internal pressure to hike the levy previously came from Cabinet ministers Louise Haigh and Wes Streeting before Andy Burnham became prime minister in July. Haigh had argued that raising the tax would reduce reliance on taxing work, while Streeting supported a tax model that avoids penalising employment. However, warnings against the move emerged from figures such as former government adviser Lord Jim O'Neill. Furthermore, estimates from HMRC suggest that a 10% increase on the higher rate could diminish Treasury revenue by £3.6 billion by 2028–29 due to behavioral changes, such as asset owners moving abroad or delaying sales.

Healey faces severe fiscal constraints in preparing his initial Budget, complicated by self-imposed debt limits and manifesto promises not to raise income tax, VAT, or National Insurance. Against the backdrop of rising prices stemming from the US-Iran war, the chancellor is under pressure to assist with cost-of-living burdens and increase funding for defence in next year's spending review. To generate revenue, senior banking executives anticipate possible tax increases on the banking industry following reports of substantial profits.

Political opponents have offered differing approaches ahead of the announcement. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch urged the government to decrease borrowing and spending, highlighting her party's proposal to save £70 billion through cuts to welfare and foreign development aid. Conversely, Liberal Democrat deputy leader Daisy Cooper proposed raising £14 billion by taxing bank profits, suggesting the funds be used to lower fuel duty, public transport fares, and hospitality VAT. Meanwhile, Communities Secretary Angela Rayner declined to comment on specific Budget proposals, emphasising that the administration has already implemented measures to alleviate living costs.

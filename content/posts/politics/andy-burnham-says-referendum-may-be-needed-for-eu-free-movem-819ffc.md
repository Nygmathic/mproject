Prime Minister Andy Burnham has indicated that a public vote could be required before any return to freedom of movement with the European Union. Asked whether free movement could be introduced without a referendum, Burnham answered in the negative, though sources told the BBC that no definitive decision on holding a vote has been reached. The prime minister added that he would "never move ahead of the British public when it comes to Brexit," with sources indicating he is against rapidly restoring free movement.

Burnham has maintained his commitment to Labour's 2024 election manifesto, which ruled out rejoining the single market, customs union, or returning to free movement. Major alterations to the UK's relationship with the EU have been ruled out prior to the next general election, which is due by 2029. Burnham has nevertheless stated a desire to launch a conversation about prospective options around the time of next month's EU summit. These choices could range from keeping the current arrangement to rejoining the EU customs union, re-entering the single market, or holding a new referendum on full EU membership.

Speaking to the Sunday Times, Burnham welcomed a candid discussion on freedom of movement, which serves as a necessary condition for EU single market access by allowing citizens to travel, live, study, and work in member states. He observed that post-Brexit migration consists of more permanent arrivals, whereas pre-Brexit migration typically involved younger individuals staying temporarily. Burnham also contended that Brexit caused the UK to lose crucial immigration controls, pointing to the small boats crisis following the loss of a returns agreement with other nations. However, he stated he does not anticipate ever recommending that the UK join the euro currency or the Schengen passport-free travel area.

The debate over freedom of movement continues to divide political leaders, mirroring discussions during the 2016 Brexit vote. Opposing any return to free movement, Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch stated that most Brexit voters rejected the policy, while Reform UK leader Nigel Farage called such a move a betrayal. In contrast, the Liberal Democrats have argued that bringing back free movement would boost the UK's economic growth.

Defence Secretary Wes Streeting has stated that the United Kingdom opposes an agreement between Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin allowing the United States to purchase millions of tonnes of Russian diesel. Speaking to BBC Radio 4, Streeting explained that the deal occurred while the US president faces an upcoming November election and amid widespread international pressure on politicians to alleviate rising living costs. Trump's fuel agreement aimed to lower domestic energy prices, which surged following conflict involving the US, Israel, and Iran.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky warned that the deal would allow Moscow to generate revenue for purchasing new military hardware to sustain its invasion. Following those remarks, Trump suggested Ukraine choose a new leader who could negotiate an end to the conflict. Streeting countered that selecting leadership is a matter for Ukrainians, while praising Zelensky's character and leadership. Conservative shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith also rejected Trump's call for Zelensky to step down, stating that even close allies can disagree, and argued that the diesel agreement "sends out all the wrong messages" regarding global security.

Streeting asserted that sole accountability for the war lies with Putin and his imperialist ambitions. He acknowledged occasional public disagreements between Washington and Kyiv, noting that the UK aims to help reconcile its allies during such moments. Additionally, Streeting highlighted the significant scale of American backing for Ukraine, pointing to weapons procurement, intelligence support, and Washington-led diplomatic initiatives.

On domestic defence, Streeting stated the UK must plan its military spending around the risk of a potential Russian attack on the 32-member NATO alliance by 2030, arguing that strength provides the most effective deterrence. Former defence secretary John Healey, who resigned earlier this year over funding concerns and now serves as chancellor, will use the upcoming Budget to plug a £4.7 billion deficit in the Defence Investment Plan. The government also intends to present a strategy to raise defence spending to 3.5 percent of national income by 2035 during the next Spending Review. However, Streeting declined to pledge reaching 3 percent by 2030 without a clear financing plan, warning that unfunded promises would prompt financial markets to raise borrowing costs.

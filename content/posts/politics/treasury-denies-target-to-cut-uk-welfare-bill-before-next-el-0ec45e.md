The Treasury has disputed suggestions that Chancellor John Healey committed to reducing the UK benefits bill before the next general election. Speaking on BBC Radio 4’s Today programme, Healey stated that the government would lower welfare spending "in a Labour way" instead of relying on crude cuts while citizens face cost-of-living pressures. However, Treasury sources subsequently clarified that his comments should not be interpreted as a pledge to achieve reductions prior to the upcoming election, signaling that ministers have made no firm commitment to that timeframe.

Describing the current welfare framework as unsustainable, Healey highlighted a moral obligation to help nearly one million unemployed or disengaged young people transition into work or training. The government expects two major reports in November to guide long-term systemic changes: a disability benefits review co-chaired by Labour minister Sir Stephen Timms and an inquiry into young people not in education, employment, or training led by former minister Alan Milburn. Ministers plan to publish a formal response and policy document by the end of the year, followed by legislation next year. The government has also already pledged to alter the state pension triple lock starting in 2030.

According to forecasts from the Office for Budget Responsibility, total welfare spending is set to increase by £19 billion this year, bringing the total to £353 billion. Driven primarily by health-related support and pensioner benefits, overall expenditure is expected to reach £431 billion by 2031/32. This future projection includes £209.8 billion for pensioners, £110.7 billion for universal credit and legacy benefits, and £69.6 billion for disability payments.

The chancellor's statements followed criticism from opposition figures regarding the recent Budget. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch claimed the government was placing welfare first and allowing costs to spiral out of control. Reform UK stated the Budget failed to address expanding benefit spending, while Liberal Democrat leader Sir Ed Davey argued that welfare reform must focus on providing care and assistance to guide individuals back into employment. Additionally, Helen Miller, director at the Institute for Fiscal Studies, observed that the government still faces numerous tough decisions after leaving key policy areas unresolved.

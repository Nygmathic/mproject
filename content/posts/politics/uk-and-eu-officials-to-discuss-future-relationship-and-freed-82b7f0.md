British and European officials are set to hold discussions regarding potential future options for the UK's relationship with the EU, including freedom of movement. Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds informed the BBC that these conversations will take place before an upcoming EU summit on November 20. Downing Street has maintained that the government’s immediate focus for the summit remains on establishing practical agreements in areas such as food, youth mobility, emissions trading, and electricity.

Reynolds explained that no assumptions have been made about what the long-term relationship will look like, adding that the prime minister will outline trade-offs and options following the summit. However, any major policy changes would require an electoral mandate at a general election, which must occur by 2029. The government remains committed to Labour’s 2024 manifesto promises, which preclude returning to the single market, customs union, or free movement during the current parliament. Under EU rules, free movement is a mandatory condition for participating in the single market, allowing citizens to move, study, work, and reside across member states.

The upcoming discussions follow calls from Andy Burnham for an open dialogue regarding the return of freedom of movement. Burnham recently expressed opposition to adopting the euro or entering the Schengen area, but left open the possibility of a future referendum on free movement. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner also told the BBC that no long-term options should be ruled out. While Burnham’s position is seen as appealing to voters considering the Greens or Liberal Democrats, it has raised concern among Labour MPs in Brexit-leaning constituencies. MP Melanie Onn warned that discussing proposals beyond the manifesto risks damaging hard-earned voter trust.

Confusion arose after the prime minister told reporters he would not reintroduce free movement without a referendum, though Downing Street later clarified that no decision on a public vote has been made. Observers in Westminster view the prime minister’s stance as an indication that free movement could remain a viable long-term option. Meanwhile, Reform UK leader Nigel Farage publicly rejected Burnham’s involvement, stating he lacks a mandate to negotiate in Brussels and demanding that any fundamental changes be put to voters in a general election.

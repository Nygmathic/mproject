UK inflation and government borrowing both surged to one-year highs in September, intensifying pressure on Chancellor John Healey just a week before he presents his first Budget on October 28.

Data from the Office for National Statistics shows annual consumer price growth accelerated from 3.1 percent in August to 3.5 percent in September, marking its highest point since October 2025. ONS chief economist Grant Fitzner attributed the increase primarily to rising costs for motor fuels, alongside price gains for live events, air travel, and computer games. Fuel prices jumped 29.8 percent overall, pushed up by international conflicts disrupting refining capacity and crude oil shipping routes. Average petrol rose to 169.5p per litre—the highest level since August 2022—while diesel reached 192.5p per litre.

Alongside accelerating price increases, public borrowing rose to £24.5 billion in September, up £1.8 billion from a year earlier as elevated inflation increased debt servicing costs. Consequently, government borrowing for the current financial year is £6 billion higher than projected by the Office for Budget Responsibility. While total public debt surpassed £3 trillion for the first time, debt relative to the nation's total economic output fell.

The September inflation rate will also determine mandatory adjustments to welfare spending next April. Under the triple lock, the state pension is expected to increase by 3.9 percent in line with earlier wage growth, while statutory benefits such as carer's allowance and personal independence payments must rise at the rate of September inflation.

Addressing the figures, Healey reaffirmed Labour's commitment not to raise VAT, income tax, or National Insurance, promising that his Budget will promote economic growth within self-imposed fiscal rules. However, Shadow Chancellor Andrew Griffith claimed the government had "lost control of the economy" and called for spending cuts across benefits, red tape, and net zero initiatives. Ruth Gregory, deputy chief UK economist at Capital Economics, stated that the borrowing data reflects difficult fiscal conditions, suggesting the Chancellor will deliver a cautious Budget that postpones major fiscal decisions to next year.

English regional mayors will be permitted to distribute direct cash grants to local residents and businesses using their upcoming shares of centrally collected tax revenues. Chancellor John Healey recently unveiled plans in his Budget to allow regional authorities to keep a portion of locally generated basic-rate income tax and business rates. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has supported giving local leaders expanded spending control to help stimulate regional economic growth and improve public services.

Conservative Tees Valley Mayor Ben Houchen told the BBC that Treasury officials confirmed mayors possess the spending flexibility to offer financial grants to local residents. Lord Houchen is developing a voluntary scheme allowing citizens to apply for a portion of the tax funding distributed to his area, calling it a tax rebate scheme. However, because His Majesty's Revenue and Customs is the only UK authority empowered to issue income tax rebates, the Treasury considers these payments to be grants. According to BBC News, any mayor opting to distribute funds this way will have less revenue available for other local priorities, including housing and transport.

Currently, England's 14 strategic authorities rely mostly on ring-fenced grants designated by central government for specific policy areas. Under the government's reformed funding framework, mayors will receive a 20% share of locally retained business rates starting next year, serving as an initial step toward total retention before the end of the decade. Beginning in April 2028, mayors will be assigned customized allocations of basic-rate income tax collected in their regions. While central government will not devolve the power to set tax rates or thresholds, mayors will gain broad control over where to direct the funding.

From 2028, strategic authorities will also be allowed to establish an uncapped Overnight Visitor Levy on local accommodations. Ten Labour mayors have agreed to voluntarily cap this charge at 5%, though Lord Houchen indicated he will not implement the levy. Meanwhile, sources close to Reform UK mayors Andrea Jenkyns and Luke Campbell stated they are awaiting further details to understand how to utilize the tax revenues.

Proponents of devolution point to OECD data showing the UK collects only 5.8% of national taxes locally—the lowest share in the G7. Conversely, critics such as the Centre for Cities think tank warn that some areas may lack the expertise needed to manage devolved powers, potentially widening economic inequalities. Additionally, the Tees Valley Combined Authority faces a central government inspection after Local Government Minister Jim McMahon raised concerns regarding its financial sustainability.

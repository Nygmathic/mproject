British Prime Minister Andy Burnham has joined the King in Antigua for the formal opening of the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting, launching discussions with international leaders regarding a potential formal apology from the UK for the transatlantic slave trade. In conjunction with these talks, Downing Street stated that Burnham will present plans for a "debt reset" designed to assist member nations facing rising interest payments on their national debt.

The issue of historical reparations has featured heavily in the run-up to the summit, which convenes delegates from 56 Commonwealth countries. The current leadership is attempting to alter the UK's approach following the previous gathering held two years ago on the Pacific island of Samoa, where former Prime Minister Sir Keir Starmer rejected placing reparations on the agenda despite requests from fellow leaders. Writing in the Guardian, Burnham stated that avoiding open dialogue about the ongoing impacts of slavery had previously compromised the functioning of the Commonwealth.

Burnham, alongside Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband and former Prime Minister Gordon Brown, is seeking to lower tensions surrounding the debate. UK officials maintain that while Britain will not offer financial compensation to victims' descendants, the government is open to issuing its first formal apology next year during its presidency of the G20. Miliband noted that an apology would mark a significant step, while maintaining that asking modern taxpayers to cover financial reparations for historical acts is neither feasible nor appropriate. 

The proposed apology has faced criticism domestically. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch strongly advised against the move, warning that issuing an apology would serve as leverage for countries demanding financial payouts. 

Despite the disagreement, British diplomats remain hopeful that Burnham and Miliband's strategy will foster productive discussions over the summit's two days. Downing Street confirmed the UK will lead two task forces focusing on trade and investment alongside critical mineral supply chains, both set to form by the end of the year and report at the next summit. UK officials consider the Commonwealth a vital platform for engaging developing nations without the involvement of China, Russia, or the United States.

The first hurricane of the Atlantic season, Isaias, is strengthening and expected to briefly become a major category three storm. Early Friday morning, the system reached category two intensity with sustained wind speeds of 104 mph (170 km/h). According to estimates from the National Hurricane Center, the storm is anticipated to reach wind speeds of up to 115 mph (185 km/h) by Friday afternoon before weakening slightly ahead of landfall.

Landfall is expected late Friday night or early Saturday morning along the Gulf Coast, impacting Alabama and the Florida Panhandle. After making landfall, Isaias will move north through Alabama and brush eastern Missouri on Saturday. Although the storm is expected to quickly weaken into a tropical storm as it moves inland and loses contact with warm ocean water, it will still generate hazardous winds, severe rainfall, and potential flooding across the southeastern United States, extending north into the Tennessee Valley and the Carolinas.

Forecasters warn of a dangerous storm surge of up to six feet in parts of Alabama and Florida, accompanied by heavy waves and strong winds, particularly around high tide. Rainfall totals between 4 and 8 inches are expected across southern Alabama, southwest Georgia, and the Florida Panhandle and Big Bend regions, with localized amounts reaching up to 15 inches. This precipitation carries a risk of flash flooding in urban areas and isolated river flooding.

In response to the approaching storm, the National Weather Service has issued Hurricane and Storm Surge Watches along the Gulf Coast, while Florida Governor Ron DeSantis declared a state of emergency across 25 counties. The storm has forced the cancellation or rescheduling of several major regional sporting events. However, a high-profile football match between the University of Georgia and the University of Alabama in Tuscaloosa, which President Donald Trump is scheduled to attend, remains expected to proceed on Saturday.

Isaias brings an end to a record-quiet start to the 2026 Atlantic hurricane season. Forecasters had previously predicted a below-average season because a strong El Niño pattern in the Pacific Ocean typically creates wind shear that dismantles developing storms in the Atlantic and Caribbean. However, Isaias managed to form in a region featuring limited wind shear and exceptionally warm ocean temperatures between 30 and 31 degrees Celsius. As the storm continues moving northward, forecasters expect it to encounter higher wind shear, which should limit further intensification over the coming days.

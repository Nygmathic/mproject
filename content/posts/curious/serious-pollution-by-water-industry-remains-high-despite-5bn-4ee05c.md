A joint assessment by regulators has revealed that the environmental performance of water companies in England and Wales worsened last year, despite £5 billion invested in the sector over the previous five years. According to the first combined report from four regulatory bodies, eight out of nine English water providers were judged to be poor or requiring improvement, leaving Severn Trent as the only company to achieve the top rating. Although companies made progress in reducing service interruptions, serious environmental pollution remained a "major concern."

Total environmental ratings for English providers reached a record low of 16 out of 36 stars. The Environment Agency attributed this decline to both poorer performance in certain areas and the introduction of stricter evaluation standards. In Wales, Natural Resources Wales awarded Dŵr Cymru Welsh Water two stars out of four. Environment Agency chair Alan Lovell told BBC News he was deeply disappointed, emphasizing that several companies failed to carry out the environmental schemes funded by recent investments. Environment Secretary Dame Angela Eagle indicated she is seeking answers from failing providers and plans to highlight the most severe violations nationwide.

Defending the sector, industry group Water UK noted that more than half of all pollution incidents resulted from blockages or vandalism outside the direct control of providers. While companies have committed to record investments through 2030 to upgrade networks, average household bills are projected to increase by 36 percent. Consumer Council for Water chief executive Mike Keil observed that bill payers are questioning if their money is being used wisely, particularly as household complaints against water companies surged 84 percent in a single year.

In response to the sector's systemic issues, the government intends to introduce legislation next year to merge English regulators into a single entity. Prime Minister Andy Burnham previously announced that a strengthened Water Bill would lift restrictions on government share ownership in major English water companies and grant new oversight authority to regional leadership.

The joint report also disclosed that daily household water consumption remained unchanged at 137 litres per person in 2025. Amid ongoing drought conditions that have led to widespread hosepipe bans, Mark Owen of the Angling Trust criticized the industry for failing to minimize water waste through leakage, arguing that lost water directly harms vulnerable rivers and wildlife.

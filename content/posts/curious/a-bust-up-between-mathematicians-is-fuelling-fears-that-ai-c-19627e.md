A recent dispute between researchers and artificial intelligence developers has highlighted growing anxieties over the role of AI in scientific research. OpenAI recently announced that its AI agents resolved a longstanding mathematical problem related to the Navier–Stokes equations in 88 hours. However, Tristan Buckmaster, a mathematician at New York University, raised concerns prior to the announcement. Buckmaster and his collaborator, Levent Alpöge of Anthropic, had been working toward the same objective while storing drafts inside OpenAI’s coding tool, Codex. After learning that news of their progress had reached OpenAI, Buckmaster publicly questioned whether their stored work fed into the tech firm's achievements. OpenAI maintained that no personnel or AI accessed the team's data, eventually stating that the team's prompts could not have influenced the model.

This controversy underscores broader fears that artificial intelligence could undermine trust in the scientific process by raising doubts about the accuracy and originality of research. Beyond intellectual property disputes, AI chatbots like Claude and Gemini are known to hallucinate false information. Experts fear that as these distortions creep into scientific literature, separating authentic discoveries from AI-generated fiction will become increasingly challenging. Furthermore, scientific journals are currently struggling to cope with a surge of AI-generated errors. Science journalist Dalmeet Singh Chawla told BBC News that publishers' integrity staff need to drastically increase their screening efforts to keep up with the problem.

Despite these risks, AI is widely viewed as a crucial tool for analyzing massive volumes of data collected by telescopes and particle accelerators. In biology, Google DeepMind used its AlphaFold system to predict 200 million protein shapes in one year, an achievement that DeepMind’s Dr. Pushmeet Kohli characterized as a "watershed moment." Elsewhere, Dr. Oliver Vince co-founded Basecamp Research to collect vast genetic datasets aimed at training an AI to comprehend biological structures. However, researchers warn that the technology is also prone to hype. Some computer models exaggerate the significance of their discoveries, leading human supervisors to accept findings without sufficient skepticism.

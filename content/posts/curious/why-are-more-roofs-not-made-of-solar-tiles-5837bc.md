Solar roofs, which integrate solar cells into tiles that resemble standard roofing materials, offer an alternative for homeowners disliking mounted panels and for historic buildings bound by appearance restrictions. However, the industry faces substantial hurdles. Tesla recently removed solar roofs from its website following disappointing sales and a lawsuit over price hikes. While this move disrupted the sector, some smaller firms view it as an opportunity. Swiss manufacturer Freesuns, which reports sales growth of 20 to 30 percent since 2019, is considering expanding into the United States.

A major barrier to adoption is cost, as a solar roof can cost twice as much as a standard mounted panel system. Because components are produced in smaller batches and each roof requires customized engineering, the industry struggles to achieve standardization. Installation requires coordination among roofers, solar installers, and electricians while managing complex building regulations, creating lengthy delays that burden roofing businesses. Maintenance is also more expensive; solar shingles lack room for monitoring electronics, and technicians walking across tiles during repairs risk causing micro-fractures.

Performance limitations further complicate adoption. Solar tiles are less efficient than standard panels because their smaller size requires more surface area to match power output. Sitting close to the roof reduces airflow, generating heat that hinders performance, while non-black colored tiles absorb light less effectively. Ben Graves, co-founder of United Kingdom solar company Sunsave, notes that most consumers prioritize savings over aesthetics and cannot afford solar roofs.

Despite these obstacles, supporters like Martin Heinrich of the Fraunhofer Institute argue that solar roofs should be evaluated as generating additional energy from roof space rather than directly compared to conventional systems. Innovations such as matrix shingling technology—which layers narrow solar cells using conductive adhesive rather than metal wires—can bypass shaded spots, improve appearance, and fit custom shapes. Combined with potential automated design software to streamline planning, these advancements could lower costs and encourage broader adoption across the construction industry.

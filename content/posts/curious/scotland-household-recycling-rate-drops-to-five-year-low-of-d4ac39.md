Data released by the Scottish Environment Protection Agency (Sepa) reveals that Scotland's household recycling rate dropped to 42.8% in 2025, down from 44.3% the previous year. This marks the lowest recycling level recorded in five years and falls significantly short of the Scottish government's 2010 target, which aimed for a 70% recycling rate by last year. Over the past decade, recycling levels across the nation have consistently hovered around 45%.

In total, Scottish households generated 2.31 million tonnes of waste in 2025, representing a minor decrease of 1,500 tonnes from the prior year. Recycling performance varied dramatically across regions. South Ayrshire led local authorities with a 55% recycling rate, whereas Glasgow registered just 26%. Island communities posted the lowest figures overall, with Orkney recording a rate of only 18.2%.

While the proportion of waste sent to incinerators remained steady at 82%, waste directed to landfills dropped by 10%. The most substantial operational shift involves black bag waste, which is increasingly routed to energy-from-waste facilities that produce electricity by burning rubbish. However, this infrastructure expansion has proceeded too slowly to meet a planned ban on landfill waste, effectively pushing the deadline back to 2028. Additionally, the shutdown of the Polmadie facility in Glasgow for long-term repairs in April led to waste being rerouted to alternative incinerators or landfills. Operated by Viridor alongside another site in Dunbar, the Polmadie closure is not reflected in the 2025 figures. Karen MacCormick of Viridor defended waste burning as a greener, lower-carbon alternative to landfilling, which produces methane and other gases.

Progress continues to face obstacles, including repeated delays to a bottle and can deposit return scheme that has successfully raised recycling rates elsewhere. Experts note that inconsistent local authority practices—such as some councils collecting glass at the kerbside while others require trips to bottle banks—cause confusion for residents. Research from Zero Waste Scotland shows that over half the contents placed in black bins could actually be recycled. Addressing the findings, Sepa chief scientific officer Dr Richard Allan described waste management as an "ongoing challenge" and emphasized the need to prioritize waste prevention alongside higher recycling rates.

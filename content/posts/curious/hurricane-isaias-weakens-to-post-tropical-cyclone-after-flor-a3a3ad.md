After making landfall along Florida's northern Gulf Coast with intense winds and heavy rainfall, Hurricane Isaias has weakened into a post-tropical cyclone. As the initial hurricane of the 2026 Atlantic season, Isaias represents the first time in more than 30 years that a hurricane failed to form before October, a delay linked to El Niño conditions. The National Weather Service reports that the system is traveling north and is expected to cross Alabama into the Tennessee Valley over the weekend.

Although the National Hurricane Center stated that all coastal warnings were discontinued on Saturday morning, significant power disruptions and hazards remain. More than 400,000 utility customers in Florida, over 300,000 in Alabama, and at least 100,000 in Georgia are without power. Emergency states have been declared across all three states to manage potential flooding. Authorities in Florida and Alabama previously implemented mandatory evacuations for threatened coastal communities and visitors, while Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin confirmed that federal emergency declarations were approved by the U.S. president to provide support.

The weather conditions caused disruptions to local businesses and public events. The restaurant chain Waffle House closed multiple locations along the storm's route. Additionally, U.S. President Donald Trump altered his plans, canceling attendance at a college football game in Tuscaloosa, Alabama, to go to a rally in Tennessee instead. In coastal areas like Pensacola, Florida, some residents chose to remain in their homes, taking measures to secure property and livestock against the weather.

Isaias is one of three major weather systems currently impacting North America due to warm, El Niño-influenced Pacific waters. On Mexico's Pacific coast, Hurricane Simon is expected to intensify from a category one to a category four storm before reaching landfall near Puerto Vallarta on Sunday. Meanwhile, Tropical Storm Rachel, a downgraded hurricane, is moving toward the border region between the United States and Mexico. Ocean swells and high tides associated with Rachel have already submerged streets in Newport Beach, California, where crews used bulldozers on the shoreline to help prevent further flooding.

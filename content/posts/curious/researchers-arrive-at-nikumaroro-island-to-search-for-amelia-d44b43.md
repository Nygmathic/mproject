A team of researchers from Purdue University and the Archaeological Legacy Institute has arrived at Nikumaroro Island to examine a target that could be the wreckage of Amelia Earhart’s airplane. The famed aviator vanished alongside navigator Fred Noonan in 1937 while attempting to fly around the globe. Following a five-day, 830-nautical-mile journey across the South Pacific aboard the ship Marin Mainiku, the expedition reached the Kiribati island on Monday. The mission represents the first officially approved trip to Nikumaroro in seven years and the first expedition to the island's Taraia Peninsula in more than fifteen years.

Led by Rick Pettigrew, the team plans to spend five days investigating the "Taraia Object," an anomaly on the edge of a lagoon initially identified in 2020 satellite photographs. While some believe the site holds plane debris, skeptics contend it could merely be a fallen tree. The crew intends to enter the island through a reef channel created by the British in 1963. Initial work involves clearing jungle vegetation and transporting equipment, followed by scans for magnetic metals on Wednesday. Researchers will subsequently use dredging machinery to clear sediment around the object, which rests in shallow water just ten feet from the shoreline.

The search effort faces severe environmental obstacles, including the strongest El Niño event on record. Marine archaeologist Michael Krivor warned that moving weather patterns could impair passage through the narrow reef channel. Additionally, safety concerns regarding giant coconut crabs will force the researchers to spend their nights aboard the Marin Mainiku. These massive nocturnal crustaceans, which are the largest land-dwelling invertebrates on Earth, can grow leg spans of up to one meter and hunt small mammals. Some historical theories even suggest Earhart may have crashed on the island before being consumed by the crabs.

Earhart had completed legs of her journey across South America, Africa, and Asia before departing Lae, Papua New Guinea, on July 2, 1937. Her twin-engine Electra aircraft vanished while attempting to reach Howland Island to refuel. Official assessments suggest the plane suffered communication failures, ran out of fuel, and crashed into the ocean, though the mystery has generated widespread speculation for nearly nine decades.

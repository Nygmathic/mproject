An environmental project has cleared an invasive plant species from a significant stretch of the River Alderbourne in Buckinghamshire. Removal efforts focused on American skunk cabbage, a non-native plant originally indigenous to the wetland environments and swamps of the Pacific Northwest. The species is widely recognized for its strong, unpleasant odor, which BBC Gardeners' World Magazine has characterized as resembling "rotting meat."

The clearance initiative covered a section of the river spanning more than 2.5 kilometers, equivalent to approximately 1.6 miles. Groundwork South, an environmental charity that spearheaded the project, received £9,900 in funding from Affinity Water's INNS Out Scheme to carry out the necessary work.

As part of the operation, specialists from the charity carried out detailed surveys and treated several targeted locations along the river's course. One of the primary sites included in the treatment process was located within the Harding's Row Nature Reserve, which is situated near Iver Heath.

Following the completion of the treatment process, Groundwork South conducted additional surveys to monitor the site. According to reports from the charity, these post-treatment evaluations demonstrated encouraging early results in suppressing the invasive species.

Matt Mountain, a senior project manager representing Groundwork, noted that the financial assistance provided through Affinity Water's scheme played a vital role in the initiative's success. Mountain explained that receiving this support allowed the project team to confront the relatively new invasive plant before its growth reached a stage where complete eradication would become impractical or unmanageable.

Furthermore, Mountain emphasized that the successful intervention provides a significant benefit to local and regional wildlife, as well as the broader environmental ecosystem. He highlighted that these conservation efforts are particularly crucial in the Colne catchment area, given that the threatened waterways in this region are considered globally significant chalk streams.

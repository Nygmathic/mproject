South Africans are casting ballots in highly contested local elections to choose leaders for the country's municipalities, amidst predictions that the governing African National Congress may suffer a significant loss of support. The voting process will determine local leadership across nearly 300 municipal councils responsible for delivering essential public services, including water, electricity, and refuse collection.

The election comes as public dissatisfaction mounts over persistent corruption, high unemployment, widespread crime, and deteriorating infrastructure. The governing party, which reached a peak of 70 percent support in the 2004 general election, saw its share fall to 40 percent two decades later after losing its parliamentary majority for the first time since the end of apartheid in 1994. An Ipsos poll indicates the party's backing could drop further to 30 percent, down from 46 percent in the 2021 municipal vote. Political analyst Melanie Verwoerd told BBC News that the election represents a verdict on the party, anticipating its weakest result to date. President Cyril Ramaphosa, speaking at a rally prior to the vote, rejected assertions of a downward spiral and expressed confidence in his party's prospects.

BBC News visited areas in Gauteng province, such as the townships of Alexandra and Soweto, where infrastructure neglect and voter apathy are increasingly evident. Residents described daily challenges, including uncollected rubbish, raw sewage, regular water outages, and crumbling roads gutted by giant potholes. While some citizens choose not to participate due to a lack of progress, others view the ballot as a mechanism to address local governance failures and demand accountability from leadership.

Financial administration across South Africa's 257 municipalities remains severely compromised. A recent audit report showed that only 39 public entities achieved clean audits for the 2024–2025 period. None of Gauteng's three major metropolitan areas—Johannesburg, Ekurhuleni, and Tshwane—received a clean rating. Auditor-General Tsakani Maluleke warned lawmakers that ongoing financial deterioration threatens service delivery, highlighting Johannesburg, which has been described as "effectively bankrupt" amidst persistent utility instability and unmaintained streets.

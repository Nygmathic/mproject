A 40-year-old Kenyan businessman has died in Nairobi after contracting Ebola and travelling through three African countries without being detected. The man, who had lived in the Democratic Republic of Congo for seven years, passed away on October 5, two days after arriving in Kenya. His illness marks the first confirmed Ebola case in Kenya and has raised serious biosecurity questions across the region.

Reconstructions by Dr. Wessam Mankoula, emergency director for the Africa CDC, detail the patient's six-week journey. His symptoms first appeared around September 15 following business travel across multiple provinces in DR Congo, including Bas-Uélé. Health workers initially treated him with antibiotics and later misdiagnosed his fever and rash as a skin infection at a health centre in Bondo, failing to administer an Ebola test. On September 20, he flew to Kisangani, spending over ten days seeking medical care at unknown clinics.

The businessman flew to Beni on October 1 and travelled through Kasindi—a region severely affected by the outbreak—before crossing into Uganda. He then travelled by road to Kampala and proceeded to Entebbe International Airport. Ugandan officials noted that his temperature appeared normal during airport checks, though experts suggest fever-reducing drugs or an absence of fever at that moment allowed him to pass. On October 3, he flew to Nairobi, passed through Jomo Kenyatta International Airport unnoticed, and was driven to Nairobi Hospital by a family member.

At the private hospital, laboratory testing confirmed he was infected with the Bundibugyo Ebola strain, the same virus driving an outbreak in DR Congo that has claimed over 4,000 lives this year. The patient suffered from severe symptoms, including fever, sore throat, and bleeding, before dying in isolation. Kenyan authorities have traced 66 contacts, with the World Health Organization expecting that figure to grow as contact tracing continues in Uganda and DR Congo. Although Kenya’s health ministry states that nearly 5,000 health workers have been trained and over 652,000 travellers screened, the handling of the patient has sparked safety concerns among some local healthcare staff.

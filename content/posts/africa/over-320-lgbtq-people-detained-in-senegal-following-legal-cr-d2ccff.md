A recent report published by Human Rights Watch outlines a worsening crackdown against LGBTQ+ people in Senegal, involving mass detentions, mandatory HIV screenings, and restricted access to legal representation. According to data from the HIV Justice Network cited in the report, over 320 individuals have been taken into custody since February on allegations of committing "acts against nature." Among those detained, at least 84 faced charges of voluntary HIV transmission based exclusively on their HIV status.

The heightened enforcement comes after parliament modified the national penal code on March 11, doubling the maximum prison sentence for consensual same-sex relations to 10 years. Under the updated law, individuals who support, finance, or promote LGBTQ+ groups or people face up to seven years in prison and fines between 500,000 and 5 million CFA francs. Although same-sex sexual activity has been outlawed in Senegal since 1965, societal opposition has intensified. During the 2024 presidential campaign, candidate Bassirou Diomaye Faye—who won the election—and former Prime Minister Ousmane Sonko vowed to enact tougher anti-homosexuality measures as a priority for the nation.

Nationwide arrests have relied on information extracted from confiscated mobile devices, reports from third parties, and searches of private residences. According to the report, authorities breached confidentiality by sharing investigation details and personal health records with the media, exposing suspects to community rejection, threats, and physical harm. The legal changes have created a deterrent effect, leaving lawyers hesitant to offer legal defense due to fears of personal prosecution. Healthcare providers report that citizens are increasingly avoiding HIV testing and treatment. Sylvie Beaumont of the HIV Justice Network noted reduced attendance at health centers and described the HIV transmission charges as nonsensical, pointing out that many of those charged were on virus-suppressing therapy, carried condoms, or only discovered their infection upon being detained.

Human Rights Watch noted that the actions violate guarantees to non-discrimination, fair trials, and privacy under domestic and international law. Affected individuals report severe anxiety and depression, with many choosing to restrict their movements, seek refuge in hiding, or leave Senegal altogether. The situation reflects a broader regional movement toward harsher anti-LGBTQ+ policies across sub-Saharan Africa, including newly enacted criminal prohibitions and severe penalties in countries such as Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Uganda.

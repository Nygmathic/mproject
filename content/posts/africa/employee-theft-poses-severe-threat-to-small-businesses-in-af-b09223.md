Employee theft is posing a severe threat to small businesses across Africa, where a small number of workers frequently oversee payments, inventory, and supplier transactions with limited supervision. Loss-prevention firm Hipora Business Solutions East Africa estimates that internal theft accounts for over 60 percent of losses suffered by small enterprises. Additionally, a 2022 survey by consultancy firm PwC indicated that 63 percent of East African respondents experienced economic crime, compared to 46 percent globally.

The scale of the issue drew public attention after Kenyan creator That Girl Boina recounted how internal dishonesty forced her Nairobi beauty parlour, Waxaholic Studio, to shut down. Opened in September 2024, the business began losing money, and Boina later learned while traveling abroad that staff were directing client payments to an unauthorized phone number. She chose to close the business in October 2025. Her social media video about the ordeal went viral, triggering widespread responses from other Kenyan business owners who reported losing money, inventory, and equipment to staff fraud.

Internal theft also affects large-scale operations and supply networks. Major East African supermarket chains Tuskys and Nakumatt collapsed this decade, with operational troubles linked in part to employee dishonesty. According to Business Daily reporting on a 2015 audit, Tuskys lost more than 100 million Kenya shillings monthly due to cashiers pocketing money, till manipulation, and supplier collusion. Similarly, Nakumatt recorded over $100 million in missing stock.

Fraud can also manifest higher up the supply chain. Cris Njoki, founder of the Kenyan clothing brand IKOJN, told BBC News that an employee conspired with suppliers to inflate raw material prices and split the difference. Njoki confronted the worker, who then left, leading the business to centralize its procurement. Elsewhere, in Accra, Ghana, electronics shop owner Muftawu Nabila nearly closed his business after suffering repeated thefts from multiple employees over four years.

Hipora chief executive John Wanjohi told BBC News that workers frequently act "like a cartel that colludes with clients and suppliers" to carry out theft, making the practice increasingly difficult for business owners to detect.

Tanzania has unveiled its first natural gas discovery in a decade, marking a significant development for the East African nation's energy sector. Located in the Mnazi Bay area in the southern part of the country, the newly identified field is estimated to contain 223 billion cubic feet, or 6.3 billion cubic metres, of natural gas. Energy Minister Juma Homera attended a ceremony at the Kasa-1X ST2 well to observe the occasion. The site is jointly owned by the state-owned Tanzania Petroleum Development Corporation and the French oil company Maurel & Prom.

Homera described the discovery as a "major milestone" in advancing the country's small oil and gas industry. Production at the new well is anticipated to start early next month, aimed at enhancing local domestic energy supplies. Homera noted that the find is crucial for ensuring that the nation maintains a dependable, clean, and safe energy supply at affordable prices, particularly as economic activities and industrial expansion continue to elevate energy demand.

This latest find adds to Tanzania's pre-existing natural gas reserves, which total 57.54 trillion cubic feet. According to government data from 2024, natural gas accounts for nearly 80 percent of the electricity generated across the nation. In recent years, the government has also encouraged the adoption of compressed natural gas for motor vehicles. Processing plants and filling stations have been established primarily in Dar es Salaam, the country's largest city, leading some citizens to convert their vehicles to dual-fuel systems to take advantage of prices lower than petrol.

The Mnazi Bay discovery builds on an energy exploration history dating back to 1974, when Tanzania made its initial natural gas find on Songo Songo Island in the southeastern Lindi region. A second discovery occurred in 1982 at Mnazi Bay in the neighboring Mtwara region. The commercial development of these resources began in 2004, spurring further exploration both onshore and offshore. The country's largest single natural gas discovery to date occurred in 2016, when explorers uncovered 2.7 trillion cubic feet of gas.

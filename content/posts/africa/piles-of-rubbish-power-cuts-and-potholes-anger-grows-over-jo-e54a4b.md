As South Africa prepares for local elections on 4 November, public frustration over failing infrastructure and deteriorating municipal services has taken center stage. Across the nation's 257 municipalities, up to 29 million registered voters face widespread disruptions, including frequent power outages, severe water cuts, uncollected waste, and deteriorating roadways. The severity of the crisis was acknowledged by President Cyril Ramaphosa, who admitted that many local authorities are failing to meet public expectations. Financial records highlight the systemic nature of these issues; only 15% of local governments received clean audits during the 2024-25 fiscal year, while two-thirds are classified as financially distressed.

The breakdown of essential services is particularly pronounced in Johannesburg, South Africa's commercial capital and largest city. Political instability has seen the city governed by a succession of unstable coalitions, resulting in 10 different mayors over the last decade. Municipal authorities face a 220 billion rand infrastructure spending backlog, while Johannesburg Water loses an estimated 25% of its supply to leaks. In Alexandra township, uncollected waste piles up and polluted rivers run through the area, while residents in suburbs such as Melville and Westdene endure prolonged water outages. During a public meeting in Rabie Ridge, approximately 400 residents confronted Mayor Dada Morero over long-term blackouts and exorbitant electricity bills. In response, Morero defended the city's performance, attributing power cuts to criminal syndicates, vandalism, and a collapsed substation.

However, independent analysts and research bodies point to deeper internal governance failures. A report by the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime warned that municipal mismanagement has created a "criminal ecosystem," while former municipal official Rashid Seedat highlighted endemic corruption spanning low-level bribes to large tenders. This growing dissatisfaction has energized political opposition ahead of the vote. The Democratic Alliance, led by former Cape Town mayor Helen Zille, has mounted an energetic campaign focusing on infrastructure failures. Recent October polling places the party ahead of the ruling African National Congress, which faces internal factionalism. Nevertheless, analysts note that complex coalition dynamics across the broader Gauteng province may ultimately decide control of the municipal government.

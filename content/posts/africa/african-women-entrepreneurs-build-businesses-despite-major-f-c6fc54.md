Africa is thought to have a higher proportion of female entrepreneurs than any other continent, yet securing business funding remains a persistent challenge for women. According to World Bank figures, women own roughly 40 percent of small businesses in Nigeria, but 56 percent of female entrepreneurs were denied their last loan applications, compared to only 17 percent for men. Despite these financial hurdles, women across the region are building successful enterprises in capital-intensive sectors.

In Nigeria, Ifedolapo Runsewe established Freee Recycle in 2020 to address environmental and health hazards caused by burning discarded tyres. Based on the outskirts of Ibadan, her company collects tyres from Oyo, Lagos, Osun, and Kwara states, turning the waste into rubber granules, playground surfaces, gym flooring, paving blocks, and tiles. After spending more than 12 years in banking, Runsewe entered the industrial recycling market despite facing gender bias from lenders who routinely assumed male colleagues were the business owners. Freee Recycle now employs around 62 staff members and has formed partnerships with entities including Michelin, Save the Children, and the International Committee of the Red Cross. The firm plans to build Nigeria's first dedicated aerosol recycling plant and operates an art residency program established in 2025.

In Uganda, 31-year-old water engineer Kyokushaba Shine is addressing agricultural challenges facing local farmers. Inspired by her mother's struggles with unpredictable onion harvests in southern Uganda, Shine launched Winta Engineering Solutions in February 2025 without initial staff or funding. Her enterprise designs, installs, and maintains affordable irrigation equipment—primarily utilizing solar-powered pumps and drip irrigation—while digging boreholes and providing technical training to clients.

Winta Engineering Solutions has installed 30 water systems across Uganda, with more than 10 dedicated to female farmers. The company currently employs a team that is 80 percent women. To make the technology accessible, Shine works with microfinance providers to help farmers access loans of approximately 5 million Ugandan shillings, or about £1,000, which are typically paid back over two years. Additionally, the company conducts educational programs focusing on soil health and the challenges posed by the climate crisis.

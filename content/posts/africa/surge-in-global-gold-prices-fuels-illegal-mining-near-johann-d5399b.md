A surge in global gold prices has driven an increase in informal gold mining across abandoned subterranean shafts around Johannesburg. These unregistered workers are known as zama zamas, a Zulu phrase translating roughly to "to take a chance." Researchers estimate that tens of thousands of these miners are active in South Africa, creating growing friction with local communities. Near Roodepoort, Cora Bailey of Community Led Animal Welfare reported that miners began taking water from supply tanks dedicated to rescued animals, underscoring the intense strain on local resources.

South Africa's modern development is deeply tied to gold, having served as the world's leading producer for over a century until 2007. Although municipal officials believe roughly 40,000 tonnes of gold remain beneath the surface, the high costs of deep extraction have led commercial operators to abandon many shafts. In 2025, South African Mining Minister Gwede Mantashe disclosed that there were 6,100 derelict mines and 1,170 unsafe shaft openings nationwide. Government attempts to suppress illegal mining have occasionally turned disastrous; in 2025, authorities recovered 93 bodies from a Stilfontein shaft after police blocked food and water shipments underground.

The persistent rise in informal mining is fueled by widespread poverty, high unemployment, political corruption, and the failure of commercial firms to seal disused sites. Most zama zamas are foreign nationals from neighboring countries, primarily Lesotho, Mozambique, and Zimbabwe. Local politicians, such as Johannesburg council member Kenny Kunene, have publicly attributed severe infrastructure damage and sinkholes to illegal digging. However, experts dispute this narrative. Rosalind Morris, an anthropology professor at Columbia University, noted that large-scale industrial dewatering by corporate mining operations is the primary cause of sinkholes, though illegal digging can trigger localized collapses.

In addition to structural concerns, informal mining is frequently associated with regional violent crime. David van Wyk, a researcher at the Bench Marks Foundation, clarified that the laborers underground are heavily exploited, while armed, feuding gangs control access and take the profits. Despite these hazards, some local communities rely heavily on the activity. Vusi Cele, a representative for Mining Affected Communities United in Action, observed that payments and spending by informal miners keep towns like Khutsong economically viable.

Fatima Zahra El Mansouri has become Morocco's first female prime minister after being appointed by King Mohammed VI, the 63-year-old monarch who retains executive power in the constitutional monarchy. El Mansouri's political party, the Authenticity and Modernity Party, won the highest number of seats in September's election before forming a coalition that now holds 207 of parliament's 395 seats. While El Mansouri publicly called her appointment a "source of pride" for women throughout the country, the decision has drawn varied reactions across Moroccan society.

The move has reawakened conservative and religious resistance to female political leadership. Mustafa Ramid, a former justice minister from an Islamist-led administration, publicly questioned whether a woman should hold the top political role, invoking a traditional Islamic saying. Local broadcaster Chouf TV captured hesitancy among several men on the street, with some explaining they only accepted the move because it was the monarch's decision—a relevant distinction given that criticizing the monarchy carries legal penalties under Moroccan law. Online platforms also saw political satire questioning women's ability to govern effectively.

The appointment also comes amid low civic participation. Dr. Irene Fernandez-Molina, an expert at the University of Exeter, told the BBC that the monarch's decision must be viewed alongside September's election turnout, which dropped to a two-decade low of 38% among registered voters. She described the move as a top-down use of state feminism by the monarchy.

El Mansouri brings over two decades of political experience to the post, having served as mayor of Marrakech and, most recently, as a minister under former Prime Minister Aziz Akhannouch. However, she has not historically taken prominent public stances on gender equality and has not yet indicated whether she will advance long-awaited changes to the Moudawana, Morocco's family code. Proposed reforms regarding polygamy and inheritance remain heavily disputed by religious scholars, including the Higher Council of Ulemas. At the same time, young advocates are pressing for social progress, pointing to official 2025 data showing that only 55% of rural girls aged 15 to 17 attend school compared to 100% in urban areas, driven by factors such as poverty, unsafe transport, and a lack of free menstrual products.

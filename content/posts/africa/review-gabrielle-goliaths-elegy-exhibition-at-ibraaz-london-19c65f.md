A soundscape of sustained grief is currently filling the basement of Ibraaz, a recently established cultural centre located in the Fitzrovia district of London. The exhibition features *Elegy*, an artwork created by South African artist Gabrielle Goliath that serves as a vocal lament for victims of genocide, murder, and systemic injustice.

The piece consists of three distinct films in which women sing a single, continuous note. As each singer exhausts her breath, another vocalist immediately takes over, maintaining an uninterrupted sound. Goliath spent ten years developing the project, which initially focused on femicide and the killings of LGBTQ+ individuals in South Africa before expanding to address broader historical and contemporary atrocities.

The exhibition's current iteration commemorates specific victims of violence. One film honours Ipeleng Christine Moholane, a woman strangled and killed near Johannesburg in 2015. Another remembers two women killed during the genocide committed by German colonial forces in Namibia between 1904 and 1908. The third film commemorates Palestinian poet Hiba Abu Nada alongside thousands of Palestinians who have died in Gaza since 2023.

The work previously triggered a major political and legal dispute in South Africa. A five-member panel of South African cultural figures had chosen Goliath's project to represent the nation at the Venice Biennale. However, South Africa’s minister of sport, arts and culture, Gayton McKenzie—who leads the far-right Patriotic Alliance party and supports Israel—demanded that the segment referencing the Palestinian poet be removed, labeling the artwork "highly divisive".

When Goliath refused to alter her creation, McKenzie canceled South Africa’s pavilion at the biennale. Goliath challenged the minister's decision in South Africa's high court, but her case was dismissed, leaving the official national pavilion empty during the event.

Following the cancellation, Goliath initially presented the installation inside a nearby church in Venice before bringing it to London. Rather than relying on graphic visual imagery of modern conflict, the installation relies entirely on an unsettling, discordant soundscape designed to convey pure emotion, anger, and a demand for justice. The result is a feminist critique of violence and censorship that leaves a lasting auditory impression on visitors.

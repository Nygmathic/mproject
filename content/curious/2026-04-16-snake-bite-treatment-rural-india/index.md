## Drowning in Faith

In the scorching heat of a rural Indian village, a 13-year-old boy lay motionless, his life slipping away as his family clung to a desperate, ancient remedy for snake bites – submerging him in the sacred waters of the Ganges River. The decision, made in the fervor of faith, would prove fatal, leaving behind a community grappling with the consequences of their deep-seated convictions.

The decision to submerge the boy in the river was not taken lightly. The family, deeply entrenched in their cultural and spiritual traditions, had been swayed by whispers of the Ganges' healing properties, which have been touted for centuries as a panacea for various ailments. The Ganges, India's most revered river, has long been considered a sacred entity, a symbol of spiritual cleansing and rebirth. For many Hindus, its waters are imbued with divine power, capable of washing away sin and disease. However, in the case of the snake-bitten boy, the family's faith proved to be a lethal combination of superstition and inaction.

The practice of using holy water to treat snake bites is not unique to this family or even this region. Across India, there are numerous instances of people relying on traditional remedies, often passed down through generations, to combat the effects of venomous bites. This phenomenon is not limited to snake bites; in many parts of the world, people continue to seek treatment from spiritual or traditional healers, often with devastating consequences. The allure of faith-based remedies lies in their perceived ability to tap into deeper, mystical forces, which are believed to supersede the limitations of modern medicine.

The Ganges, with its rich spiritual heritage, has long been a focal point for these faith-based practices. In the case of the snake-bitten boy, the family's reliance on the Ganges' healing properties proved catastrophic, highlighting the dangers of mixing faith with medical reality.

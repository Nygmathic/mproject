## The Forgotten Span of the American West

Deep in the Arizona desert, a century-old bridge stands as a testament to the ingenuity of early highway engineers. The Chevelon Creek Bridge, a 100-foot Warren pony-truss steel span, has weathered the test of time, silently witnessing the transformation of the American West from a rugged frontier to a hub of modern transportation. Its story is a fascinating one, a tale of engineering prowess, geographical challenges, and the enduring legacy of the first generation of infrastructure.

Built in 1912-1913 by the Missouri Valley Bridge & Iron Company, the Chevelon Creek Bridge was designed to bridge the nearly 90-foot deep canyon carved by Chevelon Creek on the Colorado Plateau. At the time, the bridge was part of the Santa Fe Highway, a critical transcontinental auto route connecting New Mexico to California. The engineering challenge was not the length of the bridge, but rather the terrain. Constructing a large masonry or concrete structure in this remote area would have been prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. A prefabricated steel truss, on the other hand, allowed engineers to ship components by rail and assemble them relatively quickly on site.

This innovative approach not only saved time and resources but also ensured the bridge's durability. The steel truss design, with its exposed members and narrow deck, was perfect for the rugged terrain of the American Southwest. The bridge's design also reflected the scale of the early automobile era, when roads carried a fraction of today's traffic. As a result, the bridge has survived mostly intact, a rare example of early highway infrastructure that has withstood the test of time.

The Chevelon Creek Bridge's story is also a reminder of the dynamic nature of transportation infrastructure. Within a decade of its construction, the main route shifted north toward what eventually became the U.S. 66 / Interstate 40 corridor, leaving the bridge on a quiet county road. Ironically, this bypass likely saved the bridge from the fate of many other early structures, which were either torn down or bypassed as the transportation landscape evolved.

Today, the Chevelon Creek Bridge stands as a testament to the engineering ingenuity of the early 20th century. For engineering tourists, it's a reminder that sometimes the most interesting structures aren't the biggest ones – they're the survivors from the first generation of infrastructure that opened the West to automobiles. The bridge's listing on the National Register of Historic Places is a testament to its significance, and its continued use as a county road crossing allows visitors to experience the narrow deck, exposed steel truss members, and canyon drop exactly as early motorists did more than a century ago.

## A Legacy of Innovation

The Chevelon Creek Bridge is not an isolated example of early highway engineering in the American Southwest. The region's rugged terrain and harsh climate presented a unique set of challenges that required innovative solutions. Engineers of the time had to balance the need for durable structures with the constraints of remote locations and limited resources. The use of prefabricated steel trusses, like the one on the Chevelon Creek Bridge, was a common solution to this problem.

However, the Chevelon Creek Bridge stands out for its exceptional condition and continued use. Many other early structures in the region have been lost to the passage of time, either due to neglect or intentional demolition. The bridge's survival is a testament to the foresight of local authorities, who recognized its historical significance and took steps to preserve it for future generations.

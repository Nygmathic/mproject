## Enshrined in Marble and Memory

Amidst the tranquil surroundings of Franklin College in Franklin, Indiana, a peculiar paradox exists – a testament to the enduring legacy of one of America's most influential figures, Benjamin Franklin. The campus, founded in 1834, boasts not one, but two tributes to the Founding Father, each offering a unique glimpse into his multifaceted persona. The juxtaposition of a grand, marble statue and an unassuming bench serves as a poignant reminder of the complexities of historical commemoration.

The statue, sculpted by James Mahoney in 1874, stands 12 feet tall at the northwest entrance to the campus, where it has become an iconic landmark. Originally situated on the Franklin Insurance Company building in downtown Indianapolis, the monument was relocated to the campus in 1963, courtesy of the International Typographical Union. For many years, students were encouraged to paint and decorate the statue, imbuing it with a sense of communal ownership and vitality. However, this tradition ceased in 2014, leaving the statue to its present state – a relic of a bygone era, frozen in time.

The bench, meanwhile, offers a more intimate and contemplative tribute to Franklin's legacy. Located outside the Napolitan Student Center and the B.F. Hamilton Library in Dame Mall, this unassuming bench is a poignant reminder of the importance of reflection and contemplation in the pursuit of knowledge. The mall itself was named in honor of John Dame, the first graduate of Franklin College, underscoring the institution's commitment to academic excellence and community engagement.

## The Evolution of Commemoration

The coexistence of these two tributes raises questions about the nature of historical commemoration and the ways in which we choose to remember and honor our ancestors. In an era where public art and monuments are increasingly subject to scrutiny and debate, the Franklin College tributes offer a fascinating case study in the evolution of commemoration. By juxtaposing a grand, monumental statue with a more humble bench, the campus subtly subverts traditional notions of hero worship, inviting viewers to engage with Franklin's legacy in a more nuanced and multifaceted way.

Historical parallels can be drawn to the ways in which other cultures have chosen to commemorate their ancestors. In Japan, for example, the concept of "yūgen" – a profound and mysterious sense of the beauty of the world – has long been used to describe the aesthetics of subtle, understated commemoration. Similarly, in many indigenous cultures, commemoration is often tied to the land itself, with monuments and sacred sites serving as tangible connections to the past. In these contexts, commemoration is not solely a matter of grand gestures and monumental architecture, but rather a deeply personal and introspective practice.

## A Complex Legacy

Benjamin Franklin's own legacy is, of course, a complex and multifaceted one, encompassing his roles as statesman, scientist, writer, and diplomat. His influence on American history is incalculable, from his role in drafting the Declaration of Independence to his tireless advocacy for education and innovation. Yet, Franklin's legacy is also marked by controversy and contradiction, particularly in regards to his views on slavery and his involvement in the Salem witch trials.

In recent years, there has been a growing trend towards reevaluating Franklin's legacy, with some historians and scholars questioning the extent to which he lived up to the ideals of the Enlightenment. This reevaluation has been mirrored in the ways in which his legacy is commemorated, with some institutions opting to highlight his more problematic aspects alongside his more celebrated achievements. The Franklin College tributes, in this sense, offer a thought-provoking example of how commemoration can be used to prompt critical reflection and nuanced understanding.

## **A New Frontier in Heart Failure Research: The Soft Robotic Heart**

A pioneering breakthrough in medical research has given scientists a unique tool in their quest to understand and combat a mysterious condition that accounts for roughly half of all heart failure cases. The invention of a soft robotic heart, described in the journal Nature Communications, has revolutionized the study of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a condition that has long been shrouded in mystery.

HFpEF, also known as stiff heart syndrome, is a condition where the heart pumps out a normal amount of blood but becomes too stiff to relax and refill properly between beats. This stiffening of the heart muscle is a hallmark of the disease, yet researchers still do not fully understand why it develops, making it difficult to design treatments that directly target the disease. In the United States alone, over 3 million Americans have HFpEF, highlighting the urgent need for new research and treatment options.

The soft robotic heart, developed by researchers at the University of New South Wales in Australia, is the first of its kind to actively adjust how it responds to changes in pressure. By mimicking the behavior of a diseased human heart, this innovative device has the potential to reveal new insights into the mechanisms of HFpEF and pave the way for more effective treatments.

The new model, which is made from a silicone replica of the left side of the human heart wrapped in artificial muscle fibers, can dynamically contract or relax against the pressure generated by the fluid flowing through it. This feedback mechanism is critical for reproducing the abnormal stiffness that lies at the core of HFpEF. By changing how much the artificial muscles resist being stretched as the heart fills, researchers can dial in different levels of stiffness, allowing them to model several stages of the disease's progression.

## **Breaking Down the Barriers to Understanding HFpEF**

Current laboratory models of HFpEF have significant drawbacks. Traditional "mock circulation loops" use rigid pumps and tubing to recreate blood flow but do not physically resemble a beating heart. Lab animals capture more realistic biology but are expensive and do not perfectly mirror human disease. The soft robotic heart is a major breakthrough in this field, offering a more realistic and controlled environment for researchers to study HFpEF.

Thanh Nho Do, a biomedical engineer and associate professor at the University of New South Wales, is one of the study co-authors. "HFpEF has been notoriously difficult to study and treat," he said in an email to Live Science. "Our new model attempts to solve that limitation by allowing the artificial muscle fibers to 'feel' the pressure created by the fluid they are pumping and dynamically contract or relax against it."

The researchers' hope is that their new model will pave the way for the development of medical devices that can interrupt the progression of HFpEF, rather than just treating end-stage disease. By understanding the mechanisms of the disease, researchers may be able to create targeted therapies that can directly address the stiffening of the heart muscle.

## **A New Era in Heart Failure Research**

The soft robotic heart is an early proof of concept, but its potential is vast. The researchers hope to develop increasingly sophisticated robot heart models that can complement other data on HFpEF, including that from computer simulations, animal studies, and clinical testing. This could lead to a new era in heart failure research, where scientists can better understand and combat this complex and mysterious condition.

As researchers continue to study and refine the soft robotic heart, there is a growing sense of optimism that this breakthrough could lead to significant advances in the treatment of HFpEF. With its unique ability to mimic the behavior of a diseased human heart, this device has the potential to revolutionize our understanding of this devastating condition and open up new avenues for research and treatment.

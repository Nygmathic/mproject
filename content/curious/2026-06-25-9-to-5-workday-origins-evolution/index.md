## The Evolution of a Norm: Unpacking the Origins of the 9-to-5 Workday

It's a phrase that has become synonymous with the drudgery of modern life: "Working 9 to 5, what a way to make a living." But where did this seemingly ordinary construct come from? For over 150 years, workers toiled under grueling conditions, and it took the collective efforts of politicians, businessmen, and ordinary Americans to shape the modern 9-to-5 workday. As we delve into the fascinating history of this phenomenon, we uncover a complex dance of progress, resistance, and innovation.

In the early days of American factories, workers faced 12-hour days, with some employees logging an astonishing 74 hours or six days a week. The physical toll on workers was immense, leading to state-level laws aimed at reducing hours. In 1840, President Martin Van Buren took a crucial step by issuing an executive order mandating a limited 10-hour workday for federal employees in manual jobs. This early intervention marked a turning point in the struggle for better working conditions.

As the 19th century drew to a close, labor unions began to organize, demanding more reasonable working hours and pushing for legislation to restrict them. The American Federation of Labor championed the cause, with its rallying cry, "Eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will." Some states responded by passing laws to limit work hours, but these often only protected women and children. By 1900, a staggering 16% of working men were covered under these state laws, while 49% of women and children were safeguarded.

The tide began to shift in the early 20th century, with a gradual decline in work hours and the devastating impact of the Great Depression. It wasn't until 1938 that Franklin Roosevelt's Fair Labor Standards Act set the workweek at 44 hours, introducing specific rules for overtime pay above those hours. Two years later, the act was amended to reduce the workweek to 40 hours, the standard we know today.

## Industry Leaders and the Shaping of the Workday

While politicians played a crucial role in shaping the 9-to-5 workday, industry leaders like Henry Ford also left an indelible mark. Ford, the founder of the eponymous car company, is renowned for his innovative approach to work conditions. In 1914, he announced the establishment of an eight-hour workday for $5 a day, more than doubling the salaries of his workers from $2.30 a day. Ford believed that workers who didn't have to worry about financial problems at home would be more focused at work. However, this boost in pay came with certain expectations: workers were expected to maintain high moral standards, keep their homes clean, abstain from alcohol, and contribute to savings accounts.

Ford's pioneering move caught on with other manufacturers, who adopted the plan after witnessing the success of his company. This, in turn, led to a typical eight-hour day in the private sector, with daytime workers manufacturing cars from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., even before the federal Fair Labor Standards Act was enacted. In 1926, Ford took another groundbreaking step by adopting the five-day workweek, giving his workers a routine that many Americans take for granted today.

"It is high time to rid ourselves of the notion that leisure for workmen is either 'lost time' or a class privilege," Ford said, encapsulating the sentiment of a generation. His vision for a better worklife resonated with workers and employers alike, paving the way for the modern-day 9-to-5 workday.

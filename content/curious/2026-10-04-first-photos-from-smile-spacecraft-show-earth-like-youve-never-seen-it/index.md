# First photos from Smile spacecraft show Earth like you've never seen it before

From our terrestrial vantage point, the northern lights are understood as nocturnal phantoms, ribbons of green and violet that wait for deep darkness before descending over the polar sky. Yet a newly released set of debut observations from an orbital observatory has upended that familiar picture, exposing vast electrical storms sweeping across our planet in the full glare of daytime. Captured from tens of thousands of miles away, the unprecedented imagery from the joint Smile spacecraft demonstrates that auroras do not belong solely to the night. Instead, they coil endlessly around our northern latitudes, rippling across sunlit expanses of the globe where human eyes on the ground could never hope to discern them.

Earth's auroral displays represent one of the solar system’s most captivating planetary spectacles, standing alongside total solar eclipses, bright shooting stars, and strange evolutionary marvels like the majestic blobfish as defining wonders of our world. These glowing curtains are the visual manifestation of immense electrical storms occurring within the upper atmosphere. They are ignited whenever streams of charged solar particles travel along the planet’s magnetic-field lines, eventually smashing into light-emitting molecules gathered near the polar caps. While other planets across the cosmos harbor auroral phenomena of their own, they typically fail to match the bright visible colors native to our skies. Down on the surface, human observers are inherently limited by daylight, catching glimpses of these polar dances only after sunset and under the darkest conditions.

## A Ghostly Orb in Ultraviolet Light

The newly unveiled data from the Smile mission bypasses that optical barrier entirely by examining the planet through the prism of ultraviolet light. Captured in July 2026, just two weeks after flight controllers first switched on the spacecraft’s dedicated Ultraviolet Aurora Imager, the premiere imagery strips away the familiar blues and greens of the globe to reveal a spectral, alien world. Earth appears in the foreground as a ghostly and featureless sphere, luminous like a glowing half moon suspended in the void.

Across this stark half-lit sphere writhes a colossal, serpent-like ring composed of charged particles wrapping around the North Pole. Rather than remaining sequestered in darkness, the aurora sprawls brazenly over the sunlit portion of the hemisphere, blanketing roughly a third of that illuminated face. Beside it, the dark nightside of Earth quietly dissolves into the blackness of the space environment. Behind this planetary portrait, distant pinpricks of bright starlight appear to streak downward like falling raindrops—an artifact produced as the Smile spacecraft carried out subtle shifts along its orbital trajectory during exposure.

## The Architecture of an Unprecedented Orbit

This eerie portrait marks the formal operational debut for Smile, an international partnership jointly developed and managed by the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The mission, which officially launched on May 19, has now commenced its primary scientific phase after proving that its complex detection hardware survived the voyage into deep space. The satellite is slated to spend the next three years analyzing the invisible boundaries between Earth and the sun from a highly specialized, highly elliptical orbit.

To capture these expansive vistas, Smile travels far beyond low-Earth orbits, journeying out to an apogee of approximately 75,000 miles (121,000 kilometers) directly over the North Pole. This vast distance places the spacecraft nearly a third of the way to the moon, affording its instruments a wide-angle perspective that encompasses the entirety of the northern polar zone in a single frame. From this elevated platform, the spacecraft is capable of holding the North Pole in its unblinking gaze for a record-breaking 45 hours at a time, providing an unbroken, unprecedented vantage point for monitoring solar activity as it buffets our planet's magnetosphere.

## Calibration and the Instruments Beyond the Glow

While the Ultraviolet Aurora Imager was responsible for snapping the dramatic daytime portraits, it is only one component of a broader four-part scientific payload designed to map atmospheric disruptions. In addition to ultraviolet tracking, the Smile spacecraft is equipped with instruments to measure light ions, sample ambient magnetic fields, and register X-rays generated high above the terrestrial cloud tops. Together, these tools are built to trace the full lifecycle of solar storms as they slam into Earth's protective envelope.

Even as scientific operations officially get underway, engineers on the ground are continuing to fine-tune the spacecraft's remaining observational sensors. Alongside the auroral tests, mission scientists recently put Smile's soft X-ray imager through its paces by swiveling the platform toward deep space to focus on Cassiopeia A, a celebrated supernova remnant resting some 11,000 light-years from Earth. While that celestial test confirmed the camera is fundamentally operating as planned, subsequent trials pointing back toward our home planet revealed that the sensitive detector is currently gathering too much stray light. Ground teams are presently conducting calibration routines to dampen this glare, preparing the X-ray suite to join the ultraviolet imager in unveiling the hidden electrical forces wrapped around our daytime sky.

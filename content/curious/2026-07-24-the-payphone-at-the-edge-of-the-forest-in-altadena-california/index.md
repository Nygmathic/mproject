## A Portal to the Unspoken

At the edge of the Angeles National Forest in Altadena, California, a peculiar landmark stands, a testament to the indomitable human spirit. It is a working payphone, a relic of a bygone era, yet one that has been transformed into a site of profound significance through the art project, The Goodbye Line. This unassuming metal box has become a beloved fixture in the local landscape, a place where strangers can leave the goodbyes they never got to say.

The Goodbye Line, conceived by Adam Trunell and Alexis Wood, uses payphones as physical portals to capture the deepest humanity. The project began with a single payphone in Altadena and has since expanded worldwide, with people leaving messages for loved ones, former selves, pets, dreams, and even strangers who vanished before anything could be said. The payphone at the edge of the forest is the first and most iconic of these portals, a place where the ephemeral nature of human connection is palpable.

The payphone has weathered the challenges of its surroundings, including the devastating Altadena wildfires, which damaged the device. However, thanks to a community-driven GoFundMe campaign organized by The Goodbye Line, the phone was repaired and restored to its former functionality. This act of collective preservation is a testament to the project's power to transcend physical boundaries and capture the imagination of people worldwide.

The Goodbye Line is not a crisis line or counseling service, but rather an art project and archive that invites visitors to engage with the human experience in a profound and intimate way. As visitors approach the payphone, they are met with a sticker that invites them to leave a goodbye message. The payphone, once a utilitarian device, has become a vessel for the unspoken, a space where people can express emotions, thoughts, and feelings that would otherwise remain unexpressed.

The Altadena payphone has become a site of pilgrimage for those seeking to connect with the essence of humanity.

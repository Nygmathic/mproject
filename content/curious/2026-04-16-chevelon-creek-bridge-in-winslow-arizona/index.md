## Bending Steel and Time: The Chevelon Creek Bridge

Nestled in the heart of the Colorado Plateau, a century-old steel bridge stands as a testament to the ingenuity of early highway engineers. The Chevelon Creek Bridge, a compact Warren pony-truss span, has defied the test of time, its elegant lines and exposed steel truss members a poignant reminder of the era when the American Southwest was first opened to the automobile. Built in 1912-1913, this diminutive marvel has survived the passage of decades, bypasses, and the relentless march of progress, its very existence a tribute to the resourcefulness of engineers who sought to bridge the vast expanse of the Arizona desert.

The stakes of the Chevelon Creek Bridge's story are far more significant than its modest size would suggest. As a precursor to the modern highway system, this bridge represents a pivotal moment in the development of the American West, when roads and infrastructure began to shape the very fabric of the region. The bridge's construction, facilitated by the prefabricated steel truss technology, allowed engineers to overcome the daunting engineering challenge posed by Chevelon Creek's steep canyon. By shipping components by rail and assembling them on site, the Missouri Valley Bridge & Iron Company successfully bridged the gap between the roadway and the creek, creating a vital artery for early automobile traffic.

As the story of the Chevelon Creek Bridge unfolds, it becomes clear that its construction was part of a larger narrative of American expansionism. In the early 20th century, the automobile was transforming the country, opening up new territories, and connecting distant cities. The Santa Fe Highway, which the bridge once carried, was one of the early transcontinental auto routes, linking New Mexico to California and facilitating the growth of commerce and industry. However, as the modern highway system began to take shape, the main route shifted north, leaving the Chevelon Creek Bridge on a quiet county road. Ironically, this bypass would prove to be the bridge's salvation, sparing it from the ravages of heavy traffic and ensuring its survival to this day.

The choice of materials and construction techniques employed by the Missouri Valley Bridge & Iron Company was no coincidence. In the early 1900s, steel was still a relatively new and innovative material, offering a range of advantages over traditional masonry and concrete structures. Its high strength-to-weight ratio, combined with its ease of transportation and assembly, made it an attractive option for engineers working in remote areas like the Colorado Plateau. The prefabricated steel truss technology, in particular, allowed for rapid construction, reducing the time and resources required to build a bridge in a challenging terrain.

The Chevelon Creek Bridge's listing on the National Register of Historic Places is a testament to its significance as a cultural and engineering artifact. Today, visitors can still drive across the bridge, experiencing the narrow deck, exposed steel truss members, and breathtaking canyon views that early motorists once enjoyed. For engineering tourists, the bridge serves as a reminder that sometimes the most interesting structures are not the biggest or most elaborate, but rather the survivors from the first generation of infrastructure that opened the West to automobiles.

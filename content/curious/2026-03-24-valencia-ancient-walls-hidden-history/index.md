## A City's Forgotten Fortifications: Unraveling the Enigmatic Walls of Valencia

In the heart of the Spanish city, a secret lies hidden beneath the cobblestone streets and the bustling markets. Valencia, renowned for its majestic architecture, its beautiful beaches, and its vibrant cultural scene, has a lesser-known chapter in its history. For centuries, the city was encircled by walls, each built in a different era, designed to protect its inhabitants from the ravages of time, war, and conquest. Yet, these fortifications are rarely acknowledged, often overlooked, and frequently forgotten. As we delve into the fascinating story of Valencia's walls, we begin to understand the complex tapestry of a city that has been shaped by the forces of history.

The stakes are high when exploring the remnants of a bygone era. The walls of Valencia, built over three distinct periods, serve as a testament to the city's resilience and adaptability. The Roman Valentia, founded in 138 BCE by consul Brutus, was the first to bear the city's defensive structures. This original enclosure was destroyed by the troops of Pompey the Great in 75 BCE, leaving behind a cryptic legacy that would resurface in the Almoina Archaeological Museum, where sections of Roman streets, baths, and fortifications remain beneath glass walkways. The Roman era's fate may be shrouded in mystery, but its impact on the city's development is undeniable.

As we transition to the Islamic period, Valencia's transformation is palpable. The city expanded beyond its earlier limits, surrounding itself with a new defensive wall and seven gates in the 11th century. This second iteration of the city's fortifications was a response to the turbulent times, as the Visigothic period gave way to the Islamic expansion. The conquest of Valencia by James I of Aragon in 1238 marked the end of the Islamic period, earning the city its bat emblem according to legend. The aftermath of this pivotal event would leave an indelible mark on the city's architecture and its people.

The third and final iteration of the walls, built in the 14th century under the Crown of Aragon, was a grandiose structure that would eventually meet its demise in the 19th century. The Medieval Wall, also known as the Christian Wall, was three times larger than its predecessor and incorporated elements of the earlier fortifications. Its gates, 12 in total, were shut at ten each night, leaving latecomers to sleep beneath the stars, thus giving rise to the curious expression "estar a la luna de Valencia" – to be under Valencia's moon. As the city continued to grow, the walls became an anachronism, a relic of a bygone era that no longer served its purpose.

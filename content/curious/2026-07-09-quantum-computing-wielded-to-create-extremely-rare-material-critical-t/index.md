## Quantum Leap in Nuclear Fusion: Scientists Harness Power of Quantum Computing

In a groundbreaking experiment, researchers from IBM and Oak Ridge National Laboratory have successfully employed quantum computing to blueprint the creation of tritium, a rare isotope of hydrogen critical to the nuclear fusion process. This world-first breakthrough has the potential to revolutionize the development of clean, abundant nuclear power and provide a solution to the global energy crisis.

Using a hybrid quantum computer and supercomputer, the scientists have modeled the physics inside a fusion reactor, a feat previously thought to be beyond the capabilities of classical computing. The research, uploaded to the preprint server arXiv on June 29, demonstrates the first time different kinds of computing elements have come together to propose the most effective way to create tritium.

Fusion reactors have long been touted as a clean and efficient source of energy, producing no carbon byproducts or long-lived radioactive waste. At scale, a single fusion reactor could produce approximately 4 million times as much energy as a coal-burning facility and around four times the amount of energy as a modern nuclear fission reactor. This is a staggering figure, and one that highlights the immense potential of fusion power.

However, the development of a viable fusion reactor has been hindered by numerous engineering challenges. One of the major bottlenecks lies in creating enough tritium to sustain the fusion reaction long enough to produce energy. Tritium, a radioactive isotope, is extremely rare, with only 44 pounds produced on Earth each year. Its 12-year half-life makes it difficult to use in nuclear power plants, and scientists have had to rely on painstakingly producing tritium in nuclear reactors by bombarding lithium atoms with neutrons.

The current method of creating tritium involves a complex process of superheating and binding it with powerful magnets into a whirling ring of plasma within a tokamak, a special fusion chamber designed to shape and heat plasma using magnetic fields. However, this process is limited by the availability of tritium, and scientists have been struggling to model the particle physics and chemical reactions involved in the tritium-creation process.

That is where the power of quantum computing comes in. By using a quantum computer alongside a supercomputer, the researchers have been able to simulate the complex chemistry involved in creating tritium. They have focused on modeling nine molecular configurations of a liquid salt that contains fluorine, lithium, and beryllium (FLiBe), a leading candidate material for extracting tritium.

The results of the study are promising, and if perfected, FLiBe could provide a near-limitless source of fuel for nuclear fusion reactors. The "blanket of molten salt" made of FLiBe provides both a fuel source and a thermal shield for the device, making it an attractive option for fusion reactor design.

While the research has not been peer-reviewed, the scientists are optimistic about the potential of their findings. "This is a major breakthrough in the development of nuclear fusion," said one of the researchers. "We believe that our work could help clear a path to developing clean, abundant nuclear power and solving the global energy crisis."

As the world continues to grapple with the challenges of climate change and energy production, the potential of quantum computing to revolutionize the development of nuclear fusion is an exciting one. With this breakthrough, scientists may finally have the tools they need to overcome the engineering challenges that have hindered the development of fusion reactors for so long.

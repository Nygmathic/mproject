## The Muddled Origins of a Timeless Adage

In a world where creative freedom is often touted as a license to indulge, a phrase has persisted in popular culture, fueling the notion that getting drunk can be a catalyst for artistic expression. The oft-repeated maxim, "write drunk, edit sober," has become synonymous with the rebellious spirit of writers who refuse to be bound by convention. Yet, a closer examination of this phrase reveals a tangled web of misconceptions and misattributions.

The idea that Ernest Hemingway, one of the most celebrated writers of the 20th century, advocated for writing under the influence is a notion that has been perpetuated for decades. His reputation as a heavy drinker, coupled with his disciplined approach to writing, has led many to believe that he was a proponent of this unorthodox approach. However, a thorough investigation of Hemingway's writing habits and statements reveals a stark contrast to this popular narrative.

According to his granddaughter, Mariel Hemingway, the Nobel Prize-winning novelist was a stickler for discipline and productivity. "He never wrote drunk, he never wrote beyond early, early morning," she said in an interview. This assertion is corroborated by Hemingway's own words, as expressed in a Writer's Digest interview published shortly after his death. In response to a question about his writing process, Hemingway deftly deflected any suggestion that he wrote while intoxicated, instead pointing the finger at his rival, William Faulkner.

The notion that Hemingway was a proponent of writing under the influence was likely fabricated by his contemporaries, who sought to create a more sensationalized image of the writer. Faulkner, in particular, was a rival of Hemingway's, and their respective writing styles and personal habits often served as fodder for criticism and comparison. By attributing the phrase "write drunk, edit sober" to Hemingway, his detractors may have been attempting to tarnish his reputation and create a narrative that pitted him against Faulkner.

In reality, the origins of this phrase can be traced back to a 1964 novel by American author Peter De Vries, Reuben, Reuben. In the book, a character remarks, "Sometimes I write drunk and revise sober, and sometimes I write sober and revise drunk." This line of dialogue has since been stripped of its context and attributed to Hemingway, who, ironically, would have disagreed with the sentiment.

The phrase itself is not without merit, however. As De Vries' character notes, the creative process often requires a balance between spontaneity and restraint, emotion and discipline. This dichotomy is reflected in the two parts of the quote, which highlight the importance of both elements in the creative process. While Hemingway may not have advocated for writing under the influence, he would likely have agreed that both aspects of creativity are essential.

In conclusion, the notion that Hemingway wrote drunk and edited sober is a myth that has been perpetuated by a tangled web of misconceptions and misattributions. As the truth behind this phrase comes to light, it is clear that Hemingway's writing habits and philosophy were far more disciplined and nuanced than popular culture would have us believe. The enduring appeal of this phrase lies not in its accuracy, but in its ability to capture the complexity and multifaceted nature of the creative process.

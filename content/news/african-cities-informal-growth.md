The standard way of talking about African urban growth is a warning.

Cities expanding faster than roads can be built or pipes can be laid. Housing shortages that push millions into informal settlements. Traffic that turns short distances into long ordeals. Flooding in neighbourhoods that were built without drainage because nobody planned for them at all.

These are real problems. Anyone who has spent time in a rapidly growing African city knows them directly.

But there is another story running alongside that one, and it does not get nearly as much attention. Across the continent, the people living in these cities — particularly in the communities that formal planning has passed over — have been finding their own ways to make things work. And some of what they are building is more interesting than the crisis framing allows.

## The Scale of What Is Happening

Sub-Saharan Africa is urbanising faster than any other region on earth.

In 1990, roughly 28 percent of the region's population lived in cities. By 2020, that figure had risen to around 42 percent. By 2050, more than half of sub-Saharan Africans are expected to be living in urban areas — a shift involving hundreds of millions of people within a single generation.

This is one of the largest and fastest population movements in human history. It is also largely unplanned — not because African cities lack planning departments or urban development strategies, but because the pace of arrival consistently outstrips the ability of formal institutions to respond to it.

The city builds itself, one decision at a time, faster than any plan can keep up.

## What Informality Actually Means

When researchers or planners describe African cities as "informal," they are generally pointing to the fact that large portions of housing, commerce, and services operate outside official rules and frameworks.

People build homes on land they do not formally own. Businesses run without licences. Transport networks grow up around minibuses and motorbike taxis that are not officially recognised. Water and electricity are often shared and managed through community arrangements rather than connected to the municipal grid.

From a planning perspective, this looks like disorder.

From a different angle, it looks like **communities solving problems that institutions have not got around to solving for them.** The informal settlement that a visitor might read as a sign of failure often functions, from the inside, as a dense and highly organised community with its own economy, its own governance arrangements, and its own ways of looking after its members.

As the saying goes, *where there is no path, people make one.* African urban communities have been making paths for decades.

## Where Innovation Is Appearing

Some of the most practical urban innovations emerging from the continent are not coming from city governments. They are coming from within these communities and from entrepreneurs who know them well.

Decentralised solar systems are allowing households and small businesses in areas without grid electricity to power their homes and keep their phones charged — not as a temporary fix but as a working solution that in some cases outperforms the grid in reliability.

Community health structures, often run by trained local residents, are providing basic healthcare in neighbourhoods that formal health services have never reached. Digital mapping projects have documented informal settlements in more detail than any official cartography achieved — and in some cases, those maps have been used to establish land rights and connect residents to city services for the first time.

Even transport — the most visible source of urban congestion — has been improved in some cities through digital platforms that make informal minibus networks easier to use and predict, reducing the chaos that comes from a system with no timetable.

## The Planning That Is Actually Needed

None of this is a case against planning or investment. African cities need both, urgently — in water, sanitation, roads, public transit, and affordable housing.

But the track record of top-down urban redevelopment projects in African cities is not encouraging. Large-scale programmes that clear informal settlements and relocate residents to formal housing on the outskirts of cities have repeatedly broken up the social and economic networks that those communities depended on. People often drift back, because what was moved was the housing, not the reason they were there.

What tends to work better is upgrading in place — improving conditions in existing settlements without displacing the people who live in them. Formalising land tenure for current residents rather than removing them. Investing in the infrastructure that communities have asked for. Listening to what people actually need rather than deciding from a planning office what they should want.

## Cities That Cannot Be Fully Planned

There may be something uncomfortable in recognising that African cities will not end up looking the way urban planning textbooks expect.

They will be denser, more layered, more improvised, and more informal than the models that most planners were trained on. That is not a failure of ambition. It may be a working adaptation to the conditions — of climate, of rapid growth, of economic reality — that the rest of the world is only beginning to face.

The question is not whether African cities will keep growing. They will, dramatically and without pause.

The question is whether the people who are building them — with and without permission, with and without plans — will be given the resources, the rights, and the genuine say in decisions that they need to build something worth living in.

The city is already being made. The only real choice is whether to work with the people making it.

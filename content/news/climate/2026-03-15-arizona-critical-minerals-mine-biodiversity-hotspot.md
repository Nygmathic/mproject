Deep within the rugged landscape of southeastern Arizona, a region renowned for its breathtaking natural beauty and rich biodiversity, a contentious proposal to establish the nation's first critical minerals mine is nearing approval. The Hermosa project, spearheaded by the Australian mining giant South32, has been the subject of intense scrutiny and debate, pitting the interests of economic development and energy security against those of environmental conservation and community well-being. As the U.S. Forest Service prepares to greenlight the project, the stakes are high, with far-reaching implications for the region's delicate ecosystem, the local population, and the country's broader climate goals.

The Hermosa project, situated in the Patagonia Mountains, aims to extract critical minerals such as zinc, lead, and silver, which are deemed essential for the production of renewable energy technologies, including solar panels and wind turbines. The U.S. government has framed the mine as a strategic investment in the country's energy future, one that will reduce dependence on foreign suppliers and support the transition to a low-carbon economy. However, the project's location in a biodiversity hotspot, where the Sonoran Desert meets the Sky Islands, has raised significant concerns among environmentalists and local residents. The region is home to a wide array of unique and threatened species, including the jaguar, ocelot, and Gila monster, which could be disrupted or displaced by the mining activities. Moreover, the mine's proximity to the San Rafael Valley, a vital habitat for migratory birds and other wildlife, has sparked fears about the potential impact on the area's fragile ecosystem.

## The Science of Climate Change and Mineral Extraction
The extraction of critical minerals is a crucial component of the global effort to mitigate climate change, as these resources are necessary for the production of clean energy technologies. However, the mining process itself can have significant environmental consequences, including deforestation, water pollution, and habitat destruction. The science is clear: human activities, including mining, are contributing to the alarming rate of biodiversity loss, which in turn exacerbates the climate crisis. The Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) has warned that the current rate of species extinction is unprecedented in human history, with an estimated one million species facing extinction due to human activities. In this context, the approval of the Hermosa project raises important questions about the trade-offs between economic development, energy security, and environmental protection.

The history of mining in the American West is marked by a legacy of environmental degradation and community displacement. The General Mining Act of 1872, which governs the extraction of hardrock minerals on public lands, has been criticized for its lax regulations and lack of environmental safeguards. The law, which has remained largely unchanged since its inception, allows mining companies to operate with relative impunity, often at the expense of local ecosystems and communities. The Hermosa project, which has been permitted under a streamlined process, has been subject to intense scrutiny, with many arguing that the environmental impact statement (EIS) has failed to adequately assess the project's potential risks and consequences. The EIS, which was released on March 5, has been criticized for its lack of transparency and public engagement, with many stakeholders feeling that their concerns have been ignored or dismissed.

As the U.S. Forest Service prepares to approve the project, these stakeholders are urging the federal government to reconsider its decision, citing the need for a more comprehensive and transparent assessment of the mine's potential risks and consequences.

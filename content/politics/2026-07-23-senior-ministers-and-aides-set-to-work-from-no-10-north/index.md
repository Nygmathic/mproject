## Shifting the Balance of Power: The Rise of No 10 North

Andy Burnham's ambitious plan to establish a permanent presence in the North is taking shape, with senior ministers and aides set to work from No 10 North, a newly established branch of Downing Street in Manchester. At the heart of this initiative is the prime minister's vision for the North to become the "dominant driver of the UK's economy." But as the government begins to relocate staff and infrastructure, concerns are emerging about the feasibility and implications of this radical shift in governance.

Education Secretary Lucy Powell, Housing Secretary Angela Rayner, and Louise Haigh, Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster, are among those planning to work regularly from No 10 North, currently operating from Heron House in Manchester city centre. A blueprint produced by the think tank Re:State recommends a workforce of up to 300 staff within 18 months, with Burnham aiming to base himself at No 10 North at least one day a week.

Behind the scenes, a significant programme to move staff out of London has been underway for weeks, with senior officials summoned to calls informing them of the plan. The relocation of staff, including at least seven Greater Manchester Combined Authority employees, including four senior managers, has sparked debate about the future of Whitehall. While some civil servants welcome the idea of a No 10 North, others are concerned about the uncertainty surrounding the plans and the potential impact on their careers.

Dave Penman, general secretary of the FDA union, has cautioned that relocation is a big decision for staff, with many considering the added concern of returning to London after a move. "Those considering a move out of London have the added concern that a return would be difficult given the disparity in house prices," he noted, highlighting the need for a long-term commitment to the project.

Simon Kaye, author of the Re:State report, has emphasized the importance of establishing a genuine sense of shared purpose and reaching a critical mass of people at No 10 North. "That means getting to a place where No 10 North feels different enough and vital enough that it can persist, no matter who the prime minister is," he said. While many civil servants are optimistic about the potential of No 10 North, others are skeptical about its ability to make a lasting difference, particularly given the dominance of London-based institutions.

As the government continues to develop its plans, questions are being raised about the opportunities and challenges posed by this significant shift in governance. Henri Murison, chief executive of the Northern Powerhouse Partnership, has highlighted the importance of creating opportunities for people to build careers close to the communities they serve. "No 10 North needs to make the best use of all talent and create opportunities for people to build careers close to the communities they serve," he said.

The implications of this move are far-reaching, with potential implications for the balance of power between the North and the South. While Burnham has reassured critics that No 10 North is not a gimmick, the project still faces significant challenges in its implementation. As the government navigates the complexities of this radical shift, one thing is clear: the future of governance in the UK is about to undergo a profound transformation.

Quan Hongchan is not alone in facing this kind of abuse; numerous other athletes and celebrities have been targeted by online trolls, with many reporting feelings of despair, anxiety, and even suicidal thoughts.

At stake is not just the reputation of Quan Hongchan and the Chinese sports establishment but also the country's broader social fabric. China's rapid digitalisation has created a toxic online environment where hate speech and bullying have become endemic. The government's response to this crisis will be closely watched both domestically and internationally, as it seeks to balance the need for free expression with the imperative of protecting citizens from online harm.

China's cyberbullying problem has deep roots in the country's social media landscape, where anonymity has created a culture of impunity for trolls and online harassers. Platforms like Weibo and Douyin have become breeding grounds for hate speech, with many users emboldened by the knowledge that their identities will remain hidden. The situation has been exacerbated by the proliferation of "fans" who, rather than supporting their idols, have become increasingly aggressive and entitled, often using their online platforms to lash out at athletes who fail to meet their expectations.

The phenomenon of online bullying in China has historical parallels with the country's experience with social unrest during the 1980s and 1990s, when the government faced waves of protests and demonstrations sparked by economic inequality and social injustice. Just as then, the regime's response has been characterised by a mix of repression and co-optation, with officials seeking to appease public opinion while also maintaining control over the narrative.

## A New Era of Collective Defence in the Middle East

In a significant development, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, and Turkiye have signed a joint defence agreement in Mecca, which treats an attack on any one of the countries as an attack on all three. The Makkah Joint Defence Agreement, as it has come to be known, marks a major shift in the region's power dynamics and has raised questions about the implications for the US role in the Middle East.

The agreement, signed by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman of Saudi Arabia, President Recep Tayyip Erdogan of Turkiye, and Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif of Pakistan, creates a mutual defence pact that does not name a specific threat. However, analysts suggest that the agreement is principally aimed at containing Iran and its network of non-state actors, including Hezbollah, the Houthis, and Iraqi Shia militias.

"This agreement does not target any country," Erdogan said, adding that it remains "open to the participation of all brotherly countries seeking peace, prosperity, and stability." Saudi officials have also stressed that the agreement does not pose any threat to regional security and does not come at the expense of the kingdom's ties with other allies, including the US and Gulf countries.

The pact's signing has sparked debate about the region's changing power dynamics. Analysts argue that the agreement's value lies in creating uncertainty for adversaries and strengthening collective deterrence, rather than specifying fixed military obligations similar to Western alliances. "The practical shift is minimal at this stage," said Kamran Bokhari, a senior resident fellow at the Middle East Policy Council. "This is a strategic signal rather than an operational alliance, with any integrated military capacity years away."

The Makkah Joint Defence Agreement is a significant development in a region that has seen escalating tensions in recent months. The war on Iran has pulled missile and drone fire onto Gulf oil exporters, including Saudi Arabia, and shut down much of the Strait of Hormuz. The signing of the pact comes as Yemen's Houthi rebels have resumed attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure and oil tankers in the Red Sea, while also threatening a maritime blockade on the kingdom.

Analysts say each side is bringing a different strength to the alliance, with Saudi Arabia's capital, Pakistan's nuclear status and manpower, and Turkiye's fast-growing defence-export industry. "Saudi-centric deterrence" is how Aziz Alghasian, a Saudi researcher and senior non-resident fellow at the Gulf International Forum, describes the kingdom's efforts to develop its own capacity alongside building alliances to help it deter any attacks.

The agreement has also raised questions about the implications for the US role in the Middle East. "What could the Mecca defence pact mean for the US role in the Middle East?" is a question being asked by analysts and policymakers alike. The US has traditionally been the guarantor of security in the region, but the signing of the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement may signal a shift towards a more regional approach to security.

As Dania Thafer, executive director of the Gulf International Forum, noted, there are real opportunities for the pact to expand, particularly with countries such as Kuwait and Qatar. "It is a space worth watching closely," she said. With the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement in place, the region is poised to witness a new era of collective defence, one that may have significant implications for the balance of power in the Middle East.

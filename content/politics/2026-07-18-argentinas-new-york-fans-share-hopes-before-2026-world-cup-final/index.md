## A World United in Passion: Argentina's New York Fans Share Hopes Before the 2026 World Cup Final

In the heart of New York City, a vibrant tapestry of cultures has long been woven together by the threads of football. As the 2026 World Cup final approaches, the neighbourhood of Elmhurst, Queens, is swelling with excitement, its streets painted in the iconic colours of Argentina. Among the revellers are lifelong residents and descendants of the original Argentinian diaspora who settled in New York City during the tumultuous 1970s.

For Christian Gimenez, 40, a lifelong resident of Elmhurst and owner of Rio de la Plata Bakery, the World Cup is more than just a biennial spectacle. It's a testament to the enduring power of football to unite communities across borders and generations. Gimenez, who spearheaded the decorations adorning the neighbourhood, has seen the community's passion for Argentina's national team grow with each passing year.

This year, however, is different. Argentina's path to the final has been marked by controversy, with racist incidents from fans tarnishing the team's reputation. Gimenez acknowledges these incidents, but insists they do not represent the fan base. "Whatever your race or whatever your ethnic background is, we don't look into that," he says. "If you're supporting us, we love you. Straight up, just like that."

Gimenez's bakery is a shrine to Argentina's football tradition, its walls adorned with a mural of Lionel Messi and Diego Maradona. For many, this mural is a reminder of the country's generational significance, a glue that has held the community together even as the diaspora has dispersed. Seventy-four-year-old Beatriz Jaime, who grew up in Queens but now lives on Long Island, has fond memories of watching Argentina's 1978 routing of the Netherlands at Madison Square Garden. She recalls returning to the neighbourhood soon after, only to find it "loaded with people" celebrating into the night.

As the World Cup final approaches, the community is abuzz with excitement. Henry Pachaco, 45, a resident of Queens, sees the block as "the centre" for Argentinian fans in the city. On game day, the street is transformed into a stadium atmosphere, complete with a closed street, music, an outdoor television, and street food. Pachaco calls it a "convergence of worlds," a coming together of Argentinians from all corners of the globe.

The passion for Argentina's national team extends beyond the Argentinian community, however. In the Kensington neighbourhood of Brooklyn, a rapidly growing Bangladeshi community has earned the area the title "Little Bangladesh." Shafiqul Alam, 66, who has lived in the neighbourhood for 36 years, says the area has transformed, bringing with it a passionate new base of support for Argentina's national team.

On match days, large-screen televisions have been set up in pedestrian squares in the neighbourhood. If the weather turns, it's not uncommon for people to crowd inside Alam's shop to watch on his own television. "Bangladeshi people love Argentina," he says, "and Argentina loves Bangladesh." Many factors have coalesced to fuel support for Argentina within South Asia, particularly Bangladesh, including a newfound access to televisions in the 1980s, which aligned with the Maradona-led national team's 1986 defeat of England.

For younger fans, support for Argentina comes down to one man: Lionel Messi. "Knowing this could be Messi's last match, everyone will want to watch," says Sajid Bhuyan, 31, a resident of the neighbourhood. He estimates that at least 90 percent of the local Bangladeshi community in Kensington backs Argentina. While the origin stories of their fandom may be different, Bhuyan feels the passion from Little Bangladesh to Little Argentina is the same.

As the World Cup final approaches, the city is bracing itself for the possibility of a repeat of Argentina's stunning turnaround in the semifinal against England. For fans like Bhuyan, the excitement is palpable. "I almost died when Argentina scored the two goals in just minutes," he recalls. "I couldn't breathe, I couldn't talk, I had to take five or six minutes to calm down. So if it happens again," he says with a smile, "we will enjoy!"

The stakes are high, but one thing is certain: for fans in New York City, the World Cup is more than just a tournament – it's a celebration of community, culture, and the enduring power of football to unite us all.

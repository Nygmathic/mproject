## Unseen Threats in the Shadows

The Democratic Republic of Congo's (DRC) struggle against the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record has been hampered by a series of unseen threats, including months of unreported cases before the official declaration in mid-May. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the outbreak began in February, with some early cases misattributed to malaria and typhoid. This delay has exacerbated the crisis, as responders scramble to contain the surge in cases.

Confirmed cases of Ebola in the DRC have reached 4,200, including at least 1,900 deaths, according to the latest government figures. The outbreak is unfolding in challenging conditions, with strikes by unpaid health workers, threats from rebel groups, anger among long-traumatized communities, and widespread misinformation about the existence of the disease. Health teams face significant obstacles, including remote and unpaved roads, shortages of protective gear, and a vast population of displaced people struggling to access reliable water sources for hand hygiene.

The current outbreak is unlike most previous ones, as it is caused by the rare Bundibugyo virus, which has no approved vaccines or treatments. Early testing was conducted for the more common type of Ebola, contributing to delays. This has allowed the virus to spread further, with new cases doubling in some hotspots. According to WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, Ebola is spreading faster than efforts to fight it. The Director-General's comments were made after meeting with officials in Kinshasa, the capital, where he emphasized the need for urgent action to combat the outbreak.

The DRC's healthcare system has been challenged for years due to rebel conflict in one of the country's most remote and vulnerable areas. Access to healthcare has been severely disrupted, making it difficult for health teams to reach affected communities. The situation has been further complicated by the fact that several previous Ebola outbreaks have been declared weeks or months late, including the 2014-2016 West Africa outbreak, which was declared in March 2014, although the first human case was later found to have occurred in December 2013.

In response to the current crisis, the WHO has recommended the start of full-scale human trials for the Ervebo vaccine, the only licensed Ebola vaccine. While Ervebo has proven to be safe and effective against the more common Zaire strain of the Ebola virus, WHO experts believe it may also give some protection against the rare Bundibugyo strain. A specific Bundibugyo vaccine is the preferred option for fighting the current outbreak, and several potential vaccines are currently in the early stages of clinical trials.

The WHO's regional director for Africa, Mohamed Janabi, has emphasized the need for a concerted effort to combat the outbreak. "So we are chasing the virus, the virus is ahead of us," he said in a news conference. The WHO's efforts are focused on supporting health teams, improving access to healthcare, and increasing awareness about the risks of Ebola. However, the organization faces significant challenges, including the need for more funding, personnel, and equipment to combat the outbreak.

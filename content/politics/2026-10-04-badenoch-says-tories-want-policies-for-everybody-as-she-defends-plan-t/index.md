Kemi Badenoch has mounted a staunch defense of Conservative plans to extend state-funded childcare to high-income families, insisting that the party must champion policies designed for every tier of society. Speaking in a broadcast interview with Laura Kuenssberg as the Conservative Party conference formally commenced in Birmingham, the Tory leader argued that the current system penalizes hard work through punishing fiscal thresholds. Badenoch firmly maintained that the party's ambition is to remove barriers that deter parents from advancing their careers, dismissing criticisms regarding whether multi-millionaires should qualify for state-funded support.

Under the proposals unveiled by the party, the existing £100,000 earnings cap that restricts access to free childcare would be eliminated, an intervention the Conservatives calculate will carry an annual cost of £700 million. The party intends to fund the measure through sweeping reductions in jobs across non-departmental public bodies, commonly known as quangos. Badenoch explained that the existing mechanism creates an acute cliff edge, pointing out that an individual earning just one hundred pounds over the £100,000 mark could forfeit between £10,000 and £20,000 worth of childcare assistance. To illustrate her point, she highlighted the dilemma of a deputy headteacher earning £85,000 who might hesitate to accept a promotion to headteacher at £110,000 because having three children would result in the sudden loss of tens of thousands of pounds in support. When challenged on whether parents earning £1 million ought to receive taxpayer-funded subsidies, Badenoch countered that the number of people earning tens of millions while raising very young children is negligible, reiterating her desire to demonstrate that the Conservatives offer a coherent plan for every worker, from those with no earnings to top-tier earners.

## Ideological Foundations and the Triple Lock Debate

The defense of the childcare policy coincides with an effort by Badenoch to set out her overarching political philosophy, detailed in a newly published 100-page manifesto entitled *The Right Way*. The launch of the text has seen supporters draw direct comparisons between Badenoch and Margaret Thatcher as she outlines her foundational principles. Facing accusations that the Conservatives are slipping back into a past reputation as the "nasty party," Badenoch vigorously pushed back, describing her movement as the "common sense party." As part of her core argument, she has sustained an emphasis on fairness and integrity, openly arguing that cheating represents a significant issue within the British benefits system that demands robust scrutiny.

This ideological positioning has also shaped Badenoch's complex stance on the state pension triple lock, an area that has sparked friction within center-right economic circles. While confirming that the triple lock remains the right policy for the immediate term, Badenoch declined to offer an unconditional pledge that the Conservatives will preserve it indefinitely. She defended the mechanism on the basis of a contributory principle, asserting that current pensioners deserve the lock because they have spent a lifetime contributing to society, while suggesting that the expenditure simply amounts to a ledger on a budget line that would cease to be problematic if the wider national economy were growing at a faster rate. However, this rationale has drawn criticism from former Conservative cabinet minister David Gauke, who publicly argued that Badenoch is wrong to assert that the contributory principle makes reconsidering or ditching the triple lock unreasonable.

## Westminster Battles and Party Pressures

The opening of the conference in Birmingham has unfolded alongside a series of bitter exchanges across the parliamentary divide and within the wider conservative ecosystem. The party sought to claim an early major victory over the Labour administration after ministers scrapped proposals aimed at curbing access to jury trials, though Green politicians simultaneously voiced concern that some criminal suspects would still be stripped of their right to a jury trial despite the government's retreat. Labour also faced intense scrutiny over its criminal justice handling, with Justice Secretary Alex Norris proving unable to specify how many prisoners released early under emergency capacity schemes had actually been fitted with monitoring tags.

At the same time, the Conservatives have faced attacks from figures on their own political flank. Former Home Secretary Suella Braverman accused the party of producing a tacky imitation of her own ideas after the leadership backed calls to bar foreign nationals from accessing the UK student loan framework. Despite the external attacks, the mood among the party faithful gathered in Birmingham remained celebratory, opening with the national anthem and centered around a prominent spoof exhibition titled the Birmingham Tapestry. Modeled after the historic Bayeux Tapestry, the display chronicles party events and portrays Badenoch as a triumphant victor alongside satirical depictions of her political rivals, rapidly establishing itself as the most frequented attraction in the exhibition hall.

## Splits Deepen Within the Green Party

While the Conservatives gathered to debate economic policy in the Midlands, deep ideological fractures surfaced within the Green Party over an intensely contested internal resolution. The party was thrown into public division by a motion that would formally declare the Greens an anti-Zionist party and classify Zionism as a form of racism. Green Party co-deputy leader Mothin Ali declared his absolute backing for the motion, defending his position by arguing that the core text directly addresses genocide, ethnic cleansing, and ethno-nationalism, which he described as components of a racist ideology.

The motion has nevertheless triggered immense resistance from other senior Green figures who fear severe electoral and community repercussions. Fellow deputy leader Rachel Millward openly distanced herself from the measure, describing the resolution in its unamended format as problematic and confirming she would withhold her support unless explicit amendments were added to protect Jewish members. Millward emphasized that the resolution was driven from the grassroots membership rather than forming part of the leadership's strategic direction. The former Green co-leader Carla Denyer similarly intervened to urge activists to reject the motion, warning that its passage would serve to alienate many Jewish people. Meanwhile, Zack Polanski, who is Jewish and describes himself as anti-Zionist, sought to navigate a middle path by proposing protective amendments so members would not be penalized for belonging to Zionist synagogues or institutions, before leaving the conference to campaign in the Holborn and St Pancras by-election while arguing that ideological debates over terminology distracted from concrete action on Israel and Palestine.

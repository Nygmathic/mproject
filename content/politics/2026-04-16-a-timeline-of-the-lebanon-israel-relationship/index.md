## A Legacy of Conflict

As the unlikely meeting between Israeli and Lebanese officials took place in Washington this week, the world was reminded of the enduring complexities of a relationship that has been technically at war since 1948. The latest round of direct talks between the two nations, facilitated by United States mediators, marks a significant turn in the convoluted history of Israeli-Lebanese relations. Yet, the path towards durable peace remains fraught with obstacles, drawing on decades of conflict, mistrust, and competing narratives.

The stakes of this latest attempt at reconciliation are high. Lebanon and Israel share a 79-mile border, and the fragile peace that has held since 2006 has been tested numerous times. The 2006 Lebanon War, triggered by a Hezbollah cross-border raid, resulted in the deaths of over 1,000 Lebanese civilians and significant Israeli military losses. Since then, small-scale skirmishes and periodic tensions have persisted, underscoring the ever-present threat of all-out war. Against this backdrop, the direct talks in Washington offer a glimmer of hope, but also raise questions about the depth of commitment from both sides.

To understand the intricacies of the Israeli-Lebanese relationship, it is essential to delve into the historical context. The conflict's roots date back to 1948, when Israel declared independence, leading to the displacement of hundreds of thousands of Palestinians, including those from Lebanon, who fled their ancestral lands or were forcibly expelled. This displacement created a deep-seated grievance among Lebanese Christians and Muslims alike. Since then, Lebanon has hosted a significant Palestinian refugee population, which has contributed to the country's internal dynamics and fueled tensions with Israel.

The Lebanese Civil War, which ravaged the country from 1975 to 1990, saw Israel intervene on multiple occasions, often in support of Christian militias. This involvement has been viewed by many as a form of Israeli aggression, further solidifying Lebanon's perception of Israel as a threat. The 2000 withdrawal of Israeli forces from southern Lebanon, facilitated by the United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL), marked a temporary lull in tensions. However, the 2006 war demonstrated that the underlying issues remained unresolved, with each side accusing the other of provocation and aggression.

In recent years, Lebanon has been grappling with its own internal challenges, including a crippling economic crisis, massive protests, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Against this backdrop, the direct talks with Israel have sparked debate and skepticism among Lebanese politicians and civil society groups. Some have questioned the value of engaging with a country that has shown little willingness to recognize Lebanon's sovereignty or address the refugee crisis. Others have expressed concerns about the potential consequences of normalizing relations with Israel, which could further strain ties with regional powers like Iran and Syria.

## Shifting Regional Dynamics

The Israeli-Lebanese relationship is inextricably linked to broader regional dynamics. The 2017 withdrawal of US forces from Syria and the subsequent Turkish intervention in northern Syria have created a power vacuum that has emboldened Iran-backed militias in the region. Lebanon, with its strategic location and complex sectarian landscape, has become a critical battleground in this proxy war. Israel, meanwhile, views Iran's growing influence in the region as a significant threat, and has taken steps to counter this perceived menace.

The Saudi-Iran rivalry has also had a profound impact on the Israeli-Lebanese relationship. Saudi Arabia, a longtime rival of Iran, has sought to strengthen ties with Israel in an effort to counter Iranian influence in the region. This rapprochement has led to increased cooperation between the two nations, including joint military exercises and intelligence sharing. Lebanon, meanwhile, has historically maintained close ties with Iran, which has provided significant economic and military support to the country.

## A Fragile Peace

As the direct talks in Washington continue, both sides face significant challenges in building on the fragile peace that has held since 2006. The Lebanese government, still reeling from the economic crisis and internal power struggles, must navigate the complexities of engaging with a country that has historically been seen as an enemy. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, meanwhile, faces opposition from within his own government, as well as from right-wing groups that view a peace deal with Lebanon as a betrayal.

The path forward will require significant concessions from both sides. Lebanon must be willing to address Israeli concerns about Hezbollah's military activities and its perceived ties to Iran. Israel, in turn, must be prepared to recognize Lebanon's sovereignty and address the refugee crisis. The United States, which has facilitated the direct talks, will also need to demonstrate a sustained commitment to the peace process, providing economic and diplomatic support to both sides.

## A Glimmer of Hope

Despite the challenges ahead, the direct talks in Washington offer a glimmer of hope for a durable peace between Lebanon and Israel. The Lebanese government, led by Prime Minister Najib Mikati, has demonstrated a willingness to engage with Israel, despite domestic opposition. Israeli officials, meanwhile, have shown a willingness to compromise, recognizing that a lasting peace with Lebanon is essential for regional stability.

As the talks continue, international stakeholders will be watching closely. The United States, the European Union, and other regional powers will need to provide sustained support to the peace process, addressing the complex issues that have bedeviled Israeli-Lebanese relations for decades. The path forward will be fraught with obstacles, but a durable peace between Lebanon and Israel offers a tantalizing prospect for a more stable and secure Middle East.

## What's Next

As the direct talks between Lebanon and Israel enter a critical phase, observers will be watching for signs of progress. Will the two sides be able to overcome their deep-seated differences and agree on a framework for peace? Or will the talks stall, as they have so many times before? The world will be holding its breath as the two nations navigate the complex web of regional rivalries and competing interests. One thing is certain, however: a durable peace between Lebanon and Israel offers a beacon of hope for a more stable and secure region, and it is a prospect that is worth fighting for.

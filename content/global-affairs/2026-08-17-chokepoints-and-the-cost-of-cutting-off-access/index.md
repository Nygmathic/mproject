## The Delicate Dance of Access and Control

The unassuming decision by Elon Musk, CEO of SpaceX, to refuse Ukraine's request to activate Starlink coverage around Sevastopol in 2022 has shed light on a critical aspect of modern geopolitics: chokepoints. These strategic bottlenecks, where dependence is concentrated, have long been a point of concern for nations seeking to mitigate their vulnerabilities. However, it is not the narrowest strait or rarest technology that poses the greatest threat, but rather the final decision resting with the fewest people.

The notion of chokepoints is often reduced to a simplistic question of dependence – where does a nation rely heavily on a particular resource, route, or technology? But the true significance of these chokepoints lies elsewhere. The threshold of a chokepoint is defined by the risk that someone must take – in blood, money, law, or reputation – before leverage becomes action. This is a critical distinction, one that has far-reaching implications for nations navigating the complex web of global dependencies.

In the case of China, the fear of being beholden to external chokepoints has driven a significant naval build-up over the past two decades. The "Malacca dilemma," a term coined by then-president Hu Jintao in 2003, referred to the vulnerability of Chinese energy imports through the Strait of Malacca. This anxiety has not been unfounded, as the Strait has indeed emerged as a critical chokepoint, with approximately 15% of global oil and 20% of global gas passing through it. China's response has been to build a capable navy that can deter or, at the very least, persuade others to think twice before attempting to restrict access.

The Strait of Hormuz, another critical chokepoint, has demonstrated the far-reaching consequences of restricting access to global trade. In 2022, Iran's decision to block the strait, following US and Israeli attacks, disrupted a waterway that normally handles around 20% of global oil and gas shipments. The aftermath has seen heavily constrained traffic, with Iran and Oman engaged in negotiations over new shipping lanes, and Tehran attaching broader political conditions to reopening the strait.

The situation highlights the delicate dance of access and control that nations engage in when dealing with chokepoints. While China's naval build-up has certainly increased its capacity to influence access to the Strait of Malacca, it remains a critical chokepoint where external actors can exert significant leverage. The threshold of a chokepoint, therefore, lies not in the physical characteristics of the chokepoint itself, but in the risks that nations are willing to take to access it.

As the world becomes increasingly interconnected, the ability to navigate the complex web of global dependencies will become a critical component of national power. Nations will need to carefully balance their own vulnerabilities with the risks associated with attempting to restrict access to critical chokepoints. The decisions made by Elon Musk in 2022, and the subsequent consequences of those decisions, serve as a stark reminder of the importance of understanding and navigating these delicate dynamics.

## The Anatomy of a Chokepoint

The concept of a chokepoint is often shrouded in uncertainty, with nations struggling to define the threshold beyond which leverage becomes action. However, the recent events surrounding the Strait of Malacca and the Strait of Hormuz have shed light on the critical factors that contribute to a chokepoint's significance.

The most critical of these factors is the concentration of dependence. Where a nation relies heavily on a particular resource, route, or technology, the risks associated with restricting access become exponentially greater. This is precisely what has driven China's naval build-up over the past two decades, as the nation seeks to mitigate its vulnerability to external actors.

Another critical factor is the cost of cutting off access. The Strait of Hormuz, for example, has seen significant disruptions to global trade, with Iran's decision to block the strait resulting in heavily constrained traffic. The economic and political costs of restricting access to such a critical chokepoint are substantial, and nations will need to carefully weigh these risks against the potential benefits of exerting influence over the chokepoint.

Finally, the decision-making process at play also plays a critical role in determining the significance of a chokepoint. Where the final decision rests with the fewest people – as in the case of Elon Musk's decision to refuse Ukraine's request – the threshold of a chokepoint is significantly lowered. This is precisely why nations are investing in building capable navies and other forms of national power, in order to increase their influence over critical chokepoints and mitigate their vulnerabilities.

## The Future of Chokepoints

As the world continues to evolve, the dynamics surrounding chokepoints will only become more complex. Nations will need to navigate the delicate balance of access and control, carefully weighing the risks associated with restricting access to critical chokepoints against the potential benefits of exerting influence over these key bottlenecks.

The decisions made by nations in this regard will have far-reaching consequences, shaping the global landscape of trade, politics, and security. As the world becomes increasingly interconnected, the ability to navigate these complex dynamics will become a critical component of national power.

In the end, the concept of chokepoints serves as a stark reminder of the interconnected nature of global dependencies. Nations will need to carefully balance their own vulnerabilities with the risks associated with attempting to restrict access to critical chokepoints, in order to navigate the complex web of global dependencies that underpins modern geopolitics.

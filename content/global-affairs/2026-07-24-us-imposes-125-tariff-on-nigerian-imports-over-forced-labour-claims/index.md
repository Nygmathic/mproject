## Tariffs Imposed to Combat Forced Labour: Unpacking the US Trade Measure

The United States has imposed a 12.5 per cent tariff on imports from Nigeria, one of 60 economies deemed to have failed to prohibit the importation of goods produced with forced labour. The measure is the latest development in a long-standing effort to eradicate forced labour from global supply chains. The implications of this trade restriction are far-reaching, and its impact will be closely watched by policymakers, businesses, and human rights advocates alike.

The US Trade Representative (USTR) has been investigating the trade practices of its largest trading partners under Section 301 of the Trade Act, a provision that allows the administration to impose tariffs on countries deemed to be violating US trade policies. The investigation, launched in May 2026, involved a comprehensive review of written submissions, public hearings, and consultations with over 45 governments. The USTR received over 1,600 written submissions and heard from more than 100 witnesses during the public hearings.

The tariffs imposed on Nigeria and other countries are designed to encourage trading partners to strengthen their measures against forced labour. President Donald Trump has recognised that decades of moral suasion have not been sufficient to eradicate forced labour from global supply chains, and the US has had a forced labour import ban for nearly a century. The administration is now taking a more assertive approach, using trade measures to pressure countries to adopt and enforce laws prohibiting the importation of goods produced with forced labour.

The list of countries subject to the 12.5 per cent tariff is extensive, including Nigeria, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, and the United Kingdom. However, countries that have already implemented or committed to implementing forced labour import prohibitions will face a lower 10 per cent tariff. The USTR has specified that the tariffs will not apply to certain products, including raw materials that could create domestic supply shortages, goods that could cause economy-wide disruptions, products unavailable in sufficient quantities in the United States or from alternative sources, and selected goods from countries that have adopted or pledged to implement forced labour import bans.

The impact of the tariffs will be significant, particularly for Nigerian businesses that rely heavily on exports. ## The Human Cost of Forced Labour: A Global Concern

Forced labour is a pervasive human rights issue that affects millions of people worldwide. The International Labour Organization estimates that there are over 40 million victims of modern slavery, with the majority being trapped in forced labour. The issue is not limited to any one region or country, and it affects people from all walks of life.

The use of forced labour is often linked to other forms of exploitation, such as human trafficking and child labour. It is also closely tied to poverty, inequality, and economic instability. In many cases, individuals are forced to work in hazardous conditions, with little or no access to basic rights and protections.

The global community has made significant progress in addressing forced labour, but much remains to be done. The adoption of the Palermo Protocol in 2014 marked a significant milestone in the fight against human trafficking, and many countries have since strengthened their laws and enforcement mechanisms.

However, the persistence of forced labour remains a major challenge, and it requires a sustained and collective effort to address. The US trade measure is one aspect of this effort, and it aims to pressure countries to adopt and enforce stronger labour laws.

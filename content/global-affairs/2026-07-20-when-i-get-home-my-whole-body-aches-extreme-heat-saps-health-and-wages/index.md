### Extreme Heat and Humanitarian Crisis in India

Extreme heat is wreaking havoc on India's informal sector, leaving hundreds of millions of workers on the brink of poverty. A recent study by the International Institute for Environment and Development (IIED) paints a stark picture of the devastating impact of extreme heat on the country's most vulnerable workers. As temperatures soar, these workers are losing up to 9% of their income, exacerbating existing structural inequalities and deepening poverty.

Outdoor workers, particularly those in physically demanding jobs such as construction, agriculture, and transport, are suffering the most. The IIED research found that they lost, on average, 6.6% of their salaries in 2024 due to extreme heat. In the arid desert region of Rajasthan, informal workers lost equivalent to 8.3% of their income, highlighting the disproportionate impact of heatwaves in areas with prolonged heat exposure.

The economic loss is staggering, with 21.1 working days lost in India each year due to extreme heat. This is an unaffordable loss for many of the 550 million workers in the informal sector, who lack employment security and are forced to work through illness and tolerate unsafe conditions. Access to basic amenities such as drinking water, shade, and sanitation at workplaces is poor, leaving workers vulnerable to heat-related illnesses.

The human cost of extreme heat is equally alarming. Eight construction workers who spoke to researchers all reported falling ill over the past four months due to the heat, forcing them to miss work and lose income. Some had collapsed and sustained serious injuries, yet were given no compensation by their employers. Inder and Laxmi Kumar, a husband and wife toiling on a construction site in Gurugram, described their ordeal in vivid detail. "I felt like I would collapse, like my body was on fire," Laxmi said. "We kept working because if we stopped, we wouldn't be paid."

The couple's experience is not unique. Many workers are forced to continue working despite their health deteriorating, as they cannot afford to lose a day's wages. This cycle of poverty is perpetuated by the lack of social protection and inadequate access to healthcare. As Ritu Bharadwaj, climate finance director at the IIED, noted, "Heat is quietly pushing these families deeper into poverty."

The consequences of extreme heat are far-reaching, with the Indian economy expected to lose at least £58 billion – equivalent to 2% of its GDP – in a combination of wages lost due to reduced productivity, worksite closures, and long-term losses from heat-related illnesses. This is a stark reminder of the urgent need for action to mitigate the impacts of climate change.

As the world grapples with the challenges of climate breakdown, India's experience serves as a stark warning. If left unaddressed, heatwaves could become one of the first places where human survivability is broken. The World Bank has warned that India, a country where nine out of 10 work in the informal economy, is particularly vulnerable to the impacts of extreme heat.

In the face of this humanitarian crisis, governments, businesses, and civil society must come together to address the root causes of poverty and inequality. This includes investing in social protection, improving working conditions, and providing access to healthcare and social services. It also requires a fundamental shift in the way we approach development, prioritizing the needs of the most vulnerable and marginalized communities.

As Inder and Laxmi Kumar's story makes clear, the human cost of extreme heat is immeasurable. Theirs is a cry for help, a plea to policymakers and the international community to take action. When will we listen?

### Averting a Humanitarian Crisis: Next Steps

As the international community grapples with the challenges of climate change, India's experience serves as a stark reminder of the urgent need for action. The consequences of inaction will be devastating, with the potential to push millions of people further into poverty and exacerbate existing structural inequalities.

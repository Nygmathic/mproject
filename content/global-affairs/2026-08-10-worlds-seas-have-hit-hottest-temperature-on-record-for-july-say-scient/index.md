## Record-Setting Heat Waves Engulf Europe

The world's seas have reached their hottest temperature on record for July, according to scientists at the EU's Copernicus climate change service. This development comes as western Europe grapples with devastating heat waves that have ravaged the Mediterranean and pushed national temperature records to unprecedented levels.

The average sea surface temperature outside polar regions has surpassed the previous record set in 2023, with the Atlantic coast and western Mediterranean bearing the brunt of the heat. This extreme heat has resulted in severe marine heatwaves, exacerbating the already dire situation in Europe. As the region battles wildfires that have left swaths of the Mediterranean in ruins, the consequences of climate breakdown are becoming increasingly apparent.

Climate scientist Samantha Burgess from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts notes that this summer has exemplified the intensification of climate extremes, where persistent high-pressure systems have trapped heat over western Europe, amplifying drought and heatwaves in a self-reinforcing cycle. As soils dry out, they lose their capacity to provide natural cooling, allowing heat to build more readily and creating an environment conducive to wildfires.

This phenomenon has been supercharged by fossil fuel pollution, which has led to a string of heatwaves in Europe since the end of spring. The particularly brutal heat at the end of June is estimated to have resulted in more than 10,000 deaths. The widespread dryness observed in May and June has intensified last month, with exceptionally low rainfall and soil moisture in much of western Europe. This has led to low river flows, which have affected water supply, irrigation, and power plants, including in countries downstream.

The economic implications of these heatwaves are also far-reaching, with estimates suggesting that grain farmers have lost €2bn in lost crops this summer. Economists warn that the longer-term damage can eclipse short-term losses, particularly if droughts persist over many years. A study led by the CMCC European Institute on Economics found that the mega-drought of 2015-18 cost Europe €439bn, highlighting the need for more accurate assessments of drought-related economic losses.

Researchers argue that conventional methods underestimate the impact of droughts by failing to capture the years after the initial event. Marta Mastropietro, a researcher at CMCC and the Polytechnic University of Milan, notes that even a single dry year causes real economic damage, but a persistent drought can compound across sectors and keep accumulating for years afterward.

As the region struggles to cope with the aftermath of these heatwaves, experts are sounding the alarm on the need for more effective adaptation measures. Víctor Resco de Dios, a professor of forestry engineering at the University of Lleida, emphasizes the importance of vegetation management and urban planning in mitigating the problem of mega-wildfires. However, he also warns that climate change is creating increasingly favourable conditions for these devastating events, and that the window for taking necessary adaptive measures is rapidly closing.

In the face of these record-setting heat waves, Europe must come together to address the root causes of climate breakdown and work towards a more sustainable future. As the world's seas continue to absorb the brunt of human activity, it is imperative that policymakers, scientists, and the general public acknowledge the severity of the situation and work towards a concerted effort to mitigate the effects of climate change.

## Economic Costs of Climate Breakdown

The economic costs of climate breakdown are becoming increasingly apparent, with the European Forest Fire Information System reporting that about half a million hectares have burned across the EU since the start of the year. This has been accompanied by above-average pollution as burning vegetation spews thick smoke, exacerbating the already dire situation in Europe.

The hot, dry, and windy conditions that make it harder to battle blazes have set new records for severity for this time of year. Climate change is creating increasingly favourable conditions for the development of mega-wildfires, and experts are warning that what seems extreme today will be remarkably benign in a few decades if we continue to fail to take the necessary adaptive measures.

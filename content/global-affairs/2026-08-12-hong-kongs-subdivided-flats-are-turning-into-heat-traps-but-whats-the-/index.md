## Trapped in the Heat

Hong Kong's subdivided flats have become notorious for their cramped conditions, but a growing concern is beginning to emerge as these tiny homes turn into heat traps during the sweltering summer months. For tenants like retiree Yang Fangxin, who resides in a 72 sq ft rooftop home on the ninth floor of a 62-year-old building in Sham Shui Po, the situation is increasingly untenable.

Yang's home, which she shares with her daughter and a grandchild, has seen temperatures soar above 40 degrees Celsius (104 Fahrenheit) on several occasions, with the highest recorded reading exceeding that of the Hong Kong Observatory's monitoring station in the same district. The elderly woman's only respite comes from switching on two fans and an air conditioner, but this comes at a steep cost – her electricity bill has already risen to around HK$650 (US$83) a month, a sum that is becoming increasingly unsustainable.

The situation is not unique to Yang's home, however. Temperatures throughout the city have been consistently hovering around 33 degrees Celsius, prompting the Hong Kong government to issue a very hot weather warning that has been in place since last Wednesday morning. While officials have taken steps to mitigate the impact of the heatwave, welfare advocates are urging the authorities to take a more comprehensive approach to addressing the issue of heat traps in subdivided flats.

## A Growing Concern

The issue of heat traps in subdivided flats is not a new one, but it has taken on a new level of urgency in recent months as the summer heat has intensified. These tiny homes, often found in older buildings, are frequently plagued by inadequate ventilation, poor insulation, and a lack of natural light. As a result, temperatures can quickly become unbearable, leading to a range of health problems, including heat exhaustion, dehydration, and even heatstroke.

The consequences of inaction are stark. As Yang's story illustrates, the financial burden of keeping cool in these heat-trapped homes can be prohibitively expensive, forcing tenants to make difficult choices between their health and their wallet. In some cases, this has led to a phenomenon known as "heat-induced migration," where residents are forced to flee their homes in search of cooler and more affordable accommodations.

## A Call to Action

Welfare advocates are now calling on the Hong Kong government to take a more proactive approach to addressing the issue of heat traps in subdivided flats. They argue that the current measures, which focus primarily on providing financial assistance to affected households, are insufficient and that a more comprehensive solution is needed.

One possible solution being floated is the provision of energy-efficient cooling systems, such as evaporative coolers or solar-powered fans, which could help to reduce the financial burden on tenants while also mitigating the impact of the heatwave. Additionally, there are calls for the government to implement stricter building codes and enforcement mechanisms to ensure that new developments and renovations prioritize energy efficiency and ventilation.

## A Regional Challenge

The issue of heat traps in subdivided flats is not unique to Hong Kong. Cities across the Asia-Pacific region, including Singapore, Kuala Lumpur, and Manila, are grappling with similar challenges. As urbanization continues to accelerate, the pressure on housing stock is growing, leading to a surge in subdivided flats and other forms of informal housing.

In this context, Hong Kong's response to the heat trap issue will be closely watched by policymakers and stakeholders across the region. Can the city find a solution that balances the needs of tenants with the demands of a rapidly changing urban landscape? The answer will have far-reaching implications for the future of urban development and the well-being of residents in cities across the Asia-Pacific.

## A Way Forward

As the heatwave continues to grip Hong Kong, the issue of heat traps in subdivided flats remains at the forefront of the city's policy agenda. While there are no easy answers, one thing is clear: inaction is not an option. The Hong Kong government must take a more comprehensive approach to addressing this issue, one that prioritizes the needs of tenants and provides a sustainable solution to the problem of heat traps in subdivided flats.

As Yang Fangxin's story illustrates, the stakes are high, and the consequences of inaction could be severe. But with a concerted effort from policymakers, stakeholders, and the broader community, it is possible to find a solution that prioritizes the well-being of residents while also promoting a more sustainable and equitable urban future.

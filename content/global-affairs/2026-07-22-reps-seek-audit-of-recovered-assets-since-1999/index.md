## Assets of Uncertainty

As Nigeria continues to grapple with the scourge of corruption, a stark reality has emerged: despite billions of naira in recovered assets, the country remains shrouded in uncertainty regarding the status, management, and disposal of these funds. The House of Representatives has now taken a bold step by calling for a comprehensive audit of all assets seized, forfeited, recovered, managed, disposed of, or repatriated by government institutions since the country's return to democratic rule in 1999.

The resolution, sponsored by Ibe Osonwa, a member of the House representing Arochukwu/Ohafia Federal Constituency of Abia State, was adopted on Tuesday following a motion that highlighted the lack of a centralised asset register and weak oversight mechanisms. Osonwa's motion underscored the gravity of the situation, noting that despite the enormous value of recovered assets, Nigeria still lacks a unified national database to track their status and utilisation. The absence of comprehensive public records detailing the status, disposal, and utilisation of recovered assets has led to recurring allegations of poor record-keeping, weak asset valuation procedures, and inadequate oversight.

The lawmakers' concerns are well-founded. The Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC), the Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission (ICPC), the Nigeria Police Force, and other security institutions have recovered billions of naira and numerous high-value assets linked to corruption and financial crimes over the years. However, the lack of transparency and accountability in the management of these assets has undermined public confidence in Nigeria's asset recovery process and limited the ability of the National Assembly to exercise effective oversight.

The enactment of the Proceeds of Crime (Recovery and Management) Act, 2022 was intended to harmonise the management of recovered assets, improve transparency, and prevent abuse. However, the absence of a unified national database and comprehensive public records has rendered the legislation ineffective. Osonwa's motion argued that deploying digital technology would significantly improve transparency, accountability, and inter-agency coordination while providing the public with reliable information on recovered assets.

The adoption of the motion by the House of Representatives has set the stage for a comprehensive audit and verification of all seized, forfeited, recovered, managed, disposed of, and repatriated assets held by ministries, departments, and agencies, anti-corruption bodies, security agencies, and courts from May 29, 1999, to date. Lawmakers have also advocated for the establishment of a comprehensive digital asset registry to improve transparency, strengthen accountability, and ensure proper documentation of recovered assets across government institutions.

The implications of this development are far-reaching. If implemented effectively, the audit and verification process could provide a much-needed clarity on the status of recovered assets, restore public confidence in the asset recovery process, and enable the National Assembly to exercise effective oversight. However, the success of this initiative will depend on the willingness of government institutions to cooperate and provide accurate information on the management and disposal of recovered assets.

As Nigerians continue to demand greater transparency and accountability in the management of recovered assets, the House of Representatives' resolution has provided a glimmer of hope. The country's anti-corruption efforts will benefit significantly from a comprehensive audit and verification process, and the establishment of a unified national database will go a long way in ensuring that recovered assets are managed and disposed of in a transparent and accountable manner.
